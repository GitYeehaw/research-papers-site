Microswimmers lose by swimming selfishly

Albert Alonso

At low Reynolds number, microswimmers are coupled by the flows they create, even when they are apart. The best way for each swimmer to push itself forward then depends on its neighbors, turning swimming together into a game. To study this, we optimize the shape changes of soft swimmers, alone and in pairs, with a differentiable Stokes solver. From random initial membrane motions, a lone swimmer converges to a traveling membrane wave. In a pair, the stroke of a swimmer affects its neighbor far more than the neighbor affects its own best stroke. For swimmers in line, this influence decays as $1/\ell$ with their separation $\ell$, whereas the incentive to respond decays as $1/\ell^2$. Selfish swimmers, which maximize their own payoff, respond only to the weak incentive. The cooperative gain they miss decays only as $1/\ell^2$, and their selfish adaptations leave the pair worse off than if both had kept their solitary strokes. Related swimmers, which also value the payoff of their neighbor, recover this gain, whereas competitors pay to hinder each other, both following a law that we derive analytically. Hydrodynamics alone thus makes swimming in company a social dilemma, whose outcome depends on how much the swimmers value each other.

Nonequilibrium Transient Centers in Continuous-Time Systems

Anthony Pasion, Felicia Magpantay

Analysis of mathematical models in ecology and epidemiology often emphasizes asymptotic dynamics, such as stable equilibria, periodic orbits, and attractors. However, many biologically relevant trajectories exhibit long transient dynamics in which a relevant observable evolves slowly for an extended period before a comparatively rapid transition occurs. On finite time scales relevant to data collection, management, and intervention, such transient behavior can be more informative than the eventual asymptotic outcome. In this work, we study long-lasting and slowly varying transient dynamics in continuous-time systems through the framework of nonequilibrium transient centers. These centers are points in the state space near which the observable can remain nearly constant for a long time, even when the full state is not near an equilibrium. We develop finite-time escape criteria for identifying such centers and their reachable counterparts. These criteria provide practical conditions under which nearby trajectories can remain in a slow regime for a long period of time before eventually leaving it. We apply the theory to an epidemic model with delay of stability loss. In this model, trajectories may remain close to the disease-free manifold for a long time before the infectious population resurges. The framework developed here describes this behavior as slow evolution near a nonequilibrium transient center followed by finite-time escape from the slow regime.

Locality-Controlled Out-of-Distribution Guidance for Robust Regulatory DNA Sequence Design

Dabin Jeong, Marwane Bourdim, Mo Lotfollahi

Designing regulatory DNA sequences for targeted gene-expression control is an important challenge. Recent approaches frame this task as property-conditioned generation, where diffusion models trained on natural sequences are guided toward high predicted activity through reward-based fine-tuning or sampling-time guidance. We show that single-reward evaluation can obscure reward-hacking behavior. By decomposing evaluation into property, novelty, and plausibility, we identify an empirically underexplored region of the evaluation space in which all three objectives are satisfied, but no existing baseline succeeds. We postulate that this gap arises because prior methods treat guidance primarily as a matter of magnitude. In regulatory DNA, however, sequence grammar is spatially nonuniform: motif positions are sensitive to perturbation, while background regions allow more exploration. We therefore propose LOCOGEN by reformulating guidance as a problem of locality. LOCOGEN learns an invariant motif mask through sequence-level invariance learning to protect motif positions, while applying OOD damping only to background positions. It operates at sampling time on a frozen diffusion backbone, without retraining the backbone. Across HepG2, K562, and SK-N-SH, LOCOGEN reaches this previously underexplored region of the property-novelty-plausibility space. Code is available at https://github.com/Lotfollahi-lab/LocoGen.

An extension of the classic Buneman theorem from phylogenetic trees to networks

Mirko Wilde, Mareike Fischer

Phylogenetic networks provide a natural framework for representing reticulate evolutionary events such as horizontal gene transfer and hybridization. In the present manuscript, we investigate the identifiability of phylogenetic networks under (hardwired) parsimony, a natural extension of Maximum Parsimony from trees to networks. More precisely, we ask which structural features of a network can be uniquely inferred from so-called $A_k$-alignments under this criterion. While identifiability has been studied extensively for phylogenetic trees under parsimony, little is known about the identifiability of networks under hardwired parsimony. We take a first step towards addressing this gap by developing a general framework for studying identifiability under hardwired parsimony. We then use this framework to show that the tree of blobs of a phylogenetic network can be reconstructed from its $A_1$-alignment. This result can be viewed as a natural extension of Buneman's well-known splits equivalence theorem. Finally, we show that, under hardwired parsimony, cycles of length at least $5$ cannot in general be uniquely identified from character data, establishing a fundamental limitation of hardwired parsimony.

Hyperbolic embedding of higher-order simplicial networks

Martin Guillemaud, Vito Latora, Mario Chavez

Many biological interactions involve groups rather than pairs, as when several proteins assemble into a multi-protein complex. Simplicial complexes naturally capture these collective interactions. Existing hyperbolic embeddings represent network hierarchy and similarity through a few interpretable coordinates, but rely only on pairwise links: they cannot distinguish three units engaged in a common interaction from three units connected only in pairs. We introduce here the hyperbolic higher-order (HHO) embedding, which embeds a simplicial complex into the Poincaré disk, while incorporating its group interactions. The hyperbolic area of the triangle spanned by three interacting nodes then measures how compactly their interaction is represented. Testson synthetic and real-world networks, including a co-authorship network and the protein interaction network of Escherichia coli, show that HHO reduces the hyperbolic area of triangles representing simplices relative to those obtained from pairwise network embeddings. HHO also recovers node groups more accurately than pairwise embeddings and a weighted-clique alternative, with performance competitive with Euclidean embeddings, while providing a low-dimensional representation that can be analyzed with the tools of network geometry.

Genetic weight of a mutant in a biparental Moran model with infinite selection

Camille Coron, Olivier Hénard

We study, after Coron Le Jan (2024), the genetic contribution of a single strongly advantageous mutant in a biparental Moran model under infinite selection. We prove a functional limit theorem for the entire beneficial mutation spreading period: after normalization by \(\sqrt N\), the contributions carried by the advantaged and disadvantaged subpopulations converge jointly in law to a non-degenerate random multiple of an explicit deterministic profile. Randomness is generated during the initial microscopic growth of the mutant lineage and subsequently propagated through the population along a deterministic macroscopic trajectory. At the heart of this phenomenon is an eigenmode martingale that measures the imbalance between the mean genetic weight of the founding ancestor within the two classes; its limiting value provides precisely the random amplitude of the macroscopic profile and is characterized by a smoothing equation.

Transmission and filtering of stochastic rhythms in bursty gene expression

Chen Jia

How stochastic rhythms in upstream regulatory signals are transmitted to downstream gene expression is a fundamental question in systems biology. Although experiments have shown that molecular degradation strongly influences downstream rhythmic responses, a general quantitative theory explaining how degradation, stochastic bursting, and upstream timing variability jointly govern rhythm transmission remains lacking. Here we develop a general analytical framework based on a martingale-based orthogonal decomposition, applicable to arbitrarily complex upstream dynamics, including memory effects. We derive exact autocorrelation and power-spectrum transmission laws, establish an oscillation-efficiency contraction principle, and identify a critical degradation rate that provides a necessary and sufficient condition for stochastic rhythm transmission. An explicit rhythm-transmission relation reveals that increasing burst frequency and modulation depth promotes rhythm transmission, whereas greater variability in upstream cycle duration suppresses it. In particular, increasing burst size leaves oscillation efficiency unchanged. The predicted degradation threshold correctly separates all ten pulsing and nonpulsing p53 target proteins reported in previous experiments. These results provide a theoretical foundation for understanding how cells selectively preserve or integrate rhythmic regulatory signals.

D2ML: Models for Direct-to-Biology Small Molecule Screening

AkshatKumar Nigam, Neri Amara, Hillary Dequina, Dyana Kenanova, Seth Malmersjo et al. (10 authors)

Direct-to-biology (D2B) screening enables rapid testing of newly synthesized molecules without purification. However, uncertain concentrations in crude mixtures and limited purified-compound measurements complicate the use of these data in machine learning. Here we show that models tailored to the structure of D2B data can improve prediction and guide the discovery of more potent and selective compounds. Our assay-aware models predict activity from single-point screens, relative potency across targets from crude dose-response assays, and absolute potency from purified-compound measurements. For crude assays, potency ratios between targets tested from the same well can retain selectivity information despite uncertain concentrations. A joint model that shares information across stages and balances their contributions to training improved predictions over separate neural networks. The largest gain was in crude dose-response selectivity prediction, where rank correlation increased from 0.27 to 0.40. In a separate experiment, independently trained models guided the selection of 84 new compounds to improve ROS1 potency while limiting activity against the anti-target TRKB. Synthesis and testing identified compounds that extended the measured Pareto front, achieving better combinations of potency and selectivity than those in the training data. The most selective new compound achieved a ROS1 IC50 of 14.7 nM and 233-fold selectivity over TRKB. By comparison, the most selective training compound had a ROS1 IC50 of 20.3 nM and 79-fold selectivity. ATLAS provides the experimental setting for this approach, supporting synthesis and screening of 1,000-10,000 model-selected compounds in two- to three-week cycles. Together, these results show how assay-aware learning can use the full D2B assay sequence to guide multi-objective molecular optimization.

Localizing predictive concepts encoded in neural representations via hierarchical testing

Viktoriia Huryn, Marco Simnacher, Jim Berend, Wojciech Samek, Christoph Lippert et al. (7 authors)

Understanding which regulatory sequence motifs a genomic deep learning model has learned, with statistical error guarantees, is essential for trusting its use in downstream tasks such as variant effect prediction. Existing approaches to identifying which units in a trained network encode human- understandable concepts fall into two groups. Exploratory methods rank and visualize units but offer no statistical guarantee that the selected units are associated with the prediction target across the population of inputs. Testing-based methods test only single units or one pre-defined set of them, and do not control the error across the many sets of units. We introduce CUTTIE (Cluster-tree Unit Top-down Testing of Independence with Error control), which clusters the units of each layer into a tree and tests the clusters top-down with conditional independence tests, localizing information about the target while controlling the family-wise error rate. Thereby, we identify clusters that carry information about the target beyond the rest of the layer. For the units in the identified clusters, conditional attribution maps then reveal what motifs they encode. In a controlled simulation, the procedure identifies the clusters whose concepts affect the target. We apply the procedure to a convolutional model predicting cell-type-specific chromatin accessibility from DNA sequence, where the concepts encoded in the identified clusters map onto known regulatory motifs.

Contribution of chromosome-naïve transposon-seeded network of DNA templates to intracellular pools of protein dimers of liquid-liquid phase-separated condensate-forming proteins

Gennadi Glinsky

Molecular mechanisms governing protein-protein interactions (PPI) contributing to the biogenesis of membraneless condensates remain poorly understood. Observations reported in this contribution demonstrate that liquid-liquid phase-separated (LLPS) condensate-forming proteins and their variants harboring condensate-disrupting human pathogenic mutations are significantly enriched among PPI cascade constituents of the Hybrid Assembly Pathway (HAP) of endogenous multiprotein complexes (EMC). In contrast, no structured or disordered proteins defined as condensate-negative molecules were detected among PPI cascade constituents. Cumulative evidence derived from different lines of analytical inquiries support the concept of the significant contributions of PPI cascade constituents of HAP to the biogenesis of intracellular pools of cell type-specific protein heterodimers assembled with the engagement of at least one protein monomer selected from condensate-forming protein molecules. Analytical evidence documented in this study support the concept that genome-wide chromosome-naïve networks of transposon-seeded DNA-templates nucleate the initiation of PPI cascade chain reactions with the capacity to facilitate the concurrent iterative assembly in human cells of both the membraneless biomolecular condensates and the structured multiprotein complexes. AlphaFold 3D modelling of mutation patterns and analysis of partner-bound experimental structures identified variants located at a measured protein dimerization interface. This contribution nominates numerous protein heterodimers of potential relevance to human pathophysiology for follow-up mechanistic studies of potential fundamental and translational impacts.
