cuRoboV2-Fast: Fast Torque-Limited Collision-Free Motion Planning

Balakumar Sundaralingam, Adithyavairavan Murali, Stan Birchfield

We present cuRoboV2-Fast, a GPU-accelerated planner that reduces both planning latency and execution time for collision-free, payload-aware, torque-limited motions. B-spline trajectory optimization reduces the required iterations, while accelerated robot computations and differentiable inverse dynamics reduce refinement cost and accommodate changing payloads. TimeOpt optimizes traversal speed along the fixed path under sampled velocity, acceleration, jerk, and torque constraints, exploiting local spline support to solve banded Newton systems efficiently on the GPU. With a 3,kg payload, cuRoboV2-Fast achieves 4.4 ms mean planning time on RTX 5090, 14.7x faster than cuRoboV2. Our method on Jetson AGX Orin plans faster than cuRoboV1 + TOPPRA on RTX 5090. A stereo-camera-based deployment on YAM achieves 5.93 s mean completed pick-and-drop cycles, 34.6% shorter than cuRoboV2. Perceptive Humanoid Motion from Simulation

Siyuan Hu, Abulikemu Abuduweili, Yue Meng, Shipeng Liu, Quentin Leboutet et al. (8 authors)

Humanoid robots have the potential to automate a wide range of physical tasks. Progress toward generalist humanoid autonomy, however, remains constrained by a data bottleneck: mapping pixels and free-form language to low-level joint commands requires paired supervision, and no corpus of such data exists at sufficiently large scale. Some approaches rely on human teleoperation and wearable capture devices, which require at least an hour of human time to produce an hour of data. We present HALO, a humanoid autonomy system powered by a pipeline that synthesizes vision-language-motion data across a thousand procedurally generated homes. The corpus scales with compute rather than human labor. HALO acquires a repertoire of physical skills, including sitting on chairs, navigating to named objects, stepping sideways through half-open doors, crouching and crawling beneath tables, and pushing boxes. It orchestrates these skills to solve composite, long-horizon tasks. Without ever seeing a real home during training, HALO transfers zero-shot to the physical world, achieving a 91% success rate across 360 trials in six unmapped real-world environments. See https://apple-aiml-research.github.io/ml-halo/ for code and videos.

Reinforcement Learning Enables Scaling Generalist Robot Policy Improvement

Michal Korniak, Marco Bagatella, Ralf Römer, Michal Nauman, Yarden As et al. (24 authors)

The introduction of generalist robot policies has enabled a single, large model to learn to perform a variety of tasks from a vast pre-training corpus. While these models often exhibit powerful zero-shot generalization capabilities, they typically require an adaptation phase to achieve sufficient performance in deployment. Iterated self-improvement via reinforcement learning (RL) has demonstrated promise in enabling this adaptation, yet such approaches have been largely explored in single-task settings - where the goal is to simply improve on a narrow deployment task - which lies in stark contrast to the multi-task capabilities of the base policy. In this work we ask whether generalist robot policies are capable of multi-task, generalist improvement in deployment - targeting many diverse tasks simultaneously - and what algorithmic recipe enables this. To answer this question, we first develop a set of structured evaluations to test generalist improvement capabilities, and systematically evaluate algorithmic and practical design choices regarding value estimation, policy extraction and resource allocation. We show that not only can generalist policies be improved online across diverse tasks, but that the right recipe can enable generalization and increase performance on tasks not considered during online training. We characterize when this generalization occurs and show that both the training task distribution and the use of value-based RL are critical. To the best of our knowledge, this is among the first evidence for online generalist improvement for robot foundation models.

Human Mobility Sensing Using Wall-Reflected Beamformed Millimeter-Wave Communication Signals

Tony DiMichele, Raees Matheen Mullah, Christian Lopez Garcia, Zahra Najafi, Mohammed E. Eltayeb

Existing millimeter-wave (mmWave) communication beam patterns spatially weight human-scattered signals and shape the received-power signatures induced by human motion. We exploit these beam-shaped signatures for device-free walkingpace and walk-direction classification using a wall-reflected 60.8-GHz link with closely spaced phased arrays and fixed communication beamforming vectors. Human movement alters the interference between wall-reflected and scattered signals, producing power oscillations surrounding a central blockage fade. A beam-dependent interference model provides a physical interpretation of these signatures. Using received-power statistics from a continuous-tone experiment, we extract fade duration for walking-pace classification and pre/post-fade asymmetries for walk-direction classification. Measurements comprise 144 crossings from three participants along perpendicular and diagonal trajectories. Under leave-one-participant-out validation, logistic regression achieves 86.1% binary and 79.2% three-class walking pace accuracy, and 77.1% travel-direction accuracy. The results demonstrate mobility sensing using fixed communication beams without channel estimation, Doppler processing, or dedicated radar hardware

Risk-Sensitive Stochastic Optimal Control on Positive Cones

Mohamad Al Ahdab, John-Josef Leth

We consider stochastic optimal control problems in which the state is constrained to a proper cone. Specifically, we consider risk-neutral, risk-averse, and risk-seeking formulations within a single entropic-risk framework. We investigate classes of problems whose Bellman operator preserves affine functions and characterize their optimal value functions and feedback policies for both finite-horizon and time-average infinite-horizon problems. Additionally, we establish convergence and state-bound results under finite-step cost observability. Moreover, we demonstrate an exact connection between entropic-risk optimal control on proper cones and deterministic infsup control for systems whose dynamics and stage costs are affine in the disturbances. In addition, we provide examples of classes of systems for which explicit solutions can be obtained.

VABAR: A Variational Automation Benchmark for Agentic Robotics

Shuangyu Xie, Kaiyuan Chen, Uksang Yoo, Xinyue Zhu, Meadow Shen et al. (19 authors)

For robots to operate reliably in commercial and industrial applications, they must persistently perform specific tasks with known variations in object shape and pose. We call this Variational Automation (VA) to differentiate these settings from fixed automation. Inspired by recent papers on Agentic Robotics, where pretrained LLM and VLM coding agents combine interpretable robot programming with the open-world adaptability of model free policies, we propose a general set of agentic robotics benchmark design criteria that includes accessibility, repeatability, diversity, relevance to agentic robotics and practical applications, physical realizability, resistance to benchmark saturation, and computational efficiency. We instantiate these principles in six manipulation task classes spanning industrial, medical, and infrastructure applications: tool sorting, tool hanging, gear meshing, syringe packing, cable insertion, and cable routing. We introduce the Variational Automation Benchmark for Agentic Robotics (VABAR), an open, easily replicable, simulation-based manipulation benchmark to systematically evaluating agentic robotics systems. Built fully on open-source tools, VABAR features detailed implementations using the Newton physics engine, standardized task variations, difficulty tiers, success metrics, and a library of 33 generalizable skills with human-authored programmatic reference solutions. We provide initial baseline evaluations using an open agentic manipulation harness, Graph-as-Policy (GaP), and Isaac Lab Arena.

Rethinking Self-Repair in Code-as-Policy Agents: What Drives Retry Success?

Siddharth Aphale, Ayushman Singh

Code-as-policy agents often use execution feedback to retry failed programs. Additional sampling and task guidance can also improve success, leaving the contribution of failure-specific feedback unclear. We study this question in simulated robot manipulation. A fixed lookup selects feedback templates using failure labels and the API variant. A frozen code model generates a fresh program after each scene reset. On the same 252 initial failures, Qwen2.5-Coder-7B recovers 85.3% within five retries, compared with 68.3% for resampling with the same retry cap. The advantage persists under fixed total-token budgets. Feedback from other episodes and fixed task guidance yield similar recovery point estimates, with no detected benefit from matching the coarse failure label. In a separate study, a fixed guided prompt used from the first attempt achieves an observed overall success rate similar to that of template-based feedback. Template-based feedback also lowers recovery on a nut-insertion task requiring a rim grasp, consistent with an incompatible center-grasp instruction. Evaluations of robot self-repair should include resampling, mismatched feedback, and concise, task-compatible static guidance as baselines.

Extremal-Phase-Guided Sequential Convex Optimization: Model Order Reduction

Mustafa Oğuz Yeğin, Hitay Özbay, Tomáš Vyhlídal

Fixed-order frequency-weighted H-infinity approximation is a nonconvex problem, and local optimization methods can be sensitive to initialization. We present extremal-phase-guided sequential convex optimization (EPGSCO) for stable fixed-order rational approximation. We introduce a novel phase-guidance technique motivated by the all-pass structure underlying optimal Hankel-norm approximation, with a phase template fitted to the error-magnitude derivative at dominant peaks. We integrate this guidance with dominant-peak selection, focused frequency grids, and adaptive pole-residue and polynomial-coefficient updates to address stagnation and improve approximation accuracy. CD-player examples and benchmarks with known optimal errors evaluate the algorithm against established methods. Comparisons with classical sequential convex optimization (SCO) under shared settings for the common components assess the combined contribution of phase guidance and adaptive frequency refinement. Results demonstrate improved accuracy in most of the tested cases and attainment of the known optimum from four tested initializations for a constructed order-42 benchmark, with additional computation in some cases.

Excitability of High-Frequency Positive Feedback

Gustave Bainier, Arthur Fyon, Christian Fernandez Lorden, Alessio Franci

What is the threshold of an excitable system, i.e. the criterion that distinguishes rest from excitation? Previous research has shown that this question is far from trivial to answer. This paper studies the threshold of a simple excitable primitive in which excitability arises from the positive feedback interconnection of a first-order high-pass filter and a static, strictly monotone and strictly convex activation function. This threshold is a simple inequality on the difference between the instantaneous input and the motif's internal memory. While this result may help neuro-engineers characterize spiking in biological and artificial neurons, we argue that excitability is not always a desirable property, and that our threshold may also be relevant to safety considerations in fragile systems with rate-induced tipping points. We illustrate our framework with three examples: the FitzHugh-Nagumo model of neuronal dynamics, a model of stop-loss cascades in the stock market, and a social contagion model.

CMS ECAL Phase-2 upgrade: characterisation and stability analysis of a three-stage low-voltage power-conversion system

Liv Anke Richter, Günther Dissertori, Werner Lustermann, Krzysztof Stachon

As part of the High-Luminosity upgrade of the Large Hadron Collider, the front-end electronics of the Compact Muon Solenoid electromagnetic calorimeter barrel are being replaced. A three-stage low-voltage power-conversion system supplies the upgraded electronics. As the cascade dynamics require characterisation to ensure stable operation, interactions between the three converter stages are assessed at their two interfaces using the Nyquist criterion. A validated shunt-injection setup using a Bode 100 frequency-response analyser provides in-situ impedance measurements under load to assess the accuracy of the averaged converter models. Results show that both interfaces are stable over the evaluated configurations, with modelled minimum distances to the critical point of 0.76 and 0.79, respectively. A turn-on study identifies capacitor charging at Stage 3 as the main cause of inrush currents triggering Stage 2 overcurrent protection. Two Stage 3 board variants stagger converter enables within and between boards, keeping the measured two-tower current below 5 A, within the 8.0 A protection threshold.
