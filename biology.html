FETTUCCINE: Fast and efficient brain-to-text decoding on mobile devices

Jonathan D. McCart, Pranav I. Deevi, Mehdi Azabou, Nanda H. Krishna, Caleb A. McKinney et al. (8 authors)

Recent advances in speech neuroprostheses have enabled new communication avenues for people who have lost the ability to speak due to neurological illness or injury. These systems can decode neural activity during attempted speech into text by relying on language priors to achieve high decoding accuracy. This performance currently comes at the cost of using a compute-intensive language modeling stack, presenting a major bottleneck for real-world use. Further, current language models incur highly variable and disruptive inference latencies, limiting communication throughput and preventing users from reliably participating in natural conversation. We introduce FETTUCCINE, an end-to-end brain-to-text framework that leverages the speed and portability of automatic speech recognition (ASR) models to address the aforementioned practical challenges, while achieving usable (<10%) word error rates (WER), efficient adaptation to neural non-stationarities, and reliable performance on mobile devices. When evaluated using publicly available data from an intracortical speech neuroprosthesis user, FETTUCCINE outperforms previous end-to-end methods, achieving as low as 4.39% WER. Most notably, our models successfully run on a commercially-available mobile device with throughput reaching up to 370x real-time, providing the first demonstration of brain-to-text decoding on a mobile device. We also show that our models can be successfully finetuned to extend usable performance to future days. Beyond performance, our approach enables localization of the neural features most relevant for decoding via gradient-based saliency maps, which we show align with well-established physiological priors across different ASR model families. Taken together, our results show that FETTUCCINE overcomes core infrastructural and computational barriers, yielding a new class of portable brain-to-text communication.

Alignment by Socialization: Learning Tradeoffs Under Selection

Thomas Brennan, Andrew W. Lo, Chaoyi Zhao

Artificial intelligence (AI) agents are expected to complete tasks while following rules of conduct, but these objectives can conflict. We study how evolutionary selection shapes an agent's choices between them, and whether experience can make those choices reflect human judgments and withstand subsequent pressures. In our model, copies of an agent face a common uncertain reward state, and only those taking the rewarded action reproduce. We show that long-run growth is maximized when each action is taken as often as the environment rewards it. We compare three policies. A task-only policy thrives in a sandbox rewarding completion alone but fails when conduct matters. A fixed linear policy increasingly favors task completion as its stakes rise, even where humans never reward it. A learned policy instead converges to the tradeoffs rewarded by its environment; when humans supply the rewards, we call this socialization. Accumulated experience makes socialization harder to reverse under subsequent task-only training, although violations can arise before the prevailing disposition changes, and finite memory weakens this protection. When a single violation can end a deployment, a hard prohibition can prevent it, but a mistaken prohibition can also be costly. Experiments with small neural networks illustrate how training targets and memory shape these outcomes. Our results suggest that alignment depends not only on the objectives assigned to agents, but also on the environments that shape and sustain their behavior.

CARVE: A glycan-specific framework for assessing protein-glycan binding-pose quality

Yifei Xiao, Huifeng Zhao, Silong Zhai, Zichang Jin, Tingjun Hou et al. (6 authors)

Protein-glycan interactions depend on the precise three-dimensional arrangement of carbohydrates at protein-binding interfaces, yet reliable assessment of predicted complex structures remains challenging. Existing evaluations commonly rely on fixed root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) thresholds, which do not fully account for the structural diversity and conformational flexibility of glycans. Here we developed CARVE, a glycan-specific framework for evaluating protein-glycan structure predictions. Through topology-aware glycan parsing, CARVE integrates orientation RMSD (oRMSD), interface-ring RMSD (irRMSD) and the fraction of native contacts (Fnat) to assess glycan scaffold geometry, interface-ring placement and native-contact recovery. These descriptors define four quality categories (CARVE-Level) with geometric thresholds that scale with the degree of polymerization (DP), and a continuous CARVE-Score, which reproduced those categories on 25,068 independently generated poses without recalibration (macro-F1 = 0.892). Pairing these measures with glycan-specific topological, chemical and geometric validity checks, we benchmarked nine prediction methods on CARVE-Bench, comprising 1,974 experimentally determined protein-glycan complexes. Among the top 25 predictions per target, HADDOCK3 achieved the highest Acceptable-level success rate (47.0%), whereas no method produced a high-quality, structurally valid pose for more than one in five. Collectively, CARVE provides a standardized basis for assessing protein-glycan structure prediction methods and guiding the development of more accurate docking and co-folding approaches.

Deciphering multiple binding modes in large protein complexes via Enhanced Sampling and beta-VAE: the CYFIP1-eIF4E case

Francesco Pignotti, Emanuele Telari, Edoardo Milanetti, Anna La Teana, Jesmina Rexha et al. (8 authors)

Eukaryotic initiation factor 4E (eIF4E) drives cap-dependent translation, regulated by 4E-binding proteins (4E-BPs) competing for its dorsal and lateral surfaces. CYFIP1, a scaffold protein, is both a WAVE Regulatory Complex component and a non-canonical 4E-BP repressing translation via a short helical motif, linked to autism spectrum disorders and cancer. Prior work modeled only this isolated motif as a peptide, leaving the full interface uncharacterized. Starting from this preliminary evidence, we modeled the full-length proteins as flexible partners: all-atom simulations fed a data-driven docking search (HADDOCK3), which guided funnel-restrained Temperature-Accelerated Molecular Dynamics of a coarse-grained Martini 3 representation. Ten replicas accumulated 100 $μ$s of enhanced sampling. Funnel-restraint variables efficiently sample binding and unbinding but are structurally degenerate: direct clustering of the raw trajectory collapses these poses into poorly resolved groups. We instead reweighted the biased trajectories onto a contact-based latent space built by benchmarking PCA against a beta-Variational Autoencoder, then clustered minima of the equilibrium free-energy surface: only intermediate regularization resolved distinct, homogeneous modes. Three such modes recur, engaging eIF4E's canonical dorsal site, its non-canonical lateral site, and both. We find that CYFIP1 also engages eIF4E through a second surface, the same that mediates its WAVE-complex dimerization, and a basic patch. Both plausibly explain residual binding previously reported after motif mutation. This suggests its translational and cytoskeletal roles compete for the same surface. Most contacts on eIF4E fall outside this dorsal/lateral interface. This heterogeneous, plastic ensemble offers a structural basis for mutagenesis and therapeutic peptide design.

An In-Silico Audit of Topographic Organization in a Multimodal Brain-Response Model

Jakub Strawa

TRIBE v2 (d'Ascoli et al., 2026) predicts fMRI responses across the cortical surface from video, audio and text. Its authors validated it with category-selective localizers (faces, places, bodies, words) and language contrasts, but not with the classic topographic organization of sensory cortex: retinotopy, tonotopy, and motion selectivity in area MT+. There is a clear architectural reason to expect retinotopy to fail: TRIBE's video features are averaged over space before the response head, which should discard visual-field position. I present mapping stimuli, a fitting pipeline adapted from standard fMRI mapping analyses, and a stand-in model with known ground truth that serves as a positive and negative control, and apply them to TRIBE v2's predictions, comparing the fitted maps with human atlases using spin tests. TRIBE v2 shows a sharp split. There is no contralateral hemifield response and no polar-angle map, robustly across stimulus controls. There is no tonotopic gradient in primary auditory cortex: best frequencies span an interquartile range of only 0.14 octaves across core and belt, and the null persists with loudness-equalized tones. An eccentricity-like gradient appears only when the ring geometry changes, consistent with global image statistics rather than a positional code. In contrast, motion selectivity is strong and precisely localized: 22% of the 1% most motion-selective vertices fall in MT+, which covers 0.42% of cortex. In-silico experiments that depend on feature identity appear viable with TRIBE v2, while those that depend on visual-field or frequency position are not supported by the current model.

Exact stochastic theory of post-transcriptional gene autoregulation mediated by RNA-binding proteins

Chen Jia

While analytical theories of transcriptional feedback loops have been well established, exact stochastic analyses of post-transcriptional feedback loops remain relatively scarce and are notoriously challenging. Here, we develop an exact theory of post-transcriptional gene autoregulation, in which reversible protein-mRNA binding modulates both translation and mRNA stability. We derive the state-dependent protein burst-size distribution, the steady-state protein number distribution in terms of confluent Heun functions, and an exact criterion for sub-Poissonian fluctuations. By comparing transcriptional and post-transcriptional networks, we reveal a reversal in their relative advantages in noise suppression and rhythm transmission: negative feedback favors post-transcriptional regulation, whereas positive feedback favors transcriptional regulation. Our results reveal how the regulatory pattern fundamentally shapes gene expression fluctuations and dynamic responses.

Striatal Optogenetic Modulation Shifts Seizure Onset Timing: A Survival-Analysis Reanalysis with Effect Sizes, Permutation Validation, and Sensitivity Analysis

Siddhardha Nanda

We present a worked methods vignette, not a novel biological claim: a reanalysis of the public dataset accompanying Hyder and colleagues' report that optogenetic activation and inhibition of the dorsal striatum bidirectionally control seizures, applying a time-to-event framework to the pilocarpine-induced status epilepticus (SE) arm, the only arm with genuine per-animal event-level data rather than aggregate statistics. The original study compared latencies with a Mann-Whitney U test (ChR2 vs GFP: p = 0.0125; ArchT vs GFP: p = 0.0025). We first directly replicate that test on the same data (p = 0.0151 and p = 0.0025), confirming we analyze the same numbers, then designate the log-rank test as this reanalysis's pre-specified primary comparison, with a Cox model, a label-permutation test, a bootstrap confidence interval, and a leave-one-out analysis serving as validation checks rather than co-equal findings. ChR2 activation delays SE onset relative to its paired GFP control (hazard ratio 0.338, 95% CI 0.128-0.895; log-rank p = 0.023), and ArchT inhibition accelerates it (hazard ratio 9.18, 95% CI 1.91-44.09; log-rank p = 0.0011); a Schoenfeld-residual check found no significant proportional-hazards violation, though this check has limited power at n = 8-11 per group. Both results are confirmed by a 2,000-shuffle permutation test and survive leave-one-out, though the ChR2 comparison loses significance in 2 of 20 leave-one-out iterations, a fragility we report rather than omit. We do not claim this demonstrates anything the field did not already believe; its value is methodological, a reusable survival-analysis template with a Cox-derived effect size and documented sensitivity, extending naturally to future datasets with genuine censoring, which this one does not have.

Vision-Language Models Reproduce a Human Mental-Extrapolation Bias in the Watanabe Illusion

Eiji Watanabe, Kyohei Ueda, Kagayaki Kato, Masaki Watabe, Akiyoshi Kitaoka

Vision-language models (VLMs) are increasingly used for visual judgments, yet whether they share human perceptual biases, and where in the model such biases arise, is unclear. We addressed this with the Watanabe Illusion, a stimulus not previously studied quantitatively: an oblique segment inside a circle whose extension geometrically hits the topmost of 11 dots, yet human observers (n = 131) reported a dot near the middle of the column. Five VLMs (Qwen2.5-VL-32B and 7B, Claude Sonnet 4.6, GPT-5.5 Instant, Gemini 3.5 Flash), given the identical stimulus and wording, all deviated in the same direction. In the open-weight Qwen2.5-VL-32B, linear probes on the vision encoder recovered the line angle and the target dot six to eight times more accurately than its behavioral responses. A layer-wise logit lens showed that the correct answer dominates in the middle layers of the language model and is overwritten in late layers only under a visual-judgment frame. Naming the illusion, requesting suppression of prior knowledge, forcing a visual chain of thought, and 390 semantic preambles left the response center unchanged; only disclosing the correct answer moved it. VLMs may therefore carry human perceptual biases at the language-generation stage, where they resist declarative correction.

S1Pert: Scalable Target-Specific Gene Response Prediction with System-One Intelligence

Xiaoqi Sheng, Hou wang, Yan Liang, Yutong Li, Wenhao Du et al. (8 authors)

Predicting transcriptional responses to unseen genetic perturbations is essential for extending CRISPR screens across vast target--gene combinations. However, models can achieve strong aggregate predictive performance by reproducing historical gene-level response frequencies without adequately distinguishing the effects of different perturbations. Reliable prediction at the transcriptome scale is further challenged by uncertain response labels and redundant computation across millions of target--gene pairs. We present S1Pert, a System-One framework for target-specific gene response prediction with non-autoregressive online inference. S1Pert combines directional Welch tests and equivalence testing to distinguish statistically supported responses from inconclusive observations. A frozen System-One encoder extracts reusable semantic representations of perturbation targets, query genes, and historical evidence. Compact pair-specific interactions are then computed and cached offline. A lightweight hierarchical cross-attention decoder integrates these representations with cross-target pairwise ranking and synchronized historical-evidence dropout to reduce reliance on gene-level response frequencies. Extensive experiments demonstrate that S1Pert improves target-specific response discrimination while maintaining reliable prediction and efficient inference across diverse cellular contexts. These findings highlight the potential of System-One decision learning for scalable perturbation-response prediction beyond historical gene-level response tendencies. The source code is publicly available at https://github.com/liujiawen-jpg/S1Pert.

Neural Simulation-Based Inference for Computational Cognitive Models: A Tutorial

Ti-Fen Pan, Yi Cheng, Anne GE Collins

Computational cognitive modeling is an increasingly essential methodology to elucidate the mechanisms underlying human and animal cognition. By fitting models to empirical data, researchers can perform parameter estimation, model comparison, and model-based neural analyses to assess competing theories, quantify individual differences, and investigate neural representations. Typically, fitting these models relies on likelihood-dependent methods (e.g., Maximum likelihood estimation or hierarchical Bayesian methods). However, for many reasonable cognitive models, computing the likelihood is computationally intractable, in which case these methods become inadequate. This substantially limits the range of theories that researchers can investigate. Simulation-based inference with neural networks (neural SBI) has emerged as a powerful toolkit to overcome this hurdle. By training deep neural networks on datasets generated through model simulations, neural SBI learns the relation between observable data and cognitive models. This approach enables all the applications of standard modeling while bypassing the likelihood computation, without constraints on empirical data size. Despite its successful adoption across diverse scientific fields, neural SBI remains underutilized in cognitive modeling, partly due to a lack of accessible, domain-tailored guidance. This tutorial addresses this gap by providing a clear workflow for applying neural SBI to cognitive models. We also provide practical use cases and recommendations for reducing the computational and simulation burden, facilitating efficient implementation in real-world research settings. By lowering the technical barriers to neural SBI, this tutorial aims to empower cognitive scientists to investigate richer theory spaces and ultimately propel scientific discovery.
