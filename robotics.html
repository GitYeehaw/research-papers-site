Feedback-Linearised Trajectory Tracking via Model-Informed Robust Geometric DDPC

Pranav Gupta, Deepan Muthirayan, Ravi Banavar

Data-driven predictive control (DDPC) predicts system responses from recorded trajectories, but nonlinear dynamics can limit the accuracy of fixed linear predictors. This paper studies geometric robust DDPC for trajectory tracking of a fully actuated manipulator using model-based feedback linearisation. A mechanical model maps virtual acceleration commands into joint torques, while recorded data capture residual dynamics arising from imperfect model parameters. The controller minimises worst-case prediction error over an open Grassmannian ball centred at the fitted trajectory subspace. Simulations on a planar 2R manipulator under gravity compare the proposed method with double-integrator model predictive control (MPC) and direct regularised data-enabled predictive control (DeePC). Results show improved tracking over double-integrator MPC despite increased torque effort, while performance relative to DeePC depends on the reference trajectory.

Multimodal Masked Diffusion Autoencoder for Modeling Impaired Driving

John Gideon, Anshul Gupta, Megan Applegate-Kenton, Kayli Battel, Todd J Rowell et al. (10 authors)

Human cognitive state (e.g., distraction, stress), gaze, and behavior interact with the driving environment in subtle ways that AI could use to assist drivers, but ground-truth labels for human cognitive state levels are costly to obtain at scale. We propose a multimodal masked-diffusion autoencoder, ID-MAE, that adds a self-supervised objective to impairment prediction: alongside predicting the impairment label as a masked token, it reconstructs masked gaze and trajectory. To probe the effects of different modalities and types of impairments on model inference, we introduce DIVERT, a driving-simulator dataset containing 103 hours of driving data (n = 109) with ten impairment inductions spanning cognitive, visual, and emotional states, recorded with gaze, vehicle state, and scene video. We demonstrate how ID-MAE affords significant improvements in impairment inference results on both the DIVERT dataset and existing datasets, and enables strong results in additional tasks, such as trajectory and gaze prediction. For location-matched pairs, our model achieves a paired AUC of 0.706 for impairment detection on DIVERT, compared to 0.590 without reconstruction loss, improving over existing baselines. We probe the design space in terms of input modalities, masking scheme, positional encoding, attention mechanism, and decoding. We also investigate the interaction between various impairments in training and inference, demonstrating that visually demanding distractions are separable from cognitive distractions. Code and data will be available upon acceptance.

Learned versus Kinematic Trajectory Prediction under Reduced Observation Frequency

Nukhbala Gadzhiev, Alexander Morochko

Trajectory prediction models for automated driving are usually trained on data with a fixed sampling frequency, while positions received via vehicle-to-everything (V2X) communication arrive at 1-10 Hz. We study whether a learned predictor that uses only the observation history keeps its advantage over constant velocity (CV) and constant acceleration (CA) models when the history frequency drops. On the Argoverse 2 motion forecasting dataset, the 5 s history of the focal agent was downsampled from 10 Hz to 5, 2, and 1 Hz, and the 6 s future was predicted without maps or neighboring agents. An LSTM-based recurrent neural network (RNN) trained for each frequency was compared with the same network trained at 10 Hz and applied to sparse histories after linear interpolation. Hypotheses and thresholds were fixed before training, the main RNNs were trained with ten seeds each, and results on 24,984 test scenarios have bootstrap confidence intervals over scenarios and seeds. The average displacement error of the frequency-specific RNN grew by 3.1% at 5 Hz, 10.1% at 2 Hz, and 20.3% at 1 Hz. Contrary to our hypothesis, its advantage over the better kinematic model did not vanish on straight motion and was 24-30% on both straight motion and turns at every frequency, while for pedestrians CV was more accurate. The 10 Hz model with interpolation was at most 1.3% less accurate than the frequency-specific model down to 2 Hz but 11.5% less accurate at 1 Hz. Each hypothesis was only partly supported.

WoRD: Diffusion-Based Posterior Inference for Online Goal Recognition

Almog Anschel, Sarah Keren

Goal recognition is the task of inferring an agent's intended goal from its observed behavior. Most existing work focuses on high-level symbolic recognition, abstracting away the continuous motion through which agents reach their goal. Methods that do reason over continuous trajectories rely on an explicit dynamics model that is hard to acquire and rarely transfers across settings. Neither approach explicitly learns how agents move toward each candidate goal, limiting accuracy on ambiguous trajectory prefixes where such knowledge is most needed. We present WoRD (Waypoint Recognition with Diffusion), which uses a learned trajectory prior to infer the agent's goal from partial observations. At each step, WoRD runs one warm-started denoising chain per candidate goal, steers each chain toward the observed trajectory through a closed-form log-likelihood gradient, and reweights the resulting samples by Bayes' rule into a calibrated posterior over goals. We evaluate WoRD on simulated robot settings, pedestrian trajectories, and vehicle trajectories using four metrics targeting distinct failure modes. Across all three settings, the same inference procedure preserves calibrated multimodal beliefs through ambiguous prefixes where filter- and classifier-based observers collapse to a single mode or grow overconfident.

Capability Composition in Heterogeneous Teams via Barrier-Guided Diffusion Sampling

Diana Laura Morales, Ruoyu Lin, Magnus Egerstedt, Fabio Pasqualetti

The agents in a heterogeneous team can cooperate to accomplish tasks that exceed their individual capabilities. The team trajectories for a cooperative task can be generated by a generative model, provided that the model has been trained on demonstrations of the agents cooperating. Yet, demonstrations of cooperation are costly and may not exist for tasks that the team has never performed. The conditions under which the agents can combine their capabilities, however, are physical properties of the team and are known before any demonstration is collected. We encode the conditions as constraints on the team trajectories, and we enforce the constraints while sampling from generative models trained on demonstrations of each agent alone. We prove that the proposed method can generate every team trajectory that satisfies the constraints, although the demonstrations contain no cooperation. Hence the constraints determine which team trajectories can be generated, and the demonstrations determine how likely each team trajectory is. Numerical results on two cooperative tasks show the effectiveness of the method, which generates cooperative team trajectories that satisfy the constraints and requires no retraining when agents join or leave the team. Finally, we implement the method on a multi-robot testbed.

TCP4D: A Unified Tool-Center-Point Tracking and Generation Model for Robot Manipulation

Haosong Peng, Xiangyang Liu, Zhengshen Zhang, Yunfeng Fan, Tianyu Qi et al. (8 authors)

Robot manipulation videos provide a rich visual experience, yet recovering robot action and gripper-state annotations from RGB observations still remains challenging. Using the embodiment-agnostic tool-center-point (TCP) pose as the action space, we present TCP4D, a unified model in which a shared 4D scene representation both tracks observed TCP motion and generates future TCP trajectories. Given a monocular video and initial 2D TCP queries, the tracker directly recovers metric TCP poses in SE(3) and gripper states alongside scene geometry and camera parameters. The generator samples local features from the same 4D representation along the recovered TCP tracks, then combines them with robot observations and language instructions to predict future TCP states. Our new cross-embodiment augmentation pipeline converts existing RGB videos into demonstrations for alternative embodiments by retargeting the recovered motion and synthesizing the corresponding observations, without recorded source-robot states. Experiments show that the tracker achieves absolute trajectory errors of 7.60 and 16.07 mm on DROID and RoboLab and adapts to a new embodiment with only ten demonstrations. The generator achieves 69.19% mean success on RoboTwin and outperforms well-established policy models on two real-world tasks, while joint training on source and augmented demonstrations improves target-robot success by 41.5% over augmented-only training.

Pressure Drop: Gravity Vector Estimation from a Distributed Barometric Pressure Sensor Array

William Dubois, Nicolas Lauzon, Johann Laconte, François Pomerleau

While gravity is often used in mobile robotics as a global constraint for Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (SLAM) algorithms, most estimation methods rely on indirect observation of the gravity vector. In this paper, we propose the use of altimeter measurements to directly observe the direction of the gravity vector. We provide a theoretical evaluation of the estimation noise and validate it through extensive experimental data, along with an evaluation of the impact of intersensor distance on our gravity vector estimates. Experimental results demonstrate that a median error of $0.87^\circ$ on the tilt estimation can be achieved with our system in semi-static scenarios. Furthermore, we demonstrate the potential of this approach by comparing it with state-of-the-art methods based on Inertial Measurement Units (IMUs). We highlight that we achieve similar performance to that of IMU-based systems in highly dynamic scenarios and even outperform in specific cases of accelerometer measurement saturation. Finally, an additional experiment shows the potential use within a robotic system through the integration into an Iterative Closest Point (ICP) algorithm.

Auditing a Learned Simulator for Legged Robots: The Robotic World Model on ANYmal D

Joyjeet Singh

A legged robot can learn to walk inside a learned simulator: a neural network that predicts the robot's next state and is run forward on its own predictions. They are usually trained and checked one step ahead, although a policy uses them for a hundred steps or more. We audit one published system end to end, without a GPU: the dynamics model of the Robotic World Model (RWM) for the ANYmal D quadruped, and the uncertainty estimate of its follow-up, RWM-U. We rebuilt the model to match the released code's outputs, losses and gradients exactly, measured 101 trained simulators, 200 compressed copies and 38 simulators of three other architectures, and trained policies inside simulators that we tested in a hand-built MuJoCo model of the robot. Training on the model's own rollouts beat one-step training at 368 steps on all ten held-out episodes (registered), although one-step training was better one step ahead. One-step checks therefore misjudge design choices: pruning 30% of the weights kept 90% of the model's skill one step ahead and 39% at 2 s. Ensemble disagreement ranked rollout error well (Spearman +0.90 at 100 steps), yet covered 4.6% of outcomes at one sigma, against 68% for a calibrated estimate, as each network's own variance collapses under the implemented loss. Two checks that need no recorded future ranked some pools of simulators well, but neither was shown better than 8-step error in a primary test. At the robot level, accuracy has not predicted walking: all 18 fine-tuned copies we tested predicted better than the released model one and eight steps ahead, yet 13 gave failing policies, and registered tests of whether any check ranks simulators by their policies were not settled. In a secondary test, running the authors' policy inside each simulator ranked them well. We also report nine of our own claims that we withdrew. Scope: one robot, one gait, simulated data, no hardware.

CADENCE: Learning Acyclic Gaits for Model-Based Quadruped Locomotion

Owen Sullivan, Sourav Selvaraj, Mahdi Agheli

Model-based quadruped controllers usually step on a cyclic gait, which lifts the legs in a fixed order on a fixed period, wherever the safe footholds are. We present CADENCE, a learned step planner with a small action space. Every 40 ms, it picks one of at most 65 discrete choices: no step, or one leg's step to a safe foothold. It also predicts a swing time. We train CADENCE by reinforcement learning on top of a convex model predictive controller (MPC). Nothing prescribes a gait: there is no gait schedule, and the reward has no gait term, only a cost per step. The one structural constraint is that at most two legs may swing at once. The policy we analyzed developed an acyclic gait, with no fixed period or stepping order, yet it still paired diagonal legs. An inverse footstep model (IFM), fitted on a controller's logged steps, turns each foothold into a command. With only the IFM refitted, on fewer than 20,000 steps, CADENCE ran frozen on OCS2's nonlinear MPC. In simulated trials on a range of terrains (26% to 49% voids) and speeds (0.1 m/s and 0.2 m/s), it succeeded in 85.1% (mean of five seeds), against 67.8% for OCS2-Trot, 65.5% for OCS2-Single, and 39.6% in a static walk ablation of CADENCE. At 49% voids, it succeeded in 72.9%, against 43.0% for OCS2-Trot.

PAIR: Bringing Household Robots into the Smart Home

Junseong Pyo, Hyeongjin Kim, Jongwook Han, Seungwon Kook, Sungjib Lim et al. (6 authors)

Household robots often need to operate appliances, and smart appliances offer both control panels and device APIs. Existing embodied AI simulation environments, however, model appliances solely as physical objects. We introduce PAIR, a smart-home simulation environment grounded in the Matter protocol \footnote{https://csa-iot.org/all-solutions/matter/}, the industry standard for smart-home devices. PAIR provides 84 smart-device assets whose functional control panels and APIs act on a shared device state, with ambient conditions that evolve in response to both. From a survey of 1,443 requests, we construct a benchmark of 40 tasks that all require object manipulation yet remain solvable without API access, with 340 human demonstrations for policy training. Whether run alone, with a high-level planner, or with an API-enabled planner, two fine-tuned VLA policies complete none of the 120 evaluation episodes. Robots rarely get far enough to use the API: even with oracle plans and API access, over 90\% of failed rollouts stall at navigating, grasping, or operating a door. With a perfect oracle in place of the robot's body, the best planner still gets through only 57.3\% of its plan. When choosing between pressing a button and calling an API, several planners go by whether they can read the panel, not by API latency.\footnote{We will release our code and data upon publication.}
