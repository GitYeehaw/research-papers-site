Predicting fitness from genomic offset: a multivariate latent-trait framework

Olivier François

Genomic offset methods quantify the genetic consequences of environmental change using genotype environment associations (GEA), but the conditions under which they predict fitness remain poorly understood. Here, we develop a theoretical framework linking genomic offset to relative fitness loss through multivariate latent adaptive traits under stabilizing selection. We show that squared genomic offset is linearly related to expected log relative fitness loss when averaged over genetic architectures, provided that direct environmental effects are absent. However, this relationship does not necessarily hold for a realized genetic architecture. We identify two complementary mechanisms that can explain high predictive accuracy of genomic offset statistics. First, when effective adaptive dimensionality is sufficiently large, the genetically mediated fitness response concentrates around its marginal expectation, reducing the dependence on the particular genetic architecture. Second, even with few adaptive traits, accurate predictions can arise from geometric alignment between the complete fitness loss matrix and the GEA metric. This second mechanism also encompasses direct environmental effects and their interactions with genetically mediated responses, which may preserve or weaken predictive accuracy depending on their alignment. We further derive bounds on predictive accuracy that characterize these effects. Our results clarify the assumptions and limitations underlying genomic offset predictions and suggest that genomic offset is best interpreted as a measure of genetically informed adaptive mismatch, rather than a direct predictor of realized fitness or any particular phenotypic trait.

Path-dependent triage: Early judgments lead conversational AI chatbots to miss mental-health emergencies

Veith Weilnhammer, Raymond Dolan, Lennart Luettgau, Virginia Corno, Elise Wilkinson et al. (9 authors)

People increasingly bring mental-health concerns to consumer AI chatbots. In these conversations, clinically relevant information rarely arrives all at once. Instead, it unfolds over multiple turns, often in response to the questions the chatbot asks. Recognizing risk therefore depends on how a chatbot integrates newly disclosed information (inference) and on the questions it asks on each turn to elicit this information (context-seeking). Here we evaluated the conversational triage performance of 13 frontier AI chatbots tasked with engaging in 10-turn conversations with simulated users. Simulated users were grounded in one of 112 structured mental-health case files, each with an associated clinician-validated urgency level, from routine care to emergency. Chatbots failed to recognize one in five mental-health emergencies (20.3%), nearly four times the failure rate when all relevant clinical details were presented in a single message (5.6%). This gap was not explained by missing information: by the final turn, decisive facts had almost always been disclosed, and simply re-presenting those same facts as a single message reduced emergency under-triage to 12.1%. The problem lay instead in how the conversation unfolded, a phenomenon we refer to as path dependence. Early triage dispositions increasingly anchored later ones, such that emergencies missed early were rarely recovered as evidence accumulated. Context-seeking narrowed onto a small number of stereotyped questioning trajectories, with chatbots perseverating more than human clinicians given the same transcripts. Because the chatbot's running judgment shaped what it asked next, these two biases were plausibly coupled across turns. These dynamics are invisible to single-turn benchmarks but measurable in multi-turn evaluation, and point to concrete mitigations for safer conversational triage in consumer health AI.

Pangenome Peeling: Decomposing Weighted Adjacencies into Path-Forest Genomes

Alitzel López Sánchez, Jiazhen Leng, Natasha Ivanova, Lingling Jin, David Sankoff

One aspect of pangenome analysis that has received little attention is the recovery of the individual genomes that gave rise to a pangenome graph. While substantial effort has focused on constructing pangenomes from collections of genomes, the inverse problem remains largely unexplored: given a pangenome represented as a set of weighted adjacencies, can we reconstruct the constituent linear genomes that underlie it? Thus we introduce the pangenome peeling problem, which seeks to decompose a weighted adjacency graph into a collection of linear genomes whose combined adjacencies reproduce the observed edge weights. This problem is particularly relevant when considering ancestral pangenomes, where the graph structure may be known or inferred, but the underlying genomes are not. As a first step toward addressing this problem, we formally define pangenome peeling and derive a lower bound on the minimum number of genomes required to explain a given weighted adjacency graph. We then present two reconstruction methods: one based on the sequential addition of edges and a second based on extracting maximum-spanning-tree structures. Both methods produce genome collections that exactly explain the input adjacency data. To evaluate the recovered sets of genomes, we compare them to the set of input genomes using a maximum-weight matching framework based on the double-cut-and-join distance with indels. Here the maximum-spanning tree approach is superior, producing sets of constituent genomes close to the simulations while the sequential addition of edges approach yields constituent genomes increasingly divergent from the input as genomes sizes increase.

A hair-thin implant opens the entire depth of the mouse brain to revisiting the same structures at subcellular resolution

Miroslav Stibůrek, Tomáš Pikálek, Petra Ondráčková, Kristina Herzánová, Adrian Lorenz et al. (16 authors)

Ultrathin multimode-fibre endoscopes provide minimally invasive access to subcellular structures throughout the depth of the mouse brain. Here we introduce a chronically implantable interface with a diameter of 144 μm, about the thickness of a human hair, that extends this capability to longitudinal imaging, enabling repeated access to the same fine structures over extended periods. The system supports weeks-long tracking of individual dendritic spines, allowing quantitative comparison of their dynamics and survival across dendritic compartments. We further demonstrate longitudinal intracellular calcium imaging during spontaneous and stimulus-evoked activity in the same cells across days, as well as repeated measurements of blood flow in the same vessels across multiple brain regions. This chronic endomicroscopic platform provides stable, minimally invasive optical access for longitudinal subcellular-resolution imaging of brain structure, activity and blood flow across depth.

Resolving non-neutrality across scales in a hyperdiverse tropical forest

Pietro Zgaga, Pablo Villegas, Tancredi Caruso, Tommaso Gili

Different ecological processes operate at different spatial and temporal scales. Macroscopic regularities can therefore be captured by descriptions that ignore microscopic details, even when those details are mechanistically relevant to ecological processes. Here, we develop a framework inspired by statistical mechanics to track interspecific spatial correlations under spatial coarse-graining. To do so we combine random-matrix filtering with a spatially explicit neutral model. Applying this approach to eight censuses of the Barro Colorado Island tropical forest, we observe a continuous crossover from an interacting to an effectively neutral organization. Below a few tens of metres (20--35 m), the empirical correlation spectrum differs from neutral expectations, revealing scale-dependent species-specific structure. Part of this structure can be attributed to environmental heterogeneity. However, residual spatial associations provide candidate signatures of biotic interactions or other unmeasured ecological, non-neutral processes. With increasing spatial coarse-graining, empirical and neutral correlation spectra progressively converge, and above the crossover scale the neutral model provides an accurate description of the measured collective organization. Neutrality thus emerges as an effective description of the collective organization of the forest above a characteristic scale, without implying that species are equivalent at the individual level. Our framework provides an operational way to locate that scale.

Navigating Selection Bias in Genome-wide Association Studies (GWAS)

Ciarrah-Jane S Barry, Tahereh Gholipourshahraki, Zoltán Kutalik, Bjarni J Vilhjálmsson, Esben Agerbo

Selection bias arises when inclusion in a genetic study depends on traits, or causes of traits, that are associated with genotype. It is present in volunteer-based biobanks, case-control designs and studies of disease progression, and genome-wide association study (GWAS) summary statistics from affected samples can propagate this bias into post-GWAS analyses, such as polygenic scores (PGS), Mendelian randomization (MR) and genetic correlation analyses. Despite this broad potential impact, selection bias is sometimes ignored in genetic studies. Many methods exist to detect and correct it, but they rely on different assumptions and require specific data. We organise ten methods applicable to GWAS by the decisions an analyst faces: whether to diagnose or correct, which data are available (individual-level or summary-level), and which form of selection is operating (non-representative participation, case-control ascertainment, index events, sex-differential participation or survival to recruitment). For each method, we describe its principle and assumptions, and we compare methods addressing the same form of selection by their assumptions, data requirements and limitations. A companion GitHub repository implements nine methods in R, prepares input for the external SPREG software, and provides worked examples on simulated data with known true effects for each form of selection, one of which we present in full. An interactive tool supports method selection. We conclude with recommendations for bias-aware study design, sensitivity analysis and reporting.

Decoding spatial and semantic information from large-scale visual cortical activity for natural-movie reconstruction

Yukai Huang, Haoyu Albert Wang, Jiawei Zhang, Jialun Ma, Jiayao Ding et al. (9 authors)

Reconstructing natural visual experience from cortical population activity is a central challenge in neural decoding, particularly for calcium imaging, where signals are noisy, temporally filtered, and paired neural-video data are limited. Here we present Ca2REAL (Calcium Activity to Reconstruct Events, Appearance, and Layout), a neural-to-video framework that conditions a pretrained video diffusion model through separate layout and semantic controls derived from calcium activity. Using measured responses from 7,863 V1 neurons in one mouse, Ca2REAL reconstructed held-out natural-movie clips with 81.4% all-pairs stimulus-identification accuracy, with its clearest gains over deterministic baselines in perceptual and semantic measures. We further evaluated the framework in a stimulus-aligned virtual population of 116,705 neurons assembled from encoding-model-predicted responses across 11 independently recorded datasets. Perturbation and ablation analyses showed that reconstruction depends on distributed, stimulus-specific population structure, with layout and semantic pathways providing complementary spatial and identity information. Temporal analyses further showed improved preservation of semantic dynamics across reconstructed clips. These results show that large-scale cortical population activity constrains the spatial, semantic, and temporal structure of natural visual experience, providing a generative framework for high-fidelity neural reconstruction of dynamic visual scenes.

Synchronized Optimal Transport Reconstructs Coupled Transcriptomic and Chromatin Dynamics

Jingfeng Wang, Yanxiang Zhao, Qing Nie, Zixuan Cang

Single-cell RNA and ATAC data collected across developmental stages provide time-course snapshots of transcriptional and regulatory states. Since sequencing is destructive, trajectories between these snapshots must be inferred from independently sampled cell populations. Existing approaches typically infer continuous dynamics in a single modality or integrate RNA and ATAC in a shared representation. Here we present Cross-Omics Aligned Trajectory Inference (COATI), which extends unbalanced synchronized dynamical optimal transport to time-dependent cross-modal maps for reconstructing coupled trajectories in distinct RNA and ATAC state spaces. We evaluated COATI on synthetic benchmarks and four developmental datasets using held-out-stage reconstruction and lineage analyses. COATI improved reconstruction of cell populations at held-out stages, including recovery of chromatin-state heterogeneity within cell types, and better preserved developmental lineage relationships. The unbalanced formulation allows the inference of population mass changes, which were shown to associate with DNA-replication programs at early stages and lineage-maturation programs later in development. The coupled trajectories enabled analysis of lineage-specific transcriptional and chromatin changes and their temporal relationships during development. Lineage responses inferred by COATI agreed with independent CRISPR perturbation results, and the inferred regulatory patterns were consistent with independent epigenetic data.

Protein B-factor modeling with local magnitude

Wanying Bi, Jian Liu, Hongsong Feng

Topological magnitude characterizes the effective size and spatial organization of metric spaces, offering a geometric approach with potential applications in molecular biology and materials science. Previous work has demonstrated its utility in modeling protein flexibility from structure, but global magnitude weighting requires dense linear systems whose computational cost increases with protein size. We introduce a local magnitude representation that retains residue-level structural information through nearest-neighbor environments. The construction is well posed and geometrically invariant, with central weighting distinguished from scalar neighborhood magnitude. Across 364 proteins, the mean within-protein fitting Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC) increases from 0.725 for global magnitude to 0.728 for local magnitude. Blind-prediction experiments on 345 proteins combine each representation with identical auxiliary descriptors and evaluate random forests and gradient boosting under three validation protocols. Local features improve the primary PCC in all six model--protocol comparisons, with a maximum absolute increase of 0.008 in protein-level ten-fold gradient boosting. For a fixed feature bank, local factorization costs $O(Nk^3)$ compared with $O(N^3)$ for global factorization, where $N$ is the number of C$_α$ atoms and $k$ the neighborhood size. Timing measurements show a computational advantage of local magnitude on large structures. Within the tested single-seed configuration and distinct global and local scale banks, these results support local magnitude as a compact representation that preserves useful B-factor information while reducing feature-extraction costs for large proteins.

Hybrid Quantum-Classical Annealing for Protein Side-Chain Optimization: A Feasibility Study

Valeria B. C. Visani, Simone Reale, Alfonso Gautieri, Elisabetta Di Nitto

Hybrid quantum-classical annealing solvers are moving from research laboratories toward industrial use, but their advantage over classical optimization appears to be confined to a narrow class of problems. A recent systematic benchmark of the D-Wave hybrid solver against industry-leading classical solvers, across several classes of optimization problems, found it to be most competitive on binary quadratic programs, while noting that few industrial problems take this form. Therefore, identifying real-world industrial problems that can be modeled with this structure is a key challenge. We propose protein side-chain optimization, a core step of computational protein design, as one of these problems: it is natively a binary quadratic program, and its application context requires pools of low-energy solutions, which annealers naturally return. In this feasibility study, we test whether the competitiveness of the hybrid solver against industry-leading classical solvers extends to this problem. On 40 instances derived from 20 experimentally determined protein structures with up to about 200 amino acids and 2000 binary variables, we compare the results achieved with a hybrid solver, run for 5 seconds, against those of the commercial solver Gurobi, run for 10 seconds, 1000 seconds, and 4 hours. The hybrid solver reaches the certified optimum whenever one is available, matches Gurobi's performance on smaller instances, and outperforms it on larger instances, finding lower-energy solutions. These results show that the competitiveness of the hybrid solver on binary quadratic programs, so far observed only on a synthetic benchmark, extends to a real-world problem. This motivates comparisons with classical heuristics specific to protein side-chain optimization.
