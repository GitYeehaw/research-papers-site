Same Behavior, Different Mechanism: What Finite Experiments Can Certify About Integrated Information

Linqian Wu, Diaoxuan He

Interpretability, AI auditing and assessments of machine consciousness increasingly rest on mechanism-level quantities: functionals of a system's causal transition law that describe how it computes, not what it outputs. Experiments see that law only through finite, partial and noisy interventions. We ask when such a quantity is identifiable from an experiment's access, continuous in the mechanism and certifiable from finite data, and answer exactly for a deliberately demanding case - the integrated information $\varphi$ of integrated information theory - in stochastic networks of four and five units with known ground truth. Behavior can be silent: a recurrent and a feed-forward network with identical input-output laws at their response delay carry 0.28 and 0 bits, yet internal recordings separate them within 4-53 trials. Partial access can be just as blind: in our examples, units left out of a one-step recording impose precision floors of 0.30-1.02 bits that no sample size lowers, and in all-but-self parity networks, recordings of any length see several hidden units only through the product of their fidelities - a symmetry that one intervention broke in exact checks. The measure has sharp edges: because $\varphi$ selects a partition, and the cause and effect it specifies, by one criterion and reports another, infinitesimal changes of mechanism that cross an edge change it by 25-100% at 99% of the edges we found in random networks, and about 1% of surrogate models that fit interventional data almost perfectly misreport it by more than 20%. One-sided claims survive: the same data certify a lower bound on both sides of an edge, and we prove $\varphi>1.004$ bits throughout a 64-dimensional region of mechanisms and certify it from about $10^7$ controlled transitions with false-certificate probability at most $α$. Measurability is thus a design question with computable answers.

Kinetic models of a K channel with 2 stage voltage sensor activation and an opening step

S. R. Vaccaro

The activation of a K channel with 2 voltage sensors and a separate opening step may be described by a 4 state kinetic equation. If $k_{o}$ and $k_{c}$ are the opening and closing rates, and $α$ and $β$ are the voltage sensor rates such that $k_{o}$ $\ggα,k_{c}\ggβ$ and $k_{o}$ $\gg k_{c}$, the kinetic equation may be reduced to a 3 state model where the effective backward rate from the activated-open state is $2βk_{c}/(k_{o}+k_{c})$. However, if a K channel voltage sensor activates in 2 stages, and there is a separate opening step, the occupation probabilities of the states of the channel satisfy a 10 state kinetic equation. When the time constants of the characteristic equation for each voltage sensor differ by an order of magnitude, and $k_{o}$ $\gg k_{c}$, the open state probability is proportional to $n^{2}$, where $n$ satisfies a rate equation, and the derived rates have a similar voltage dependence to the rates $α_{n}$ and $β_{n}$ from voltage clamp data.

Geometric Relevance Is Not Sufficient: Evaluating Focused Post-Training Supervision in Diffusion Docking

Michael Brown, Maryam Rahimimovassagh, Mehdi Yazdani-Jahromi, Sanjeda Sara Jennifer, Ivan Garibay

Diffusion-based molecular docking requires learning a corrective score field across a broad corruption distribution, yet individual pose errors can differ substantially in their structural consequences. This creates a post-training conflict between preserving broad score-field coverage and concentrating supervision on corrections that appear more relevant to docking geometry. Using DiffDock, a fixed-budget study compares broad continuation with torsional supervision focused on Cartesian consequence or conditional target consistency, and with a broad/focused mixture. Evaluation separates fixed score fidelity, generated-pose quality, best-of-10 sampling capacity, and confidence-selected Top-1 docking performance. At <5 Angstrom, broad continuation had the highest mean Top-1 success among trained policies, and focused or mixed supervision showed no consistent advantage under the tested budget. The starting model had better fixed score fidelity, whereas the evaluated broad-continuation model had higher mean Top-1 success with the same aggregate best-of-10 recovery. Focusing supervision on structurally consequential or more consistently specified corrections did not consistently improve returned-pose success over broad continuation.

Do sEMG Foundation Models Improve Cross-Subject Generalisation?

William Raftery, Dario Farina

sEMG Foundation Models aim to learn general purpose representations over diverse subjects and recording conditions that boost performance on a variety of downstream tasks. However, existing evaluations make it difficult to discern whether pretraining alone is sufficient to combat the cross-subject overfitting which makes machine learning on sEMG so challenging. We conduct a systematic evaluation of three sEMG foundation models across three gesture classification datasets under both intra-session and inter-subject splits, using three fine-tuning strategies designed to isolate the contribution of pretraining. We find that pretraining boosts performance when train and test samples are drawn from the same recording session, but this benefit largely disappears under inter-subject evaluation. In low subject count datasets, pretraining provides little to no benefit and classical ML baselines match or exceed foundation model performance.

MUSS: A Multi-scale and Sequence-based Model for Single-cell Gene Regulation

Kangjie Zheng, Amirhossein Vahidi, Liying Jin, Joseph Clarke, Vijaya Baskar MS et al. (10 authors)

Modeling gene regulation in single cells requires jointly representing DNA sequence, chromatin accessibility, and gene expression across regulatory scales. Sequence-based models capture DNA-level regulatory grammar but often lack cell-level resolution, whereas many single-cell omics models represent genes and peaks as fixed-vocabulary tokens without their underlying sequence. Here, we present MUSS, a sequence-based multi-scale model for single-cell gene regulation that preserves both DNA-level and cell-level resolution. MUSS derives peak- and gene-level representations from DNA using a frozen pre-trained sequence encoder and integrates them through peak-peak self-attention and distance-biased peak-gene interactions. Peak masked language modeling followed by training on paired scATAC-seq and scRNA-seq aligns these representations with cell-specific regulatory states. Across gene expression prediction, enhancer-gene linking, motif-guided regulatory prioritization, and zero-shot cell clustering, MUSS achieves strong performance against sequence-based and single-cell omics baselines. It further predicts expression for genes and peaks unseen during task training. These results support sequence-grounded, multi-scale representations for decoding cell-type-specific regulatory programs. Code, datasets, and model checkpoints are available at https://github.com/Lotfollahi-lab/MUSS_neurips26.

Leveraging deep generative modelling at the agent level in discrete-event simulations

Margherita Molaro, Matthew M. Graham, Oliver Watson, Rachel E. Murray-Watson, Martin Chalkley et al. (9 authors)

Emulators are often invoked to facilitate parameter inference in computationally intensive agent-based simulations (ABSs). Most approaches, however, require a large sample of pre-existing outputs on which such emulators can be trained. For computationally intensive simulations, this sample can be prohibitively expensive to generate. We propose a new approach to leverage emulators at the agent level in event-based ABSs to reduce the computational requirements of the simulation itself. We demonstrate this in a computationally intensive ``multi-disease'' ABS, Thanzi La Onse, which self-consistently simulates a wide range of medical conditions in a population. By applying conditional tabular generative adversarial networks to replace the modelling of one of the simulated causes of ill health, road traffic injuries (RTIs), we are able to improve its simulation run-time by 86.6% (95% bootstrap confidence interval [85.2, 88.1]), while recovering the evolution of cause-specific mean disability adjusted life years with a 13% error, despite the low assumed incidence of fatal RTIs (~2%). Crucially, we do so while retaining the full ABS framework, and correctly recovering multi-disease dynamics. This represents a crucial step towards enabling full-model emulators for very computationally intensive simulations, and making large ABS amenable to rigorous parameter inference and a full understanding of parameter sensitivity.

Reversible Dynamics, Asymmetric Actions: Parking as a Trajectory-Level Affordance

Adam Y. Shavit

Drivers are often told to back into a tight parking space, not drive in nose first. The advice looks paradoxical, because a slow car is dynamically reversible: reverse its speed and replay the steering backward, and it retraces a feasible path. Played backward, though, reverse-in shows a car driving forward out of the space. Reverse-in and forward-in are different tasks: they share a start position but end facing opposite ways. We separate three clearances between car and obstacles. Static fit $F$ is the clearance the parked car keeps at its best acceptable destination pose. Eventual access $A_\infty$ is the clearance it can keep all the way there with unlimited corrections. Resource-bounded access $A_K$ is the clearance that survives a budget of $K$ reversals, switches between forward and reverse. They satisfy $F\ge A_\infty\ge A_K$. In one fully specified scene (one car, bay, aisle and start), a certified argument proves $A_\infty=F=0.5000$ m for both tasks. In this scene, all certified forward/reverse asymmetry therefore arises at finite $K$: it lies in how many corrections each task needs, not in the clearance either can eventually keep. With one reversal, an explicit path gives reverse-in at least $0.4410$ m clearance. A certified reachability argument caps forward-in's one-reversal clearance at $d_0<0.1720002$ m. With two reversals, forward-in gets at least $0.2833$ m. To keep a clearance strictly above any $d$ with $d_0\le d<0.2833$ m, reverse-in thus needs exactly one reversal and forward-in exactly two when both tasks move forward first. If both move in reverse first, the counts are two and three. The contrast depends on the start position (Section 4.15). Proposed experiments test whether observers distinguish fit, eventual access and budgeted access. They compare these with simpler cues, such as how the turning body sweeps, and learned manoeuvre labels.

Invariance before structure: two modes of emergent compression in representation learning

Hardik Rajpal, Dan Goodman

Learned representations show striking invariance across biological and artificial neural networks, with different substrates and variations driven by drift, heterogeneity and initialisation. To understand this invariance, we propose Emergent Adaptive Representation Learning (EARL), a framework that distinguishes two modes of emergence in learned representations: state-space compression, where a learned representation gains predictive power over its own future beyond what the network's units can predict individually, and parameter-space compression, where networks with different micro-level parameters converge to the same representation. We formalise both as Fisher sensitivities and introduce ensemble-level information-theoretic measures for each. In a recurrent reservoir with a trained bottleneck, parameter-space compression rises within the first few epochs of training and partly independently of the task, whereas state-space compression rises about 30 epochs later and only when the network begins to learn the temporal structure of the task. These results suggest that invariance to the substrate and task-specific emergent structure are acquired in distinct phases of learning.

Self-Supervised Learning of Local Predictive Directions as a Neural Computation

Marco Zenari, Xuehao Ding, Daniel Burnham, Austin R. Ellis-Mohr, Anirvan M. Sengupta et al. (6 authors)

Natural sensory dynamics are globally nonlinear but often exhibit simple local geometry, with nearby trajectories evolving preferentially along a small number of predictive directions. We ask how neural circuits could discover this geometry directly from temporal experience. We introduce Local Predictive Directions (LPD), a self-supervised framework that learns local directions whose projections maximally predict near-future trajectory evolution. LPD applies past-future canonical correlation analysis to temporal differences, making the learned representation invariant to translations of the underlying state while avoiding estimation of equilibria or a global dynamical model. To represent systems with multiple predictive regimes, we introduce a non-negative similarity-matching objective, with offline and online algorithms, that partitions trajectory segments into predictive domains, each associated with a distinct local predictive direction. The online algorithm admits a natural neural-network interpretation: the resulting activities provide soft domain assignments, while synaptic weights encode the corresponding local predictive filters. On stochastic nonlinear dynamical systems, LPD recovers the local predictive geometry and partitions trajectories into distinct dynamical regimes. On naturalistic sensory streams, the online algorithm produces center-surround spatial receptive fields and ON/OFF sustained and transient temporal filters resembling those of early visual neurons. Our results suggest that predictive learning can provide a self-supervised mechanism by which neural circuits discover the local geometry of environmental dynamics and represent it in receptive fields.

Static Recovery Is Not Dynamic Stability: Dynamics-Aware Benchmarking of Protein Motif Scaffolding

Emile de Bruyn, Moritz Schäffler, Jannik Schneider, Sebastian R. Schmidt, Chanwoong Hwang et al. (12 authors)

Protein motif scaffolding benchmarks assess whether generative models can build scaffolds around functionally important residues. Their conclusions depend on which motifs are tested and which criteria define success. Existing benchmarks typically use small hand-curated motif sets and static refolding criteria, but protein function often depends on conformational ensembles and transitions. Here, we address both benchmark scope and success criteria. We introduce BILBY-PRO, a systematically derived benchmark of structurally conserved functional motifs from PROSITE-linked experimental structures, comprising 220 cases from 174 PROSITE motif-pattern entries. Evaluating six recent scaffold-generation models reveals distinct patterns in target coverage and unique-solution yield: RFDiffusion and Proteina solve more targets, whereas ESM3 produces more unique successful scaffolds on the targets it solves. We further introduce BILBY-MD, a reference-based all-atom molecular dynamics evaluation framework. Applied to statically solved designs from the original MotifBench benchmark, it compares motif drift, scaffold drift, motif-contact preservation, and motif-dihedral free-energy landscapes against reference simulations. Of 55 statically solved model-problem pairs, 31 satisfy our MD-supported criterion and only 8 pass all checks. Compact, self-contained motifs are often MD-supported, whereas interface-like or context-dependent motifs fail more frequently. Static refolding success therefore provides useful evidence of local motif recovery but does not establish dynamical fidelity of the full scaffold. BILBY highlights three complementary axes of motif-scaffolding evaluation: target coverage, unique-solution yield, and MD-supported dynamical fidelity.
