Automatic Generation of Novel Highly-Functional Molecular Sequences

Vincent R. Ragusa, Chrisoph Adami

While multiple methods now exist to generate functional sequence variants from existing annotated examples, it is unclear how well such methods can be used to generate sequences that exceed the functionality of those contained in the example set. Using two complete fitness landscapes, we quantify how effectively generative tools can search sequence space and find rare, novel, highly functional sequences, focusing on variational auto-encoders and information-theoretic methods. We find that an information-theoretic method that does not require training and runs significantly faster than standard Machine-Learning/Artificial Intelligence (ML/AI) methods also is significantly more effective in finding rare highly-functional sequences. We discuss the application of this novel method to discover rare polypeptides with anti-bacterial properties that are specific to bacterial, but not eukaryotic, cell membranes.

Can AI Do Formal Demography? Why Vaccinating the Oldest Saves Both the Most Lives and the Most Years of Life

Joshua R. Goldstein, Kenneth W. Wachter

Can AI contribute to formal demography? We report an experiment in which an AI model, Claude, was asked to explain and then simplify the proof of an earlier result: that vaccinating the oldest against COVID-19 saves both the most lives and the most years of life, because the years saved per person treated rise with age. The model proposed a new proof that is shorter, more intuitive, and somewhat more general than the original, built from a known life-table identity and the definition of convexity. It states the condition for the result as the convexity of the reciprocal of the hazard, which can be checked from the hazard alone and holds for most familiar parametric models, and it yields a lower bound on life expectancy whenever that condition is met. Prompted by a further question, the model also gave an exact form of the condition, showing how far convexity can be relaxed. The model traced these identities and related results in earlier literature. An edited record of the sessions is provided, and complete transcripts are available on request. The exercise suggests that AI can revisit established results, make them easier to understand, and prove valuable generalizations.

In-depth data movement characterization of DNA read mapping algorithms

Berkan Şahin, Ecem İlgün, Klea Zambaku, Can Alkan

Read mapping algorithms represent a critical bottleneck in genomic analysis pipelines due to the resources required; a problem that is becoming more pronounced as sequencing technologies advance and datasets expand. We present an extensive workload characterization of leading read mapping algorithms across diverse sequencing technologies to identify hardware acceleration and optimization opportunities. We analyzed the following implementations: MashMap, wfmash, and Minimap2 for whole-genome alignment (WGA); NGMLR, Minimap2, and LRA for long reads; and Bowtie2, BWA-MEM, StrobeAlign, and minibwa for short reads. We used the Intel VTune Profiler for detailed memory analysis to identify memory- bound functions, cache usage patterns, and data transfer bottlenecks. We further analyzed a selected subset of alignment tools using the DAMOV-SIM simulator to obtain a more granular view of data movement characteristics for two different cache structures. Based on our results, Minimap2 emerges as the fastest alignment tool for long read scenarios, but falls behind in whole-genome alignment due to poor multithreading. Across all three categories, some programs (e.g., LRA, BWA-MEM) are highly memory-bound. More in-depth analysis via DAMOV-SIM revealed that some memory-bound tools, in particular, do not fully utilize the multi-level cache hierarchy in modern multi-core CPU architectures. Our results show that while some tools require algorithmic redesign for performance gains, some others can benefit from running on PIM architectures.

RangeFinder: Predicting Ligand Property Ranges from Protein Pockets

Alan Nadelsticher Ruvalcaba, Chris Alvin, Supratik Mukhopadhyay, Adam Bess

Identifying ligands for a protein target requires searching a vast chemical space for a comparatively small set of compatible molecules. Existing machine learning approaches primarily address this problem by generating candidate ligands or scoring individual protein-ligand pairs, but provide limited information about the broader chemical space compatible with a binding pocket. We introduce RangeFinder, an SE(3)-equivariant transformer pocket-level model, trained and evaluated on BioLiP2 data, that predicts ranges over nine physicochemical ligand properties directly from pocket structure, chemistry, and evolutionary context. Using an intersection-over-union objective and evaluation metric (range-IoU), RangeFinder achieves a range-IoU of $0.464 \pm 0.002$, outperforming all deployable baselines while requiring only $12.2$ ms per pocket. We also quantify our method's ability to reduce the ligand search space while retaining compatible binders; in this regard, RangeFinder achieves a Reduction-Recall F-Score of $0.543 \pm 0.011$ --- once again outperforming all deployable baselines. Ablation experiments indicate that RangeFinder's performance depends more strongly on the density and quality of ligand supervision than on any individual input modality or specialized training strategy. Together, these results position RangeFinder as an inexpensive and effective molecular filtering prior, with demonstrated potential in narrowing the ligand search space of target pockets before docking, generation, or synthesis. The code for this project can be found at: https://github.com/alannadels/RangeFinder.

Intrinsic network dynamics catalyze switching in functional connectivity states of the brain

Steven S. Kang, Dae-Jin Kim

Dynamic functional connectivity (dFC) reflects ongoing reconfiguration of large-scale brain networks, but the temporal organization and network processes underlying spontaneous state transitions remain poorly understood. We developed functional connectivity state switching (FCSS), a phase-coherence-based framework that tracks multiscale changes in dominant whole-brain connectivity configurations. In Human Connectome Project fMRI, FCSS was strongly evoked by cognitive state and condition switches, and distinct transition dynamics differentially predicted working-memory performance. During rest, spontaneous FCSS was characterized by progressive changes in network segregation, integration, and cross-network communication as connectivity approached a switching event. Critically, event-related analysis time-locked to spontaneous FCSS revealed opposing pre-switch dynamics: salience-network contribution increased while default-mode-network contribution decreased, suggesting coordinated engagement and disengagement of these networks before switching. This reciprocal reweighting may provide a network-level mechanism for spontaneous FC reconfiguration. Together, these findings identify both global reorganization and network-specific processes underlying spontaneous and task-evoked brain-state transitions in the human brain.

Keep on growing or change direction? Dependence of clonal plant survival on the probability of side-branching

Róbert Juhász, Beáta Oborny

In clonal organisms, the genetic individual (genet) may consist of several physiological individuals (ramets). Clonal plants typically grow horizontally by means of stolons or other clonal growth organs. Some species grow linearly for several ramet generations, while others produce side branches frequently. We hypothesize that the probability of side-branching ($b$) affects genet survival ($G$) depending on the ramets' ratio of birth to death rates ($λ$). We used a spatially explicit model of ramet population dynamics to compare three growth strategies. In the neutral case, growing forward and side-branching were equally probable, whereas forward-growing and side-branching plants had lower and higher $b$, respectively. We found that the genet's survival threshold (the critical $λ_c$) strongly depended on the branching probability. The lowest threshold, and hence the broadest range of survival, occurred in moderately forward-growing plants, in which the average length of linear (non-branching) intervals was $1.89$ units. Above the threshold, the $G(b)$ relationship strongly depended on $λ$. Genet survival was promoted by forward growth in sparse ramet populations (low $λ$). However, this advantage gradually disappeared as $λ$ increased, and branching probability became effectively neutral across a broad parameter range encompassing both forward-growing and side-branching plants. This suggests strong selection for low branching intensity in unfavorable habitats, but weak or negligible selection in favorable habitats.

HistoTx: Early fusion of H&E images and spatial transcriptomics at varying spatial transcriptomics resolution with self-supervised learning

Alice Driessen, Emil Malmsten, Steffen Wolf, Bernd Illing, Isinsu Katircioglu et al. (14 authors)

Spatial transcriptomics maps gene expression across tissue sections and, when integrated with whole slide images, jointly captures both morphological architecture and molecular activity to provide a rich, multimodal view of tissue biology. ML approaches combining spatial transcriptomics and WSIs typically learn a mapping from morphological features to molecular profiles via contrastive alignment or supervised fine-tuning of pathology foundation models. In both cases, transcriptomics serves as a training signal rather than a true input modality, leaving inference fundamentally image-driven. We propose an early-fusion vision transformer architecture in which transcript tokens are constructed at arbitrary spatial resolution, merged with image patch tokens, and jointly processed through a shared transformer stack for deep cross-modal interaction. Starting from a pretrained vision-only pathology foundation model, we train HistoTx by continuing self-supervised pretraining on paired image and spatial transcriptomics data and demonstrate that: 1) at inference with image only, performance remains on par with the baseline on most tasks while improving on tasks that inherently benefit from molecular knowledge, such as gene expression prediction; 2) when both modalities are available at inference, jointly providing image and transcript inputs outperforms either modality alone.

AVID: A 5T fMRI Dataset for Benchmarking Auditory-induced Visual Mental Imagery Decoding

Shiqi Shen, Shurui Li, Xilin Zhang, Yuanning Li

Visual mental imagery (VMI) decoding uses brain activity to reconstruct internally generated visual scenes, providing a potential pathway for externalizing internal experiences and developing neuroprosthetic communication interfaces. However, progress is limited by the lack of dedicated, training-scale VMI datasets. Current VMI paradigms often rely on visual prompts or short-term memory images, introducing visual and mnemonic confounds that make it difficult to disentangle neural activity associated with endogenous image construction from signals driven by prior or concurrent visual input. Furthermore, existing VMI datasets offer limited descriptive control over individual visual attributes, making it difficult to systematically assess fine grained visual details. To address these gaps, we introduce AVID, the first dedicated training-scale 5.0 Tesla functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) resource for auditory-induced VMI decoding. AVID comprises approximately 67 hours of dense recordings from nine participants. The dataset is structured into two complementary splits: a scene-level complex split using naturalistic Mandarin descriptions and a controlled simple split with explicitly specified visual attributes. We further formalize a standardized evaluation protocol that decouples source-image consistency from semantic alignment, allowing for a more nuanced assessment of imagery reconstruction. Baseline qualitative and quantitative results show that current reconstruction pipelines remain limited on AVID, although the most successful reconstructions preserve recognizable scene level semantics from spoken descriptions. By combining dedicated training-scale neural recordings and standardized evaluation resources, AVID provides both a data foundation for imagery-specific model development and a benchmark for evaluating VMI decoding with substantially reduced sensory contamination.

Substrate hypergraph cycles: unique steady states and persistence for every enzyme assignment; oscillations enabled by enzyme sharing

Badal Joshi, Tung D. Nguyen

We associate a directed substrate hypergraph with an enzymatic reaction system. The hypergraph records which substrates each composite reaction consumes and produces, and each composite reaction is realized by a Henri--Michaelis--Menten (HMM) module whose enzyme may be distinct, shared with other reactions, or absent. We call the hypergraph a cycle when it is strongly connected and every substrate is produced by exactly one reaction and consumed by exactly one reaction, each time with coefficient one; equivalently, its dual is a strongly connected ordinary directed graph. For a cycle, every HMM realization, under every enzyme assignment and for all positive rate constants, has exactly one positive steady state in each stoichiometric compatibility class that contains a strictly positive point, and no steady state on the boundary of such a class, and every trajectory in such a class is persistent. The interior dynamics, in contrast, depend on how enzymes are shared. When each enzyme catalyzes at most one reaction, every trajectory in such a class converges to its unique steady state. With enzyme sharing, convergence can fail even for the three-substrate transformation cycle $X_1\to X_2\to X_3\to X_1$: if two transformations share an enzyme and the third has its own enzyme or none, the steady state can undergo a supercritical Hopf bifurcation that produces a stable limit cycle. Thus the substrate structure controls existence, uniqueness, and persistence, while the pattern of enzyme sharing controls the interior dynamics. The results extend to weighted cycles, which allow higher stoichiometric coefficients, and to longer chains of intermediates.

Prior Action Control Causally Alters Language-Model Plasticity

Marc-Andre Schulz

Two copies of one language model start from identical weights and receive the same rewards during training. For one copy the rewards depend on its own answers. The other copy is yoked: it receives the first copy's rewards no matter what it answers, a design taken from the learned-helplessness experiments of animal learning. We ask whether the copy whose rewards depended on its answers, the copy with control, later learns a new task faster, that is, whether control changes the model's plasticity. In Experiment 1, copies of Qwen3-0.6B generated their own answers in a two-turn game and afterwards learned a new task. The copies with control learned the new task faster in every pair, and the advantage held on different starting models as well as on the larger Qwen3-1.7B. Copies with no reward signal, trained only on the updates that every copy shared, learned about as fast as the yoked copies. The advantage is therefore mostly a benefit of control, not damage from lacking it. Control had also changed how the copies answered, so the twins practiced different answers. That practice could carry part of the advantage, and Experiment 2 removed the difference. Both copies of a pair trained on the same answers, fixed in advance, in the same order, and received the same number of rewards. Only one copy's rewards followed the answers, and that copy again learned a new task faster. The advantage of the copies with control over their yoked twins could be forecast before the new task began from each copy's weights. In sum, control over rewards during earlier training left models faster at learning a new task later on, and a conventional model evaluation need not show it.
