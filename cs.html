Latent Lattice Language Models

Shivam Duggal, Zongze Wu, William T. Freeman, Antonio Torralba, Richard Zhang et al. (6 authors)

Language models typically increase per-token computation through deeper or looped layers and latent reasoning steps, while richer residual connections improve information flow across layers. We instead increase computational capacity: the number of active computational states a model maintains. We introduce the Lattice of Latents, a set of per-layer horizontal latent streams that run recurrently along time, alongside the vertical residual stream that flows through depth. Like hyper-connections, mHC, and attention or value residuals, the Lattice enhances the residual stream, but rather than adding or rerouting residual pathways through depth, it provides companion computational states that persist across tokens. Unlike hybrid Transformer-SSM architectures, whose recurrent states primarily serve as internal memory for sequence mixing, these latents directly condition the vertical stream's attention, KV-cache writes, MLP computation, and decoding. On Transformers trained on 20B FineWeb-Edu tokens, a 406M-parameter Lattice model lowers validation perplexity from 15.41 to 14.40 over a parameter-matched Transformer. These gains extend across architectural variants. The Lattice composes with prior residual-stream enhancements: with hyper-connections and input residuals, it lowers validation perplexity from 15.41 (base Transformer) and 14.22 (Transformer with the same enhancements) to 13.61. On Gated DeltaNet, Mamba-2, Triadic GDN, and GDN-Mamba-2 hybrids, it improves perplexity at every tested native state size, with Lattice-GDN and Lattice-Triadic-GDN variants outperforming pure baselines with 4x larger native states. These results identify persistent per-layer state as a complementary axis for scaling language-model computation.

ViRA: Toward Ultra-High-Resolution Visual Agents via Cross-Input Relative Policy Optimization

Huawei Lin, Jinting Liu, Tongyan Wang, Xuyang Chen, Ruihua Shi et al. (12 authors)

Visual agents answer questions about ultra-high-resolution photos by zooming into the relevant regions. However, existing agents often answer from common sense instead of reading the evidence. Reinforcement learning (RL) on the final answer cannot correct this, because a guess earns the same reward as reading. We propose Cross-Input Relative Policy Optimization (CrossRPO), which trains agents on contrast sets. A contrast set asks one question about several versions of an image that differ only in a small region with the evidence and have different answers, so only reading the evidence solves the whole set. CrossRPO rewards solving the whole set, and we prove that it does not reinforce a set answered from common sense. We build contrast sets for training (ViRA-Train) and evaluation (ViRA-Bench) by editing one small object in images of 21 to 120 megapixels, and we photograph further sets with real changes (ViRA-Bench-Real) to exclude editing traces. We train two Visual Reasoning Agents, ViRA-9B and ViRA-27B, with CrossRPO on only about 3,500 items of ViRA-Train each. CrossRPO achieves 63.3% set accuracy on ViRA-Bench at the maximum resolution, against 59.3% for GRPO. ViRA-9B is the most accurate open model of its size on all benchmarks, and ViRA-27B outperforms Claude Haiku 5.5 and GPT-6-Luna, both at their highest thinking level, on most benchmarks.

FaceJev: Direct Visual Decisions for Face Forgery Detection

Siran Peng, Tianshuo Zhang, Yibo Xue, Ajian Liu, Haoyuan Zhang et al. (9 authors)

Face forgery detection methods often perform well on seen manipulations but generalize poorly to unseen ones. Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) can recognize facial parts and assess their visual plausibility. Existing MLLM-based methods typically express these judgments through textual explanations, but seldom treat individual part decisions as explicit learning targets. We present FaceJev, an MLLM-based method for face forgery detection through direct visual decisions. Following the Jev formulation, FaceJev answers eight independent questions about facial parts using a shared image encoding and reads anomaly probabilities from the native answer logits. A face is classified as forged if any part is predicted to be abnormal, directly linking the detection result to the part decisions. We construct PartForensics, a dataset of approximately 160k images with binary anomaly targets for the eight facial parts, to supervise these decisions. We further propose FS-GRPO, which optimizes each part decision through an exact reward comparison with its alternative, using both detection and part rewards. FaceJev outperforms the strongest baseline by 7.25 percentage points on unseen manipulations. Extensive ablation studies validate our design choices and the effectiveness of the main components.

Quantifying the added value of human forecaster discussions for tropical cyclone intensity prediction

Ilia Azizi, Frederick Iat-Hin Tam, Marc-Olivier Boldi, Valérie Chavez-Demoulin, Tom Beucler

The National Hurricane Center routinely releases Tropical Cyclone Discussions that offer insights into its tropical cyclone forecasts. This study examines the added value of these discussions for intensity forecasting beyond a set of numerical predictors. For 637 Atlantic and eastern North Pacific tropical cyclones from the 2002-2023 hurricane seasons, we analyze the discussion text and its combination with 13 hurricane environmental predictors from SHIPS and ERA5 10-meter wind fields, for forecasting intensity changes at lead times of 24 to 120 hours using three modeling methods. In one approach, an end-to-end deep learning framework for the text shows that the discussions alone offer comparable value for 24-hour intensity forecasts to the SHIPS predictors. Adding discussion information to the SHIPS predictors in an ensemble of five such models reduces the mean absolute error of 24-hour maximum sustained wind forecasts for 2022-2023 cyclones from 9.89 to 8.90 kt, with a confidence interval that excludes zero when whole storms are resampled. The ERA5 wind fields have a minor effect on mean absolute error in wind predictions, and model rankings differ across predicted variables, lead times and model types. We provide prediction intervals for these forecasts, whose coverage falls below the 90% target during rapid intensification. Analysis of the text shows that words related to intensity and intensity change (such as intensify, weaken, eyewall and category) are important for the model predictions. This retrospective study shows the added value of the forecasters' discussions and suggests that multi-modal learning can help understand the predictive contribution of expert human judgment. The reanalysis data differ from real-time analyses, and the discussions are issued at least three hours after the valid time of the other inputs, so these results should not be interpreted operationally.

How VLMs Plan What's Next

Rohit Gandikota, Arnab Sen Sharma, Adam Belfki, David Bau

When a vision-language model describes an image, it names one object at a time and it rarely repeats itself, yet how the model plans this order is far from obvious. We characterize the mechanism a VLM uses to decide where to look before it looks. The mechanism has four main parts. First, binding heads match the relevant text tokens against the image and resolve them into pointers to the matched image region. Second, the same binding heads bind each written answer to the region it came from, and the model turns it into a progress signal: a cursor, the position in the image up to which the list has been written. Third, a select step chooses the nearest unwritten region after the cursor, or stops. Finally, gaze heads execute the chosen pointer and return a concept of what is drawn there, which language heads and the late MLPs turn into a word in the language of the question. We validate each part of this mechanism through causal patching in Qwen3-VL models on a synthetic visual retrieval task, and test our mechanisms against alternative hypotheses. We further show that the mechanism holds across input formats, natural photographs, and languages, and that its binding, relay, and gaze heads appear at every model size from 2B to 32B. This work allows us to investigate how a VLM turns a question and an image into a sequence of answers, layer by layer and token by token. More broadly, it opens a way to study how models plan over complex multi-step tasks with long rollouts. Our project is available at https://plan.baulab.info/

Hyperlyve: Parallelized Enumeration and Exact Lyapunov Stability Certification for Deep ReLU Networks

Brian Yarbrough, Jesse Wayment, Jingbo Wang, Philip E. Paré, Shreyas Sundaram

We introduce Hyperlyve (Hyperplane Partitioned Lyapunov Verification and Enumeration), a framework for proving the asymptotic stability of nonlinear dynamical systems, including those with neural network controllers. Hyperlyve learns and formally certifies continuous piecewise-affine (CPA) neural Lyapunov functions within a counterexample-guided inductive synthesis (CEGIS) loop. Hyperlyve's key innovation is a parallel algorithm that enumerates the hyperplane arrangement formed by the ReLU activations of deep networks and certifies that Lyapunov conditions hold within each polyhedral region. We derive sound and complete regional tests to verify the asymptotic stability of the exact smooth dynamics (rather than a piecewise-linear approximation) within a compact domain of interest. Furthermore, we formally characterize the computational complexity of the CPA neural Lyapunov verification problem as co-NP-complete, filling a key gap in the literature. Empirical results demonstrate that Hyperlyve enumerates ReLU networks over 150-times faster than competing tools, which contributes to Hyperlyve proving the asymptotic stability of systems that are currently intractable for state-of-the-art verifiers.

Mitigating Catastrophic Forgetting with Physics-Indexed Specialist Memory: the Evolutionary Neuroplastic Agent

Blas Eugenio Vicco Zarate, Miguel Hernán Vicco

Continual reinforcement learning requires adaptation to changing conditions while preserving performance under previously encountered dynamics. It was investigated whether specialists generated by evolutionary search can provide a reusable memory when combined with environment detection and failure-driven search. The Evolutionary Neuroplastic Agent (ENA) evolves small neural networks without gradients, archives selected policies by detected dynamic regime, and retrieves them through Bayesian policy reuse. The architecture was evaluated in non-stationary CartPole and HalfCheetah environments. In the 30-replicate CartPole ablation, the complete architecture achieved a mean success rate of 83.2% across the five test environments. Among variants that learned the harder CartPole environments, the complete architecture showed the most consistent retention. A selection ablation also showed that the retrieval rule affected performance. In the evaluated sequences, ENA adapted more slowly than PPO on CartPole and achieved lower returns than SAC on HalfCheetah. The evidence concerns the complete architecture and does not isolate gradient-free optimization as the cause of retention or establish superiority over other continual-learning methods.

Optimal Autoregressive Chain-of-Thought Learning via Bagging

Awni Altabaa, John Lafferty, Omar Montasser

We study how to learn an autoregressive next-token generator $f\colonΣ^\star\toΣ$ from a class $\mathcal{F}$, given chain-of-thought (CoT) demonstrations of length $T$ generated by $f$. We present a simple algorithm based on bagging, which outputs a majority vote of generators that are trained to be CoT-consistent on bootstrap samples of the training data. For binary token sets, it achieves the optimal sample complexity of $O((d_{VC}+\log(1/δ))/ε)$, independent of the CoT length $T$, where $d_{VC}$ is the VC dimension of $\mathcal{F}$. For finite token sets $Σ$, the VC dimension is replaced by a multiclass analogue, the Graph dimension $d_{G}$, and the sample complexity is optimal up to a $\log|Σ|$ factor. The number of generators in the majority-vote ensemble is $O(\log(T/δ))$, and we show that the ensemble can be distilled into a single generator in $\mathcal{F}$ at the expense of $n\log(nT)$ unlabeled prompts.

SADL: On the Demand-Supply Geometry of Representation Collapse

Junhan Sun, Yigui Feng, Mingzhe Liu, Haoyan Xu, Lei Ye et al. (10 authors)

In Joint-Embedding Predictive Architectures (JEPAs), a well-constrained projector output does not by itself prevent representation collapse. We find that, from initialization, the encoder representation is dominated by a component shared by different inputs. A batch-normalized projector removes this component, so regularization after the projector cannot correct it, and the differences between representations shrink until they collapse. We propose SADL (Supply And Demand Loss), which keeps the original training objective and constrains the encoder directly when needed. SADL compares the representation differences that the projector output demands with the differences the encoder can retain and pass on to the projector, and adds a constraint on the encoder only when supply falls short of demand. It works with different Demand Regularizers and introduces no auxiliary networks, additional trainable parameters, or architectural changes. In single-task and jointly trained four-task LeWorldModel (LeWM) and INTACT experiments, SADL lowers the collapse rate over ten random seeds from 50% to 0%. After one epoch of training, encoder effective rank is 3.4-6.4 times that of the corresponding baseline without SADL, and latent prediction error falls to 1/20-1/303 of it. Without additional tuning, SADL stays robust as its loss weight spans orders of magnitude, and with several Demand Regularizers INTACT exceeds 95% mean search-free control success. On four image datasets, SADL also consistently improves accuracy and stability across random seeds. These results show that matching the representation differences a regularizer demands with the differences the encoder can supply leads to faster and more stable representation learning in control and image classification.

Surrogate-Assisted Compact-Model Extraction for SKY130 CMOS at 77 K

Aaron Wang, Olivia Gzamouranis, Vaibhav Lohia, Hershel Fei Xun Weiner, Olivia Seidel et al. (9 authors)

Cryogenic circuit design requires compact transistor models that reproduce measured low-temperature behavior and run in standard circuit simulators. We fit BSIM4 parameters to measured I-V curves of SkyWater 130 nm (SKY130) transistors at 77 K using a neural surrogate of NGSpice. The surrogate maps compact-model parameters and device geometry to the complete set of output and transfer characteristics. Extraction is a bounded least-squares search: a bank of NGSpice simulations supplies initial candidates, finite differences of the surrogate supply the local Jacobian, and NGSpice re-simulates the resulting cards before selection. On 18 measured geometries, we compare a search over seven card parameters with a search over 43 parameters selected by NGSpice sensitivity. Relative to NGSpice re-simulations of the previously released cards, the two searches reduce the in-sample mean relative root-mean-square error (RRMS) by 17% and 26%, respectively. The 43-parameter search has the lower overall error, but the seven-parameter search has the lower mean on high-bias curves, and some individual curves have higher error than with the released cards under both searches.
