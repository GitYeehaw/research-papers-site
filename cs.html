Pairing AIS with RF: Analysis of a Markovian Approach for Maritime Data Fusion

Daniel Grimm, Kyra Jongman, Renato Cordeiro Ferreira, Giuseppe Cascavilla, Alfredo Cuzzocrea et al. (6 authors)

Monitoring maritime activities is important for safety and security, particularly for detecting illegal activities, but remains challenging due to the size and complexity of the maritime domain. The Automatic Identification System (AIS) is commonly used for this purpose, despite its known vulnerabilities. In contrast, space-based radio frequency (RF) observations provide an independent source of vessel activity but lack ship identification and therefore cannot identify vessels on their own. Following the CRISP-DM process, this study applies a Pair Hidden Markov Model (PHMM) to align individual RF detections with AIS trajectories, using temporal and spatial information while modeling noise and timing discrepancies. We evaluate it on NOAA AIS data with synthetic RF detections against nearest-neighbor baselines and analyze its errors with bias tests on trajectory length, sampling density, stationarity and AIS--RF distance. The length-corrected Forward score reaches a top-1 precision of $0.7776$ on the validation split of multi-candidate detections, where errors are possible, and an AUC of $0.637$ for detecting dark vessels among the detections that pass the prefilter. With a single RF detection, the score mostly reflects the AIS trajectory itself, and its errors favor longer and more stationary trajectories. A log-odds score, which compares the full model with a null model without match transitions, raises precision to $0.8580$ and AUC to $0.852$. Its length-corrected variant no longer shows a significant bias towards stationary trajectories and short inter-message distances, but still favors candidates farther from the RF detection. A time-weighted nearest-neighbor baseline without fitting remains ahead (precision $0.9013$, AUC $0.904$), so this single-detection, hand-designed PHMM formulation approaches but does not beat it.

FETTUCCINE: Fast and efficient brain-to-text decoding on mobile devices

Jonathan D. McCart, Pranav I. Deevi, Mehdi Azabou, Nanda H. Krishna, Caleb A. McKinney et al. (8 authors)

Recent advances in speech neuroprostheses have enabled new communication avenues for people who have lost the ability to speak due to neurological illness or injury. These systems can decode neural activity during attempted speech into text by relying on language priors to achieve high decoding accuracy. This performance currently comes at the cost of using a compute-intensive language modeling stack, presenting a major bottleneck for real-world use. Further, current language models incur highly variable and disruptive inference latencies, limiting communication throughput and preventing users from reliably participating in natural conversation. We introduce FETTUCCINE, an end-to-end brain-to-text framework that leverages the speed and portability of automatic speech recognition (ASR) models to address the aforementioned practical challenges, while achieving usable (<10%) word error rates (WER), efficient adaptation to neural non-stationarities, and reliable performance on mobile devices. When evaluated using publicly available data from an intracortical speech neuroprosthesis user, FETTUCCINE outperforms previous end-to-end methods, achieving as low as 4.39% WER. Most notably, our models successfully run on a commercially-available mobile device with throughput reaching up to 370x real-time, providing the first demonstration of brain-to-text decoding on a mobile device. We also show that our models can be successfully finetuned to extend usable performance to future days. Beyond performance, our approach enables localization of the neural features most relevant for decoding via gradient-based saliency maps, which we show align with well-established physiological priors across different ASR model families. Taken together, our results show that FETTUCCINE overcomes core infrastructural and computational barriers, yielding a new class of portable brain-to-text communication.

Training Reasoning Models in Simulation to Natively Use Real Robots

Jiaming Tang, Zhuoyang Zhang, Junxian Guo, Fangzhou Zhao, Yilong Zhao et al. (11 authors)

Robot-use agents (RUAs) have emerged as a promising approach to general-purpose robot control. However, strong performance has so far been achieved only by frontier proprietary models such as GPT-6 Astra and Claude Fable 5.2, whereas open-weight models remain substantially less capable. We show that an open-weight 27B reasoning model can be trained to use real robots natively through reinforcement learning, entirely in simulation and without demonstrations. Like a computer-use agent (CUA), the model observes the environment, reasons in a chain of thought, and generates the next end-effector pose, without any harness. We build a Post-training Engine for Agentic Robotics (PEAR) and use it to train the model on simulated robot tasks at scale with robot-use reinforcement learning (RURL), mirroring how frontier models acquire computer use. We find that the skills acquired in simulation generalize well beyond the training tasks: they transfer to an unseen robot, to the real world, and to novel instructions. After training on LIBERO and RoboDojo in simulation, the model succeeds in 60% of our trials on a real dual-arm robot, whereas the base model succeeds in none. On LIBERO, training raises the success rate from 1.6% to 74.4%, approaching GPT-6 Astra and GPT-6.1 Sol at maximum reasoning effort. Compared with $π_{0.5}$, a vision-language-action model fine-tuned on demonstrations, our model is substantially more robust to changes in the task itself. On LIBERO-Pro, $π_{0.5}$ succeeds in only 0.75% of episodes when assigned a new task in a familiar scene whereas our model succeeds in 57.4%. Finally, the model retains its general capabilities, with no measurable degradation on nine reasoning, coding and vision benchmarks. These results highlight robot-use reinforcement learning as a promising component of foundation-model post-training.

BAL: Bidirectional Autoregression in Latent Space for Learning Human Movement Representations

Genki Kinoshita, Ko Nishino

Learning semantically meaningful human movement representations from 3D pose sequences is essential for human behavior modeling. Existing self-supervised methods derive supervision from artificial assumptions, e.g., invariance to augmentation within a certain range or recoverability of masked regions at a certain temporal scale, introducing heuristic hyperparameters that make the learned semantics sensitive to their settings. We propose BAL, a self-supervised framework that instead exploits supervision intrinsic to human movement: the natural ordering structure of everyday behavior. BAL autoregressively predicts in a semantically abstract latent space, analogous to how humans anticipate others' actions rather than their precise joint configurations. Bidirectional autoregressive prediction, both forward and backward in time, fully leverages these ordering constraints while enabling representation extraction using both past and future context at inference. To suppress representation collapse, BAL decomposes each prediction target into a global component capturing sequence-level semantics and a local component capturing context-independent movement. We prove that the local targets retain nontrivial angular diversity unless the representations are completely collapsed, preserving a meaningful training signal. Experiments on large-scale datasets demonstrate that BAL's representations are highly effective for motion captioning, forecasting, and interpolation, outperforming baselines on most evaluation metrics.

WeightBench: Evaluating Language Models as Weight-Space Programmers

Aayush Marasini, Barsat Khadka, Nick Rahimi

Recent work shows that large language models (LLMs) can operate directly on the parameters of other neural networks. They can read what a network was trained to do, fix a network that fails, and write working networks from scratch, all without gradient descent. This capability has been termed Weight-Space Programming (WSP). Exploring this new paradigm systematically requires a robust benchmark. We introduce WEIGHTBENCH, a benchmark that evaluates LLMs of increasing capability on 42 WSP tasks across five tracks: reading, fixing and writing weights, recognizing edits that are impossible, and data-free compression. The networks whose weights the LLMs read and edit range from 2K-parameter control policies to 7B-parameter language models. Experimental benchmark results show that frontier agents can already read small networks and write working control policies without ever running them, but cannot yet patch language models, compress them without data, or tell an impossible edit from a hard one. On all 42 tasks, Claude Opus 5.5 reaches a macro score of 0.535 (Sonnet 5.5 0.466, Haiku 5.5 0.402, DeepSeek v4-flash 0.215); on the 38 tasks it was run on, GPT-6.1-sol reaches 0.568 against Opus's 0.546. Code, the task registry, our OS-level sandbox harness, and the live leaderboard are publicly available at https://github.com/Aayush-Marasini/WeightBench.

Restoring Diversity in Distilled Image and Video Generators by Probing Activations

Furkan Horoz, Ozgur Kara, Kaan Oktay, Adil Kaan Akan, Atakan Kara et al. (7 authors)

Distilling diffusion models into few-step student models substantially reduces sampling latency, but induces severe diversity collapse: samples generated from different random seeds show minimal variation. In this work, we investigate the root cause of this failure by probing the internal representations of student models relative to their teachers. We find that distilled students maintain total activation variance comparable to their teachers, but concentrate it into substantially fewer directions. This directional collapse is localized to an identifiable intermediate block at the initial denoising step. Consequently, diversity collapse stems from a misallocation of activation variance rather than an inherent loss of generative capacity. Guided by this finding, we propose ReDiverse to reinstate the missing variance directions. At training time, ReDiverse-FT fine-tunes a lightweight adapter that regresses student activations onto the teacher's, reviving the teacher's feature variance characteristic. At inference time, ReDiverse-TF introduces a training-free mechanism that steers activations along precomputed teacher principal directions. Across image and video distillation benchmarks, ReDiverse consistently restores sample diversity while preserving generation quality, further supported by the user study.

JVT: Just Video Transformer makes pixel-space video diffusion simple

Jianjun Zhou, Hua Geng, Haoyu Guo

Direct diffusion modeling of high-dimensional raw video data is challenging, and existing pixel-space methods often rely on specialized architectures. We introduce JVT (Just Video Transformer), a simple large-patch DiT-style Transformer for pixel-space video diffusion, without a pretrained autoencoder, auxiliary detailers, cascaded stages, or U-Net-style skip connections. We systematically examine video patch embeddings, register tokens, and adaptive layer normalization to identify design choices that balance generation quality and computational efficiency. The resulting JVT design achieves generation quality competitive with latent diffusion baselines in both class-conditioned and unconditional settings. The pixel-space formulation of JVT also naturally supports joint generation of pixel-aligned modalities. We demonstrate this capability through camera-conditioned RGB-disparity generation, which improves camera following relative to RGB-only baselines and yields explicit scene geometry without an additional reconstruction model at inference. These results highlight the potential of simple DiT-style Transformers for pixel-space video diffusion, and we hope our findings offer useful insights and practical guidance for future research.

Imitation Bootstrapped Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning

Tony H. Zhang, Connor Mattson, Daniel S. Brown

Demonstrations are an effective way to accelerate single-agent reinforcement learning (RL), but extending this idea to multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) is non-trivial as it is difficult and often impossible for a single person to teleoperate multiple agents simultaneously. Round-Robin Behavior Cloning (R2BC) addresses this by letting a single demonstrator control one agent at a time while the others execute their current policies. However, the resulting imitation policies are limited by the quality of the demonstrations. We propose Imitation Bootstrapped Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (IBMARL), a framework that extends Imitation Bootstrapped Reinforcement Learning (IBRL) to cooperative teams and uses R2BC to train the imitation policies. At each time step, every agent receives one action proposal from its imitation policy and one from its RL policy, and an ensemble of centralized critics selects among the per-agent combinations of the two. A gated imitation penalty, a soft Q-filtered behavior cloning loss, regularizes each RL policy toward its imitation policy only where the critics value the imitation action more, so that after training each agent executes its RL policy alone, without the centralized critics. On four cooperative multi-agent RL benchmarks, IBMARL surpasses its imitation policies $2.6$-$4.2\times$ faster than demonstration-based baselines on two tasks, is the only method to surpass it on the other two, and exceeds the demonstrator itself on two of the four. With real human demonstrations IBMARL still surpasses imitation learning alone, and does so more than twice as fast as the best baseline.

Elliptic Sampling: Optimal Interpolant Schedules for Gaussians and Beyond

Cole Becker, Taylan Kargin, Kimia Hassibi, Asuman Ozdaglar, Pablo A. Parrilo

Generative modeling based on stochastic interpolants requires specifying two key choices: an interpolant schedule bridging the initial and target distributions, and an efficient discretization of the resulting probability-flow ODE. In this work, we introduce a new framework for joint schedule/discretization design based on the analyticity properties of the (complexified) ODE trajectories, which in turn ensure good numerical behavior. Our formulation can be solved exactly via conformal mapping, yielding a natural family of variance-preserving elliptic schedules, parametrized by Jacobi elliptic functions. Pairing an elliptic schedule with a tailored discretization, we obtain Zoloflow, a new sampling algorithm closely connected to Zolotarev's optimal rational function approximants. Crucially, both the elliptic schedule and Zoloflow explicitly incorporate the condition number $κ$ of the target distribution and reduce to the classical trig schedule in the limit $κ\rightarrow 1$. For Gaussian targets, Zoloflow achieves minimax optimality among all oblivious score-span algorithms, attaining total-variation error $\varepsilon$ using $Θ(\logκ\,\log(\sqrt d/\varepsilon))$ (exact) score queries. For the log-concave case, the elliptic schedule allows sampling with at most $\varepsilon$ 2-Wasserstein-error by requiring merely $\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{d} \, polylog(κ, 1/\varepsilon))$ score evaluations. Complementing these theoretical results, we empirically evaluate the elliptic schedule on general (non-log-concave) target distributions, including detailed comparisons with alternative schedule proposals on both synthetic benchmarks and existing image generation datasets.

Why Finite Intelligence Works in an Infinite World: A Mathematical Theory

Mojtaba Soltanalian

An information system often has to learn before it knows what it will be asked. A finite representation can succeed without recording an infinite environment in full when those asks share collective structure, rather than demand unrelated facts scattered arbitrarily across it. We formalize this phenomenon through future-task information, the minimum retained information needed to preserve decision value relative to the available evidence. A joint decision-regret state records posterior comparisons among actions for every permitted task. Under compactness assumptions, every retained representation admits a calibrated replacement based on this state, preserving all task regrets without increasing information; the resulting information-regret curve equals an operational coding rate. Two exact boundaries govern finite preservation: weak-star closure for a fixed action count, and the singular mass of the source-evidence law when all finite action menus are admitted. Collective structure determines information requirements beyond individual-question difficulty. Families with identical source laws, pairwise task metrics, and individual-task rates can have different high-resolution exponents. Covariance-identical coordinate and parity families have different exact rate-distortion curves, with optimal parity retention characterized by a sharp Fourier-entropy inequality. For balanced nested task families, refinement sizes and weighted reach determine the rate within an additive constant, attained by prefix coding under explicit decay conditions. Nonlinear learning and computational separations distinguish preservation from efficient discovery and use. The proposed mathematical theory identifies which shared decision requirements allow finite information to remain useful for infinitely many possible purposes.
