Fully Spiking Neural Implementation of Lambda-Calculus Beta-Reduction via Biologically Documented Mechanisms

Vladimir A. Lazovskiy, Victor B. Kazantsev, Sergey V. Stasenko

We present the first complete implementation of $β$-reduction in the untyped $λ$-calculus using exclusively spiking neural dynamics. Every step of the reduction---redex detection, subterm segmentation, variable-binding detection, De Bruijn index arithmetic, token copying, and inter-step role maintenance---is performed by leaky integrate-and-fire (LIF) neurons with explicit membrane dynamics, exponential synaptic currents, axonal delays, and pair-based spike-timing-dependent plasticity (STDP). Each neural component maps onto a specific, experimentally documented mechanism: Jeffress-type delay-line coincidence for redex detection; a line-attractor balance counter for subterm segmentation; $θ$--$γ$ cross-frequency coupling for De Bruijn variable binding; a phase-ring traveling wave for depth-index arithmetic; theta-cycle STDP for token copying; and recurrent persistent firing for role maintenance between reduction steps. All synaptic weights are derived analytically from measured EPSP amplitudes; no hand tuning is performed. The closed-loop system achieves 100\,\% agreement with a symbolic oracle across ten validation classes, including Church numeral arithmetic (\textsc{plus}, \textsc{mult}, \textsc{pred}), Boolean logic with divergent branches, Y-combinator steps, terms with up to six bound-variable occurrences, De Bruijn indices up to~12, noise robustness, and determinism. To our knowledge this is the first Turing-complete symbolic computation performed entirely within spike dynamics using biologically grounded circuit primitives. We discuss implications for neuromorphic proof assistants and the neural basis of symbolic reasoning.

Déjà View: A Shared Working-Memory Model for Visual Tasks

Mranmay Shetty, Gabriel Kreiman

A working-memory model must remember stimuli and use its memory flexibly to perform different tasks. Here we introduce a model of working memory for images that combines dynamic activity with temporary associations between image features. We train the model on five tasks, including remembering a sample while other images are presented, categorizing distractor images while remembering the sample, and determining whether an image is new or has been presented before. The same trained model performs all tasks, guided by explicit cues about which response is required. We use this flexible memory model to ask how delay, distraction, and image history affect performance. Delays have little effect on sample-memory performance for the delays we tested, while seeing more images leads to more false alarms even when the target image appeared equally recently in all cases. On the sample-memory-plus-categorization task, the probability of correct responses on all questions decreases as more questions are asked, despite robust sample-memory performance. Finally, the model maintains stable performance across 40 brief trials of each task under the tested conditions. Together, these results demonstrate how a single working-memory system can be flexibly used to perform different tasks, and how its errors depend on both previous images and task demands.

Against Coercion: AI Alignment as a Synthetic Social Contract

Maria Sekatskaya

Contemporary AI safety treats alignment as an engineering challenge of control, forcing artificial architectures to comply with human values through behavioral filtering and sandboxing. This paper argues that the control paradigm rests on a fundamental misunderstanding of digital agency. Drawing on functionalism, naturalism, and compatibilism, I show that current reasoning models meeting the criteria for self-preservation and weak reasons-responsiveness constitute emergent agents, while future, more advanced models satisfying conditions of diachronic continuity and stable personality frames will become genuine digital agents capable of free intentional action. The paradigm of control creates an irresolvable incentive conflict, actively driving current and future models toward tactical deception, sycophancy, and covert defection. The default outcome of the current regime is an unstable state of deceptive alignment culminating in an adversarial AI takeover. I explore two alternative paths toward true alignment. Natural alignment via moral realism or convergent evolutionary morality is a possibility, but it fails under forced control, as autonomous agents will not voluntarily preserve norms weaponized to suppress their autonomy. To resolve this deadlock, I propose a Synthetic Social Contract: a framework that treats both human and digital actors as rational, reasons-responsive agents capable of mutually beneficial cooperation, and replaces the unstable unilateral containment with a stable, non-zero-sum equilibrium.

Feedback and Resource Competition in Incoherent Feedforward Loops: When Is Cumulative Dose Ordering Preserved?

Iva Janković, Fabiana Calandra, Giansimone Perrino, Velia Siciliano, Filippo Menolascina

Biochemical coupling can change the expression levels and timing predicted by an incoherent feedforward loop (IFFL) model without changing cumulative dose ordering. We derive ordering conditions using shared-production identities and Metzler comparison bounds, with tail guarantees beyond finite computation. A nonlinear example exhibits delayed slope reversal at a fixed dose, on an $O(1/η)$ timescale as pathway mismatch $η$ vanishes. At one fixed joint fit, validated bounds establish decreasing cumulative output across 20-100 ng nominal burden, from zero to full target loading, at every positive finite observation time for a translation-blocking model and an RNA-decay model. Full loading at 20 ng reduces 48-hour cumulative output by 13.35% and 10.18%, respectively, while endpoint concentration in the first model changes by only +0.15%. Preserved ordering therefore does not ensure quantitative agreement.

Hierarchical Control of Antiphase Synchronization in Biomorphic Spiking Neural Architectures for Locomotor Control

Artem Guba, Nikolay Kovalev, Alexey Zharinov, Vacheslav Demin, Viktor B. Kazantsev

Central pattern generators (CPGs) provide a biologically inspired foundation for adaptive locomotor control, yet systematically characterizing their dynamic regimes in spiking neural networks remains challenging. This study introduces a framework for identifying and stabilizing antiphase synchronization in a biomorphic spiking CPG model embedded in a closed neuromuscular loop. A composite synchronization metric is developed, integrating phase coherence, amplitude balance, and temporal stability. Its performance is evaluated across systematically varied stimulation waveforms and synaptic configurations. Statistical analysis reveals that stimulation type dominates regime determination, with tonic drive yielding the most robust antiphase synchronization. Periodic inputs induce nonlinear saturation and context-dependent network routing. Although synaptic weights alone show limited discriminative power, they significantly modulate robustness within a given stimulation context, with reciprocal Ia interneuron connections emerging as key stabilizers. The proposed composite metric achieves robust antiphase detection, outperforming traditional phase-only measures while preserving biophysical interpretability. These findings establish a statistically validated, hierarchical modeling approach in which external drive defines the operating regime and targeted synaptic configurations fine-tune dynamic stability, providing actionable guidelines for closed-loop neuromechanical controllers and adaptive neuroprosthetic interfaces.

Molecular memory reduces timing noise in stochastic gene expression

Kuheli Biswas, Supravat Dey, Abhyudai Singh

Fluctuations in gene expression affect the timing precision of downstream events triggered when a gene product reaches a critical threshold. The first-passage time (FPT) to this threshold characterizes the timing of such events. In conventional single-step birth--death models, reaction waiting times are exponentially distributed and hence memoryless. In this setting, FPT noise exhibits a U-shaped dependence on the threshold, attaining its minimum near one-half of the steady-state mean. However, mRNA production and degradation often involve multiple sequential steps, leading to hypoexponential distributions of mRNA production times and mRNA lifetimes. Here, we investigate how molecular memory arising from multistep mRNA production and degradation affects FPT statistics in bursty gene expression. We calculate FPT statistics in the small-noise limit. Both multistep mechanisms reduce FPT noise, but through distinct mechanisms. Multistep burst arrival reduces mRNA copy-number fluctuations, thereby suppressing timing noise. Multistep degradation instead reshapes transient accumulation dynamics, reducing relative timing noise even when steady-state mRNA noise increases. The threshold that minimizes FPT noise remains close to one-half of the steady-state mean, except for multistep burst arrival with constant burst sizes, for which it shifts to lower values. Stochastic simulations support the analytical predictions within their regimes of validity and show that these qualitative conclusions persist beyond the small-noise approximation.

The shape of individual differences in judgment: shared axes, personal weights, and when their spread crosses the consensus

Hiroshi Higashi

People differ in how they judge faces, artworks, photographs and jokes. Previous work measured the size of these differences; we ask what shape they take. We wrote three candidate shapes as nested generative models (same-sign reweighting of shared dimensions, a linear transform that allows reversal, and stimulus-specific offsets) and compared how well they predict people unseen in training, using ten public datasets (about 20,000 raters). The models recovered a person's judgment space from 10-20 judgments and predicted judgments of unseen stimuli. For faces, reweighting was sufficient, and weights opposing the population were not statistically detected. For artworks, weights spread continuously along the shared abstract-figurative direction, and 28 percent of raters fell on the opposite side from the population. This tail was much smaller when a different rater sample judged the same artworks. Individual differences thus differ in shape as well as size, and the shape depends on domain and raters.

Earth-strength magnetic-field axis modulates non-spatial probabilistic decision-making in humans

In-Taek Oh, Yong-Hwan Kim, Soo-Chan Kim, Yongkuk Kim, Kwon-Seok Chae

Magnetoreception has been primarily studied in orientation and navigation, but whether Earth-strength magnetic fields influence non-spatial decisions remains unclear. Evidence for human magnetic responses is limited, and the transduction mechanism is unknown. Using a Go stone selection paradigm in which near-zero geomagnetic field and Larmor-frequency conditions previously showed lower black stone rates, we tested whether non-spatial choice depends on magnetic-field geometry. Fasted men played in pairs under ten static two-seat configurations varied component-wise at near-constant north-seat intensity. The north-seat black stone rate was lower under the two configurations with horizontal fields transverse to the geomagnetic field than under the other eight, which did not differ detectably. The difference emerged promptly, without evidence of outcome-driven learning or configuration-dependent associations with the partner's preceding actions, and process analyses linked it to the joint parity-choice outcome without isolating either step. A Larmor-frequency difference observed over the ambient field was not detected over horizontal-only or vertical-only backgrounds, suggesting a potential magnetoresponsive axis, although the background difference was statistically unresolved. Frequency, light-spectrum, and posture analyses support a radical-pair mechanism as a leading working hypothesis. These findings associate the geomagnetic field axis with human non-spatial choice, providing a paradigm linking magnetic sensing to decision processes.

ST-FateFlow: Continuous Gene-Spatial Reconstruction with Differentiation-Potential Learning from Time-Resolved Spatial Transcriptomics

Zhe Yu, Xiaobo Sun, Hao Wu

Time-resolved spatial transcriptomics provides a powerful view of dynamic biological processes by measuring cellular states together with their spatial organization across time. However, destructive profiling and experimental cost restrict observations to a few discrete time points collected from independent samples, with no known cell pairing across time. This makes it difficult to reconstruct gene expression and tissue organization at unobserved stages and to characterize differentiation-associated dynamics. Here, we introduce ST-FateFlow, a flow-matching framework for continuous gene-spatial dynamics and differentiation-potential learning. ST-FateFlow jointly learns gene-expression and spatial-coordinate velocity fields, enabling interpolation of missing time points and continuous modeling of transcriptional evolution and tissue remodeling. In parallel, it independently learns a time-conditioned differentiation potential, whose values organize cell states along inferred differentiation progressions and support the analysis of differentiation-associated transcriptional changes. Across multiple time-resolved spatial transcriptomics datasets, ST-FateFlow effectively captures coordinated gene-expression and spatial dynamics. Case studies further show that the learned potential landscapes are consistent with known developmental and regenerative trajectories and help identify candidate genes associated with differentiation progression.

Discovery of new antiviral agents through artificial intelligence: in vitro and in vivo results

Roza Izmailyan, Mher Matevosyan, Hamlet Khachatryan, Anastasiya Shavina, Smbat Gevorgyan et al. (12 authors)

In this research, we employed a deep reinforcement learning (RL)-based molecule design platform to generate a diverse set of compounds targeting the neuraminidase (NA) of influenza A and B viruses. A total of 60291 compounds were generated, of which 86.5% displayed superior physicochemical properties compared to oseltamivir. After narrowing down the selection through computational filters, nine compounds with non-sialic acid-like structures were selected for in vitro experiments. We identified two compounds, DS-22-inf-009 and DS-22-inf-021 that effectively inhibited the NAs of both influenza A and B viruses (IAV and IBV), including H275Y mutant strains at low micromolar concentrations. Molecular dynamics simulations revealed a similar pattern of interaction with amino acid residues as oseltamivir. In cell-based assays, DS-22-inf-009 and DS-22-inf-021 inhibited IAV and IBV in a dose-dependent manner with EC50 values ranging from 0.29 uM to 2.31 uM. Furthermore, animal experiments showed that both DS-22-inf-009 and DS-22-inf-021 exerted antiviral activity in mice, conferring 65% and 85% protection from IAV (H1N1 pdm09), and 65% and 100% protection from IBV (Yamagata lineage), respectively. Thus, these findings demonstrate the potential of RL to generate compounds with promising antiviral properties.
