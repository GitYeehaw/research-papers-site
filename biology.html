Structure-Preserving Leaky Integrate-and-Fire Dynamics on Arbitrary Time Scales

Billur Kaymakçalan, Behiye İlayda Selçuk

The leaky integrate-and-fire (LIF) neuron combines a passive membrane law with threshold-triggered firing and reset. We formulate this hybrid neuron model, under constant input, on an arbitrary Hilger time scale $\mathbb{T}$. Our central result is that, after the first spike, the firing times satisfy $t_{n+1}=Π_{\mathbb{T}}^{+}(t_n+T_c)$, where $T_c$ is the classical reset-to-threshold travel time. This recursion requires no periodicity, homogeneity, or translation invariance of $\mathbb{T}$; it holds for every time scale that is unbounded above. To preserve the passive membrane physics, we use the inverse cylinder transformation to construct a positively regressive coefficient $p_m$. The resulting delta-dynamic equation is exactly the restriction to $\mathbb{T}$ of the classical continuous-time LIF trajectory. By contrast, the direct transcription $V^Δ=-(V-V_\infty)/τ_m$ exhibits graininess-dependent sign reversal and amplification at coarse right-scattered points. The first firing time is the forward projection of the classical continuous threshold-crossing time onto $\mathbb{T}$, and, when the graininess is bounded by $μ^\ast$, the per-event forward-projection delay -- from each reset to the next realized firing time, relative to the continuous crossing time computed from that same reset -- is bounded above by $μ^\ast$. On $\mathbb{T}=h\mathbb{Z}$ we obtain the explicit lattice-quantized law $T_{ISI}^{(h)}=h\lceil T_c/h\rceil$, which converges to the classical continuous-time inter-spike interval as $h\to0^+$. The framework therefore separates preserved membrane physics from the temporal opportunity structure imposed by the time domain. To our knowledge, this is the first systematic Hilger-calculus treatment of a threshold-and-reset LIF spiking neuron on an arbitrary time scale.

Using an energy pixels approach to estimate the size a 2nd Generation DEL must be to train a high-resolution binding model

Boris P. Belotserkovskii, R. Edward Watts

DNA-encoded libraries (DELs) are commonly used to find small-molecule binders to a target protein. But the hard part is no longer finding a hit. It is building a machine-learning model good enough to guide the medicinal chemistry that follows, one that can resolve the shift in affinity when a chemist changes a single atom. Such a model needs two things from its training data: dense sampling of the small patch of chemical space around the hit, and an adequate number of genuinely tight binders within it. A diverse naive DEL gives neither. It samples an enormous space, so any one neighborhood is covered far too thinly, and the tightest binders are rare. A model trained on that naive DEL data must extrapolate to binders tighter than any it has seen, and that is where models can fail. One way to get training data with both properties is a second-generation DEL built around the hit. We ask how complex that library must be. We treat binding as a sum of small, independent interactions, called pixels on the target and antipixels on the ligand. From the spread of affinities measured in the naive library, with no structural information and nothing yet synthesized, we estimate the complexity such a library must have to supply that dense sampling and those tight binders. The method does two useful things. It turns the number of tight binders a model needs into the number of compounds a library must contain. And it estimates the expected yield of a randomly designed second-generation DEL, so the gain from designing the library with machine learning or active learning can be measured against a baseline, without the expense of a control library as large as the experiment itself.

AI for Biologists: The Knowledge Boundaries of Foundation Models in the Age of Agentic Systems

Tian Cai, Andrew Kleist, Jaimin Patel, Duccio Malinverni, Alexander K. Taylor et al. (8 authors)

Artificial intelligence (AI) is changing how biological research is done. Biological foundation models trained on millions of nucleotides, protein, and single-cell measurements predict protein structures, mutation effects, and cellular responses to genetic perturbations. Agentic systems, AI assistants built on large language models, can run multistep analyses from plain-language requests and increasingly call foundation models as tools. Yet it is often unclear when these models' outputs can be trusted. We frame this as a model's knowledge boundary: the range of inputs, tasks, and conditions over which its outputs are reliable. This review explains how foundation models are built and proposes that five design elements (data, architecture, training scheme, evaluation scheme, and post-analysis) define this boundary. We examine evidence on where current models succeed or fail and present standardized descriptions of model limits so that researchers, and the agents acting for them, can use these tools reliably.

Random Exploration is Random

Yingkang Luo, Hua-Dong Xiong, Hanbo Xie, Caroline Phelps, Madeline Harms et al. (8 authors)

Decision making often requires balancing exploitation of familiar options with exploration of uncertain alternatives. One strategy for exploration is random exploration, in which choices become more variable and less tied to the option that currently appears best. However, it remains unclear whether this variability reflects genuine stochasticity or instead a deterministic structure that traditional cognitive models fail to capture. We examined this question using the Horizon Task in an aggregated dataset of 1,392 participants completing over 1,270,695 trials. In this task, exploration becomes more valuable when participants have more future choices, and choice variability increases under longer horizons. We tested whether more expressive neural network models could recover deterministic structure from this additional variability. Although neural networks provided better overall fits to behavior than the traditional model, long-horizon choices remained consistently less predictable than short-horizon choices. These findings show that limited model expressiveness cannot fully account for the increased variability associated with random exploration, suggesting random exploration truly is substantially random.

Dynamical mean-field theory of latching Potts networks

Anindita Basu, Carlo Orientale Caputo, Fernando Lucas Metz, Alessandro Treves

Spontaneous transitions between memories, as observed in internally generated cognition such as mind-wandering and free association, require neural dynamics capable of destabilizing one attractive memory state and moving towards another. We ask what minimal representational structure is required for such transitions to emerge in random associative networks. To address this question, we study Potts associative networks with $S$ active states per unit and slow adaptive thresholds. The $S=1$ case can model a standard Hopfield-like network of binary units. We employ dynamical mean-field theory in the extensive-load regime, where the number of stored patterns scales linearly with network size and connectivity, and we focus on transitions between two condensed memories, which are singled out in the connectivity matrix. In the finite-load regime, we show that transitions are governed by an effective drive term, whose sign and temporal evolution distinguish high-latching (structured), low-latching (random), and non-latching regimes. The resulting phase diagram shows that adaptation alone is not sufficient: transitions only occur within certain ranges of the overlap between the two memories. In the binary case $S=1$, in particular, no unit can be active in both memories but in different Potts states, thus strongly supressing the high-latching, structured transitions that exist for $S>1$. In the extensive-load regime, non-condensed memories generate colored fluctuations and retarded self-interactions through an effective memory kernel. We find that latching trajectories change the effective timescale of this kernel, a dynamical signature of transitions in the extensive-load regime. These results indicate that multi-state Potts-like representations are essential for structured memory-to-memory transitions, a hallmark of spontaneous thought.

Universal Infectious Disease Forecasting with Mantis 2

Carson Dudley, Reiden Magdaleno, Yutong Bi, Anthony Valencia, Xiaofeng Liu et al. (6 authors)

Public health agencies need to anticipate how outbreaks will unfold, when they will peak, and how long demand on health services will remain elevated. We previously developed a foundation model, Mantis, which was trained entirely on simulated outbreaks---enabling us to generate the diversity and scale of training data needed to support forecasting across diseases. However, our previous work left unresolved when simulation improves predictions and how it should complement observational data. Here, we varied the balance of simulated and observed training data while holding forecasting tasks fixed and varying the available surveillance history. We found that simulation-rich training was particularly valuable with limited local history, and its relative advantage diminished as more observations became available. We also found that simulation-based training reduced exceptionally large forecast errors, suggesting that mechanistic knowledge can help compensate for limited local evidence. Building on these findings, we developed Mantis 2, which combines simulated and observational training to jointly forecast up to six variables sixteen weeks ahead. Across 30 forecasting tasks, Mantis 2 achieved the best aggregate probabilistic accuracy over short, medium, and long horizons among the machine learning, statistical, and baseline models evaluated. It missed roughly 30\% fewer outbreak peaks than the strongest comparison model, with successfully anticipated peaks identified more than six weeks in advance on average. We publicly release Mantis 2, enabling public health agencies and researchers to forecast disease outcomes without developing a separate model for each disease or location.

On the minimum energy required to trigger a spike

Guanchun Tong, Rodolphe Sepulchre

We formulate and numerically study the problem of finding the minimum energy required to trigger a spike in a three-dimensional conductance-based excitable model. The variational formulation seeks a state trajectory that reaches the boundary of the spiking region with the least peak cumulative work supplied through the membrane port. We approximate voltage histories by sums of real exponentials, reducing the problem to a finite-dimensional optimization. The best-found voltage waveform consists of a hyperpolarization phase followed by a depolarization phase, which seems to match the experimentally determined optimal stimulus in squid giant axons.

Diversification under Global Competition: A Cutoff-Induced Localization Mechanism

David A. Kessler, Nadav M. Shnerb

Niche differentiation promotes biological diversity but does not necessarily eliminate competition for shared limiting resources. What determines the extent of diversity when evolutionary diversification takes place under such constraints? We investigate this question using a simple, analytically tractable model combining mutations in continuous trait space, local competition, and global competition for shared resources. We show that, without an establishment threshold, the population spreads indefinitely in trait space even as its total abundance approaches a finite limit. In contrast, an arbitrarily small establishment threshold allows a stable localized distribution. Together with mortality induced by global competition, the threshold creates bistability, and global feedback drives the system exponentially close to the Maxwell point, where an isolated invasion front is stationary. The resulting soliton has height $H\propto\varepsilon^{2/3}$ and width $W\propto\varepsilon^{-2/3}$. Simulations with discrete individuals reveal an archipelago of fluctuating clusters whose spatial extent eventually reaches statistical saturation, unlike the continuous deterministic soliton. Nevertheless, an effective cutoff $\varepsilon_{eff}\simeq0.8/(N\sqrtμ)$ reproduces the observed scaling of the population mass deficit. Remarkably, both occupied trait-site richness and effective species richness exhibit approximately the same scaling as the deterministic soliton width. Thus, deterministic cutoff theory captures global diversity scaling despite failing to reproduce the community's spatial organization.

Similarity-Guided Optimization Strategies for Approximate Network Symmetry Detection

David Hartman, Jaroslav Hlinka, Dominik Klepl, Anna Pidnebesna, Petra Vidnerová

Symmetry in complex networks is a structural property with broad implications for network function, dynamics, and robustness. While classical approaches rely on exact automorphisms and thus remain highly sensitive to small perturbations, recent work of Liu has introduced approximate formulations that relax the strict invariance condition and recast symmetry detection as an optimization problem over permutations. We investigate how node-level similarity information can be used to bias the search toward structurally plausible vertex correspondences, with network centralities serving as one interpretable instance. Building on this perspective, we adapt simulated annealing, genetic algorithms, tabu search, particle swarm optimization, differential evolution and the quadratic symmetry approximator to this problem, add similarity guidance to each, and introduce Bilinear Approximate Symmetry Inference (BASIN), which learns a soft assignment matrix via bilinear embeddings. Using a unified evaluation pipeline, we compare their guided and unguided variants, BASIN and a random baseline on four synthetic graph families, structural brain connectomes, and three empirical networks with known automorphism groups. Node-similarity guidance is decisive where a planted orbit exists that the unguided search misses, and algorithm-dependent elsewhere, on synthetic and empirical networks alike. Overall, tabu search achieves the strongest performance; its guided variant (SGTS) has the lowest median score among the guided variants in all 44 synthetic conditions, while BASIN is the better of the continuous relaxations on rewired lateral graphs. The effect of node-similarity guidance is therefore regime-dependent rather than uniformly positive.

Exact power spectra and effective dimensionality in critical block-GOE networks

Konstantinos Grammenos, Dionissios Hristopulos

Criticality in neural networks is crucial for optimal information processing. Recent studies have shifted focus from static criticality to frequency-dependent behavior, particularly in oscillatory systems modeled by block-Gaussian orthogonal ensemble (block-GOE) matrices. However, analytical tools to predict spectral properties such as power spectrum and effective dimensionality remain scarce. In this work, we derive exact solutions for the network-averaged power spectrum $\operatorname{Tr}_{N}[\mathbf{S}(ω)]$, the network autocovariance $\operatorname{Tr}_{N}[\mathbf{C}(τ)]$, and the normalized effective dimensionality $D_N(ω)$ in block-GOE ensembles. These results provide a comprehensive framework for understanding how oscillatory networks operate near criticality, offering insights into the dynamic balance between stability and flexibility in large-scale neural systems.
