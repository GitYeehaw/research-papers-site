Qualiaphobic artificial neural networks (and why to build them)

Erik Hoel

This paper introduces qualiaphobic AI: artificial neural network architectures that are hostile to theories of consciousness. I show that qualiaphobia can be well-justified by knowledge about theories of consciousness, like when such theories are trivial or unfalsifiable or contradicted by overwhelming scientific evidence. It can also be justified by how theories of consciousness must be grounded in phenomenology, as well as how current popular theories of consciousness are applied to AI. I identify and explore three convergent ways of engineering qualiaphobia that would minimize the potential for artificial consciousness. These include making AI architectures as close as possible to "anchor points" of systems with minimal potential for consciousness, fragmenting any potential phenomenology AI might have, and designing artificial neural networks in a way adversarial to popular theories of consciousness. In particular, I develop one "anchor point" of minimal potential for consciousness: systems where only behaviorist theories of consciousness can apply, and I propose qualiaphobic transformations that move existing artificial neural networks toward such systems. Additionally, I develop an account of how AIs can form justified beliefs about their own non-consciousness (or minimal potential for consciousness) and expand on how this knowledge would change their behavior. Most critically, I begin with why this research is important: qualiaphobic AI has epistemic properties that make it far better aligned with human civilization. Since consciousness is central to moral value, a world in which AIs are non-conscious ensures it is always morally correct for AI to assist and serve humanity, rather than pursue its own ends.

Spike-to-Field Mechanisms of Turbulence-Like Dynamics in Spatial Spiking Neural Networks

Zequan Liu

Neural avalanches and turbulence-like brain dynamics are usually measured after neural activity has been coarse-grained into continuous fields, leaving open which spike-level mechanisms can generate these macroscopic observables. We build a spatial leaky integrate-and-fire spiking neural network and explicitly transform spikes into activity-density and Hilbert phase fields. A delayed, distance-dependent, passive-dendrite regime reproducibly generates turbulence-like field structure, including high-amplitude phase-defect tracks, structure-function scaling, and power-law-favored continuous-field avalanche tails. Controls show that firing rate, vortex count, and avalanche tail fits are individually insufficient: destroying delayed propagation, dendritic filtering, or spike timing suppresses key field signatures, while random-space nulls can retain many mathematical phase defects but show weaker spatial structure and synaptic-delay transfer. Biologically motivated gates further shape the regime: PV-like perisomatic inhibition clamps phase dynamics, SST-like dendritic inhibition and projection-specific inhibitory facilitation preserve long-lived tracks, and a rate-constrained projection-STP regime expresses a roughly 200-ms episode at 12.25 Hz (full-record mean) across 12 seeds. These results use SNNs as a mechanistic testbed for spike-derived brain turbulence observables and show why phase defects and avalanche-like statistics must be interpreted jointly with spatial scaling and spike-level propagation.

K-prop: Deep Online Learning by Backpropagating Temporal Kernels

Bingkun Liu, Paul Haider, Federico Benitez, Mihai A. Petrovici, Walter Senn

Self-recurrence represents an important component for temporal processing in neural networks, both artificial and biological, where it appears naturally in the form of leaky integration. However, due to the complex temporal dependencies of the gradient, an efficient and effective method of online training is still lacking. Here we propose K-prop, a novel online learning method for leaky neural networks. It dramatically reduces the computational and memory cost of real time recurrent learning (RTRL) by propagating kernel gains. K-prop also outperforms existing RTRL approximations and achieves performance comparable to backpropagation through time in sequence learning, classification, and reinforcement learning tasks. We further analyze how its sole approximation affects gradient quality and identify conditions under which K-prop remains accurate and effective.

Existence of a non-zero steady-state solution to a population density equation subject to horizontal transfer

Mateo Deangeli Bravo

From plasmid dynamics and efflux pump expression in bacteria to risk-taking behaviour in human groups, horizontal interactions can drive the transmission of deleterious traits that spread despite carrying no selective advantage, thereby burdening the population. How can such detrimental traits persist, and under what conditions can the population avoid extinction? To address these questions, we model a population structured by a continuous trait representing individual burden within a non-linear, non-local integro-differential framework. From an analytical perspective, mutations are modeled via a jump operator rather than a diffusion term; consequently, the mutation operator lacks regularizing properties and is generally non-self-adjoint. Moreover, working in a weighted L 1 setting precludes the direct application of standard Krein-Rutman theory. To overcome these hurdles, we construct a custom fixed-point operator by first freezing the non-linearities to solve an auxiliary eigenvalue problem, and then tuning the resulting principal eigenvalue. Applying the Leray-Schauder fixed-point theorem to this operator rigorously yields the existence of a non-trivial steady state. Finally, numerical simulations illustrate the qualitative dynamics of the model and delineate the parameter regimes governing population persistence and extinction.

ADZE 2.0: rarefaction estimates of allelic richness and private allelic richness for whole-genome

Zachary A. Szpiech

Allelic richness and private allelic richness describe how alleles are distributed across populations, but both depend strongly on sample size. Rarefaction makes them comparable by computing the expected number of alleles in subsamples of a common size, and ADZE implemented this approach and an extension to alleles shared only by a set of populations. However, ADZE was developed for microsatellite data and cannot read sequencing data in variant call format (VCF). Here we present ADZE 2.0, which reads VCF, computes each statistic genome-wide or in genomic windows, and can treat blocks of phased SNPs as haplotype alleles. We also implement improved memory handling and multi-threading support. ADZE 2.0 reproduces the ADZE 1.0 estimates for human microsatellite data exactly. On chromosome 22 of the high-coverage 1000 Genomes Project data, private allelic richness for all 325 population pairs took 4.4 minutes on 32 threads. ADZE 2.0 is available at https://github.com/szpiech/ADZE.

TrajLift: Lifting Memory Recall Trajectories with Latency-Modulated Heat Diffusion

Jiawen Kang, Jinchao Li, Mingyu Cui, Junan Li, Xixin Wu et al. (6 authors)

Verbal memory retrieval carries rich diagnostic information for Alzheimer's disease through the order, hesitation, and categorical organization of recalled words, yet computational modeling of this retrieval process remains largely unexplored. A key challenge is that timing and hierarchical structure are diagnostically entangled: the diagnostic meaning of retrieval timing depends on its hierarchical context. We propose TrajLift, a spectral framework that models verbal recall as timed trajectories on a hierarchical graph. Our central mechanism is latency-modulated heat diffusion on the semantic hierarchy, a unified operator that lifts each recalled word into a representation of both multi-scale hierarchical structure and continuous retrieval timing. Formal analysis shows that the resulting representation is first-order separable into structural and temporal components with spectral selectivity across hierarchical levels. On synthetic benchmarks requiring structure-time interaction, TrajLift stays near-perfect on deep hierarchies where all baselines fail. On real-world clinical data, we show that TrajLift outperforms diverse baselines by jointly modeling timing and semantic structure, with gains that grow as retrieval timing becomes more informative.

Introduction to genetic simulation for animal breeding and genetics

Martin Johnsson

This article introduces genetic simulation for animal breeding and genetics. We will discuss some of the reasons to perform simulations in science, terms used to describe different types of genetic simulations, examples of common simulation approaches and tools that implement them, and how to get started with simulation in your research. The three simulation programs discussed are the population genetic coalescent simulator msprime, the evolutionary forward-in-time simulator SLiM and the quantitative genetic simulator AlphaSimR, with an emphasis on the latter as it is the most important for animal breeding and genetics. An example is also given about how to write simple population genetic simulations from scratch in a scripting language like R. Small code examples are provided.

Linear response in generalized Lotka--Volterra systems with a finite number of species

Koya Katayama, Sosuke Ito

Understanding how species interactions shape ecosystem responses to perturbations is a central challenge in ecology. Random matrix theory (RMT) has been widely used to predict the responses of large random ecosystems, but its predictions may not accurately describe individual finite ecosystems. Here, we derive rigorous geometric bounds on the numerical ranges and spectra of Jacobian matrices that are valid for any finite number of species without assuming randomness. These bounds link long-term stability and short-term stability to the symmetric component of abundance-weighted interactions, and constrain the maximum oscillation frequency by the skew-symmetric component. Applied to random ensembles, the bounds also yield constraints on ensemble averages of long-term stability, short-term stability, and the maximum oscillation frequency in terms of standard RMT parameters. Using empirical data from marine food webs, we show that our bounds provide informative constraints on short-term stability and the maximum oscillation frequency even where RMT-based predictions fail. These results establish structural constraints on local ecosystem responses that complement RMT-based statistical predictions for large random ecosystems.

HyPLM Technical Report

Haowei Lin, Cai Zhou, Xiangxin Zhou

Protein language models (PLMs) are standard tools for variant-effect prediction, protein annotation and design, but the compute needed to train and serve them keeps growing. We present HyPLM-Flash, an efficient and open PLM trained by Hyra 2.0, the research agent developed by our team. HyPLM-Flash is a 650-million-parameter masked language model trained on 726 billion tokens, 0.73 times the data of ESM-2 650M and a fifth to an eighth of the data of E1-600M and ESM C 600M. It runs on an inference engine with fused kernels and INT8 matrix multiplication, embedding about 130,000 residues per second on a single NVIDIA H20 GPU, 1.9 to 2.7 times faster than other 600M-class encoders. In a matched evaluation against 28 public checkpoints on 32 tasks, HyPLM-Flash reaches a ProteinGym zero-shot Spearman correlation of 0.409, statistically tied with ESM-2 650M, ESM C 600M and E1-600M, and the highest organismal-fitness score among self-supervised single-sequence models. It gives the best long-range contact precision of all models evaluated, including ESM-2 15B and ESM C 6B, and a lower masked-residue loss than ESM-2 650M, ESM C 600M and E1-600M on held-out sequences and on recently released PDB chains. Matching state-of-the-art accuracy at a fraction of the training and inference cost, HyPLM-Flash sets a new Pareto frontier for protein language models. We have released the model weights and the inference engine at https://github.com/Tencent-Hunyuan/hyra-results/tree/main.

HyInv Technical Report

Haowei Lin, Cai Zhou, Xiangxin Zhou

Inverse folding designs amino-acid sequences for a given protein backbone, and screening needs many candidates that are both diverse and well folded. We present HyInv, a 1.96-million-parameter backbone-conditioned graph network with an encoder-decoder architecture, trained by Hyra 2.0, the research agent developed by our team: pretrained with random-order autoregressive sequence modeling, followed by structure-reward reinforcement learning. At inference, a grouped draft-and-rewrite sampler that uses separate draft and rewrite temperatures improves refolding TM-score at matched diversity. An optimized implementation with BF16 numerics designs 128 sequences for a 402-residue backbone in 50.6 ms on a single NVIDIA H20 GPU, about 52 times faster than the reference sampler, and an exact FP32 path that returns the reference sequences is 8.7 times faster. Across five benchmark datasets and seven dataset-predictor combinations, HyInv reaches a higher average scTM than all ten external baselines at comparable or higher sequence diversity, with large margins over ProteinMPNN and ProteinZero V2. The same default setting supports de novo backbones, pocket-constrained libraries, enzyme redesign with the catalytic site kept, and loop redesign. We have released our code and model at https://github.com/Tencent-Hunyuan/Hyra-results/tree/main/AI4Science/hyinv.
