Two Routes to Interoceptive Prioritisation: Dissociating Likelihood Precision and Preference Reweighting in Active Inference

St John Grimbly, Nicolas Kuske, Evert A. Boonstra, Charel van Hoof, Rowan Hodson et al. (9 authors)

An embodied agent must prioritise among competing bodily needs. In active inference, it can respond to an urgent need by increasing the precision of information about that need or by increasing its preference for outcomes that relieve it. We compare these two interventions, separately and together, in a foraging gridworld with three lethal needs, and ask how each changes survival, perception and the body model the agent learns. Precision sharpens interoceptive posteriors and improves survival. Preference reweighting keeps bodily states further from their lethal boundaries and changes the states from which the agent learns, so its learned model covers fewer need levels, with lower quality across all levels but slightly higher quality on the levels it visits. An experiment on 72 further layouts does not establish the predicted preference benefit on difficult layouts, but both interventions improve average survival there and their interaction is positive. Survival alone therefore gives an incomplete account of how a model prioritises needs and learns about its body.

The influence of decay in dynamic models of gene regulatory networks

Oleksandr Pokutnyi, Felix Sadyrbaev

We investigate the influence of decay rates on the dynamics of three-dimensional gene regulatory networks modeled by nonlinear differential equations with logistic sigmoidal regulatory functions. Particular attention is paid to the existence, location, and stability of equilibrium points in systems involving numerous interacting parameters. An explicit analytical representation of a central equilibrium is obtained under suitable relations between the regulatory weights, thresholds, and decay coefficients, allowing its location to be determined without solving the corresponding nonlinear algebraic system numerically. General formulas for the characteristic polynomial of the linearized system are derived, and spectral conditions for instability are established for several classes of regulatory interactions. We demonstrate how variations in decay rates can change the number and stability of equilibria, transform oscillatory dynamics, and influence the emergence or suppression of complicated attractors. The possibility of chaotic behavior is investigated through eigenvalue configurations, Lyapunov exponents, and the Kaplan-Yorke dimension. Numerical examples illustrate the sensitivity of the dynamics to decay parameters, including the suppression of previously observed chaotic behavior. The results provide an analytical framework for investigating equilibrium configurations, stability transitions, and complex dynamics in parametrized gene regulatory networks.

Sense-Making phase transition framework for Affect

Miguel Bosch, Laura Fanfarillo, Gustavo Diez, Victor Gómez Mayordomo, J. Ricardo Arias-Gonzalez et al. (6 authors)

Contemporary accounts of affect emphasize different perspectives, ranging from the regulation of bodily states in allostatic approaches to the relational and sense-making character stressed by enactive theories. Here, we show that affect can be made explicit within the sense-making phase transition framework previously introduced for the perception of an inner sensation. Within this same formal structure, each stable minimum of the sense-making potential defines an affective state: its position determines valence, while its depth determines arousal. Affect therefore is not introduced as an independent ingredient, but emerges as the associated state in the valence-arousal space, providing a link between allostatic and enactive approaches to affect. This formulation distinguishes valence intensity from arousal, allows for dominant and secondary affective states, and naturally accommodates mixed-valence configurations beyond a strictly bipolar description. Revisiting representative landscapes of the model, we show how neutral, uncertain, polarized, and mixed-valence regimes arise as a function of contextual sensory information, learned historical evaluation, and externally supplied information. We further show that the resulting valence-arousal relation provides a theoretical basis for the upper-open V-shaped organization discussed in the affect literature, while also opening a natural route toward a dynamical description of affect and emotions.

Lossless Reductions and Hybrid Sampling for Phylogeny Using the Maximum Compatibility Criterion

Orson Ye, James Bonfield, Joshua Cudby, Richard Durbin, Sergii Strelchuk

Maximum compatibility provides a criterion for phylogenetic reconstruction in which the objective is to retain a maximum set of mutually compatible characters. For binary characters, pairwise compatibility can be represented by a weighted graph whose vertices correspond to characters and whose edges connect compatible pairs. A leading algorithm by Cherry [BMC Bioinformatics, 18(1):127, 2017] uses this representation to identify maximum compatible character sets as maximum weight cliques. We formulate the problem as maximum weight independent set enumeration on the complement graph and use deterministic postprocessing to map sampled bit strings to maximal feasible solutions. To explore the prospects of quantum computational advantage, we further construct bespoke correction-in-the-loop Quantum Approximate Optimisation Algorithm instances and find that traditional Lagrange multiplier choices may not be optimal for the enumeration problem on noisy devices. We also introduce lossless graph reductions with reconstruction rules that preserve the complete set of optimal solutions. Our results indicate that graph reduction and sampling can be effectively combined to address the enumeration bottleneck in maximum compatibility phylogeny, while retaining exact reconstruction when the residual optimum set is completely resolved. We enhance Cherry's approach to maximum compatibility by introducing graph reduction techniques and quantum sampling. Our classical benchmarks show a clear difference between finding one optimum and recovering the optimum set in practice. We demonstrate the utility of our methods on graphs constructed from SARS-CoV-2 sequences.

Rewiring of adult neural networks - learning from structural plasticity across multiple scales

Michael Fauth, Han Lu, Jannik Luboeinski, Johanna Senk, Simone Albani et al. (39 authors)

Synaptic connections are continuously formed and eliminated even in adult neuronal networks. This process - called structural plasticity - allows networks to change their topology to learn and adapt to their environment or compensate for lesions. Yet, in contrast to synaptic plasticity, structural plasticity is less well studied and understood. We here present the current state of knowledge as well as open problems in the field along three guiding questions: (1) What regulates structural plasticity in biological neural networks? (2) What are the functional consequences and advantages of structural plasticity in adult neural networks? (3) How can our understanding of structural plasticity be translated into clinical and technological applications? We argue that understanding structural plasticity is a problem involving multiple temporal and spatial scales. Solving this problem thus requires collaborative efforts of experimentalists, theoreticians as well as computational modelers, and has the potential for comprehensive neuroscientific insights that can guide the development of medical applications, artificial neural networks as well as neuromorphic computing.

Spatial Kernel Restricted Canonical Correlation Analysis: Population Targets, Identifiability, and Stability

Zhen Miao, Yilong Qu, Nancy R. Zhang

Relating observations of different categories across space requires methods that accommodate distinct sampling locations and spatial dependence. Spatial kernel restricted canonical correlation analysis (skrCCA) uses spatial weights to identify associated feature combinations across data categories. Here, we develop a statistical theory of its covariance form after feature reduction. Conditional on the observed locations and under a mean-zero model, the pairwise association matrices target kernel-weighted averages of cross-covariances. For prespecified directions, we derive the exact null variance under independence between blocks. We establish recovery and stability bounds in two- and multiblock settings under explicit population models, and show that exact cyclic updates have convergent objective values. The variance characterization shows how spatial weighting and within-block dependence jointly determine the effective information available for estimating association. The recovery bounds separate statistical and optimization error, relating stability to population separation and the strength of connections between blocks. These results clarify what fitted spatial association axes estimate and provide conditions under which they can be recovered reliably.

Understanding Gene Expression-to-Image Learning: Spatial Structure and Information Fidelity

Xiaoqing Lian, Pei Liu, Jiahui Tan, Quan Zou, Chen Lin et al. (6 authors)

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) enables cell-level analysis of biological heterogeneity, but learning effective representations from high-dimensional, unordered gene expression profiles remains challenging. Gene Expression-to-Image (GE2I) offers an effective learning strategy by arranging gene values on pixel grids for convolutional neural networks, but its underlying mechanisms remain unclear. We study GE2I on full-transcriptome data while keeping the network and training procedure fixed. Our results reveal a trade-off between spatial smoothness and gene-level fidelity. Structured mappings produce smooth, low-frequency patterns that support faster early optimization, whereas assigning multiple genes to the same pixel obscures expression differences and can reduce predictive performance. This trade-off explains why random mappings can compete with structured layouts when many genes are represented. One-to-one gene-to-pixel assignment improves prediction across the tested visual backbones even when image smoothness decreases. We further find that the benefits of pretraining and augmentation depend on the backbone and mapping. These findings provide practical guidance for pixel allocation and the transfer of vision priors to gene expression data.

Rethinking World Models for Biosignals

Xianglong Bao, Bosong Huang, Xiaoda Wang, Keqi Han, Carl Yang et al. (7 authors)

World models promise to learn how systems evolve and respond to actions, enabling simulation, planning and decision-making. Bringing this promise to biosignals requires more than accurate prediction of physiological recordings. We examine existing biosignal world models through two requirements for intervention use: a genuine action and evidence of its causal effect. We identify three fundamental bottlenecks rooted in physiology: Temporal Latency, the variable delay between an action and its observable effect; System Openness, the influence of drivers beyond the modelled intervention; and Endogenous Dynamics, the system's ongoing evolution without that intervention. Drawing on published evidence, we outline corresponding research directions and propose an Intervention-Effect Validation Checklist to guide evaluation. The aim is to help the biosignal community realise the core promise of world models by learning the latent mechanisms that govern physiological evolution and responses to interventions.

Collective Benefits and Efficiency in Coupled Echo State Networks

Polyphony Bruna, Rick Dale, Michael J. Spivey, Christopher T. Kello

Simple aggregation of independent responses ("wisdom of crowds") generally improves collective performance through statistical facilitation, but interaction among members can sometimes hinder group outcomes. However, under certain circumstances, coordination studies show that interacting groups can outperform their individual members and nominal groups, and interaction can also be more efficient than independence. We present a reservoir computing model to investigate how interaction can enhance joint performance in terms of accuracy and efficiency. Echo state networks were trained independently to identify Japanese speakers from vowel recordings and tested jointly with and without coupled feedback. Model pairs reproduced the dyadic advantage reported by Bahrami et al. (2010) with interaction via pooled-feedback, but not when independent responses were aggregated together. Model dynamics revealed that interaction was beneficial whereas aggregated outputs were prone to diverging from runaway feedback over time. In terms of efficiency, larger groups of smaller interacting models outperformed smaller groups of larger models while equating for total amount of group computation. Our results offer a formal framework for explaining when and why interaction enhances collective intelligence.

Beyond Successful Play: Diagnosing Opponent Modeling in Large Language Models

Hanbo Xie, Yue Zhao, Robert C. Wilson

Language-model agents are expected to adapt to others whose behavior changes through interaction. Yet adaptation may rely on learned behavioral patterns rather than theory of mind (ToM): the ability to infer others' mental states, including beliefs and intentions. We introduce a benchmark and diagnostic framework for repeated hide-and-seek against algorithmic opponents with explicitly defined recursive belief updates. Their ground-truth beliefs and action probabilities let us distinguish adaptive play, action prediction, and belief tracking through live interaction, fixed-history evaluation, and controlled information interventions. With structured elicitation and behavioral supervised fine-tuning (SFT), we find improved play in several tested settings, but diagnostics reveal limitations in opponent prediction and belief tracking. We test whether supervising opponent action probabilities can address this predictive deficit in Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct. Predictions improve when the model is told the opponent's type and parameter settings, but become less accurate when it must infer the opponent from history without this information. Training on examples that also omit these details reduces the gap, although improvements vary across opponents. Is this limitation specific to smaller open-weight models, or does it also appear in a frontier model? GPT-5.4 also benefits from correct opponent beliefs: supplying them improves its predictions and choices, whereas deeper explicit belief reports and self-generated memory do not consistently improve forecasting from interaction history. This contrast suggests that using useful belief information is easier than inferring it autonomously, even for a frontier model. Together, our findings distinguish improved play and action prediction from reliable belief tracking, providing a framework for evaluating mentalizing and testing targeted improvements.
