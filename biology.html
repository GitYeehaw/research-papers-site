How Far Can Linear Go? BiPert, a Unified Framework for Single-Cell Perturbation Modelling

Abhishek Sharma, Aleksej Zelezniak

Understanding how genetic perturbations affect cellular behaviour is essential for studying gene function and regulatory mechanisms. Large-scale CRISPR screens measure these effects across thousands of genes, but cover relatively few cellular contexts. Predicting perturbation responses in unseen cell types and identifying perturbed genes from their transcriptional effects therefore remain challenging. Here, we show that both tasks can be addressed through a common linear decomposition of transcriptional responses into shared and perturbation-specific components. Building on this principle, we introduce BiPert, a simple linear framework for both forward perturbation prediction and inverse identification. For forward prediction, BiPert outperforms existing methods that use no external biological knowledge when predicting gene expression changes in unseen cell lines. Its few-shot extension, BiPert-FS, adapts to new cell lines using as few as ten measured perturbations. For inverse identification, BiPert-ID identifies perturbed genes from single-cell expression profiles, outperforming deep-learning methods in unseen cell lines and donors. We further show that perturbations whose effects transfer more consistently across cellular contexts are also easier to identify, even after controlling for perturbation effect size. Together, these results establish a connection between perturbation transfer and identification, and demonstrate that simple linear models provide strong, interpretable baselines for both tasks.

LeCellModel: Interpretable Density Estimation over the Gene Expression Manifold

Gil Karin, Artemii Bakulin, Nir Yosef

Representation learning for single-cell RNA-seq has been evaluated by the identities representations capture -- cell types, lineages, perturbations -- rather than how faithfully they model the distribution of cell states on the gene expression manifold. As a result, density estimation, rare-state detection, perturbation scoring, and gene-level attribution are bolted onto frozen embeddings as separate models, disconnected from the geometry of the data. We argue that modeling the distribution of cell states should be the primary objective, and that representation quality follows from it. Building on LeJEPA, whose SIGReg objective enforces a provably optimal isotropic Gaussian latent, we introduce LeCellModel, which reads the encoder's Jacobian log-volume (JEPA-SCORE) under this Gaussian latent as a relative typicality score. Across several datasets, LeCellModel outperforms established methods on the scIB benchmark for representation quality. Furthermore, we show that LeCellModel enables detection of rare expression states using JEPA-SCORE as a typicality measure, applying it to a controlled cytokine panel, where it identifies stimulated cells across minority fractions and tracks pathway-response magnitude. We introduce gene-level interpretability of the typicality score -- to our knowledge, the first method for analyzing JEPA-SCORE in feature space. Leveraging this framework, LeCellModel recapitulates the known association of a profibrotic macrophage program with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, and discovers a CD177+ activated regulatory T cell subpopulation previously described only in tumor contexts. Both findings emerge directly from the geometry of the learned embedding, without relying on supervised annotations.

Big Tree Viewer: A high-performance browser-based phylogenetic tree viewer with agentic AI integration

John B. Allard, Sudhir Kumar

Phylogenetic trees represent evolutionary history visually, and the ability to display and explore them is a key function in research across biological disciplines. Very large gene and species trees are becoming common with the increasing availability of low cost DNA sequencing, creating performance and informativeness challenges for display software originally designed for phylogenies with a few hundred to a few thousand tips. We discuss these limitations and introduce a browser-based Big Tree Viewer (BTV) highly optimized for fast display and biologically informative exploration of phylogenetic trees containing even a million tips. BTV can automatically map binomial species names to the NCBI or Catalogue of Life taxonomies and display colored ribbons to indicate taxonomic groups for enhanced exploration. Even without taxonomic mapping, BTV automatically labels groups of neighboring tips that share the first word of their names, such as a genus or gene family name, providing readable large-scale landmarks when individual tip labels are too dense to read. BTV supports metadata display, visual customization, tanglegram-like tree comparisons, statistics, figure export, and multiple tree layouts, including a new spiral form to display enormously large trees. BTV is also designed to integrate with common agentic AI tools, allowing agents to display, style, annotate, and inspect trees at the user's request. The BTV web app is freely available at https://BigTreeViewer.net and runs entirely in the user's browser, with no data uploads to any central server. Desktop versions of the BTV app for macOS, Windows, and Linux are also provided for download.

From joint attention to shared symbols: A free-energy principle account

Tadahiro Taniguchi, Koji Hashimoto, Ryuichiro Ishikawa

Joint attention precedes vocabulary acquisition. How can attending to the same object help two people with different sensory experiences come to use a shared word? In a free-energy principle account, marginalizing the common object induces a statistical dependence between private perceptual states. In a categorical world, the joint prior inherits a mixture structure whose component index provides a candidate for shared reference. The collective predictive coding model approximates this prior by representing the dependence through that shared index. An established Metropolis-Hastings naming game lets agents infer and exchange words using private evidence. Toy and multiview image experiments illustrate how word exchange can coordinate private categories and improve the fit of collective models. The account connects joint attention to shared symbols through the coupling of private inference.

Learning Multimodal Representations from Partially Paired, Small-Scale Data

Merel Kuijs, Till Richter, Rushin H. Gindra, Korbinian Traeuble, Christian Matek et al. (8 authors)

Modeling multimodal data from partially paired samples is critical for advancing domains like biomedicine, where vast unimodal datasets and foundation models exist but paired data remains scarce. Existing fusion methods rely on large-scale paired datasets, limiting their use in scenarios with incomplete pairing. We introduce CAMEO, an adversarial learning-based modality fusion framework that integrates modalities from small, partially paired datasets. Combining any pre-trained unimodal encoder with a cross-modal latent alignment mechanism, CAMEO learns shared representations requiring only minimal paired samples. Evaluated on computational pathology tasks such as niche classification and cell type composition prediction, CAMEO achieves superior data efficiency, outperforming contrastive approaches like CLIP in low paired-data regimes, and highlighting the benefits of adversarial alignment when paired annotations are scarce. To facilitate further research, we additionally release a fully annotated HuggingFace dataset comprising three organs and paired image and gene expression modalities. By extending fusion methods to address limited pairing and small-scale datasets, we provide a framework that advances multimodal learning and broadens its applicability to real-world biomedical problems.

Interactions with hidden traits induce diversification and conceal its origin

Vikas Kumar Jha, Praveen V, Suman Chakraborty, Vaibhav Madhok

Adaptive diversification is often inferred from a small number of measurable traits, even though evolutionary dynamics arise from many interacting phenotypic dimensions. Unmeasured traits may therefore do more than bias inference: through their interactions, they may determine whether diversification occurs at all. Here we develop a theory of the onset and attribution of adaptive diversification by partitioning phenotype space into focal and hidden sectors. Eliminating the hidden sector yields an exact effective operator. Coupling to hidden traits that are stabilizing in isolation can destabilize an otherwise stable phenotype and generate a diversifying composite mode: the interaction creates diversification rather than merely strengthening it. Conversely, when the hidden sector is itself diversifying, the focal traits diversify as a correlated response to a hidden-driven process: no effective description in the focal traits, not even the exact one obtained by eliminating the hidden sector, predicts the variation they display, and stabilizing the focal traits reduces it without removing it. These effects become strongest near the hidden sector's own threshold, so a weakly stabilized hidden trait can have a large effect even with modest coupling to the focal traits, and they can generate correlations between focal traits that have no direct interaction. Onset and observability are distinct: systems with the same leading eigenvalue can project very differently onto the measured traits, so the theory tracks both the full operator and the focal-hidden partition. Individual-based simulations recover the predicted threshold and variance law, coupling-induced trait splitting, and the reversal of induced correlations. Interactions with unmeasured traits can both create adaptive diversification and determine whether the diversification seen in measured phenotypes can be attributed to them.

Impact of Topology and Sequence Length on the Pressure-Dependent Collapsibility of Proteins

Jiayi Wang, D. Thirumalai, Margaret S. Cheung, Andrei G. Gasic

Several physical and evolutionary factors, including temperature, pressure, solvent quality, and natural selection, influence how amino acid sequences adopt their native structures. Although protein folding has been extensively studied, the physical determinants of protein collapse and their relationship to native-state folding remain less understood. In particular, "collapsibility", the intrinsic propensity of a protein sequence to form compact conformations, is distinct from but closely related to "foldability", the ability to reach the native structure. Previous theoretical studies suggested that collapsibility is encoded in native-state topology, potentially reflecting evolutionary selection. However, the roles of solvent-mediated interactions and hydrostatic pressure remain unclear. Here, we extend the theoretical framework of protein collapsibility by incorporating a pressure-dependent desolvation potential to investigate how hydrostatic pressure influences protein collapse. We find that increasing pressure destabilizes the fully folded native state, but can still favor the compact, water-mediated conformations. Furthermore, the magnitude and direction of pressure-induced changes in collapsibility depend strongly on native-state topology, including secondary and tertiary structures. Proteins and domains with a higher proportion of non-local native contacts generally exhibit greater pressure-induced water-mediated collapsibility. These findings highlight the role of native-state topology in determining the pressure sensitivity of protein collapse and provide insights into the interplay among protein structure, solvent-mediated interactions, and environmental perturbations.

What Do Co-Folding Models Learn? Insights from Enhanced Transition Path Sampling

Muhammad R. Hasyim

Co-folding models are diffusion-based generative models that sample biomolecular complexes from distributions learned on a large corpus of experimental data. Although these models are powerful for structure prediction, it remains unclear whether the conformational ensembles they learn resemble physically meaningful statistical ensembles. To understand what these models learn, we introduce transition path sampling (TPS), which uses Markov chain Monte Carlo over denoising trajectories to sample the learned distribution. Because its Monte Carlo trajectories resemble biomolecular dynamics, TPS couples directly with enhanced-sampling techniques to recover the full conformational ensemble. Applied to Boltz-2, enhanced TPS reveals physical conformational changes biased toward crystal-like states near the training data, indicating both learned physics and memorization. These results suggest new ways to evaluate and improve co-folding models by jointly assessing structural recovery, proximity to training data, and conformational ensembles.

Separating Genetic and Protein Evidence in Open Targets: An Auditable Re-ranking Study

Ye Ye

Biomedical target-ranking platforms combine genetics, literature, experimental evidence, tractability, and clinical precedent because each is useful for target discovery. A single integrated score, however, can be awkward to reuse for a narrower question such as which genes have direct evidence for disease risk or etiology. We examine this issue in Open Targets associations for Alzheimer disease (AD) and Parkinson disease (PD). Genetics-linked evidence and protein-oriented evidence are scored in separate channels and combined only at the end. In AD, the Open Targets overall top 20 contains 7 curated genetics-supported genes and 10 symptomatic pharmacology controls; the default late fusion contains 15 curated genes and no symptomatic controls. In PD, late fusion retains 13 curated genes and removes the 5 symptomatic controls present in the platform top 20. A genetics-only ranking reaches similar curated recall (14 genes in AD, 13 in PD), so most of the gain over the platform score comes from emphasizing genetic evidence; the protein channel mainly raises the number of tractable targets in the shortlist (from 9 to 12 in AD and from 8 to 10 in PD) without readmitting symptomatic controls. These results are stable across fusion weights from 0.35 to 0.75, perturbed protein-channel weights, and rule thresholds. An optional post-ranking rule further lowers high-clinical/low-genetic targets such as ACHE and GRIN1 without changing top-20 curated recall. The procedure is deterministic and uses public Open Targets fields. Because the component scores remain available, individual rank changes can be inspected and the ranking can be reproduced from the same data snapshot.

Second-Order Dynamics in Noisy RNNs Produce Exploration and Temporal Compression in Replay

Josue Casco-Rodriguez, Nanda H. Krishna, Richard G. Baraniuk

Biological neural networks (like the hippocampus) can internally generate "replay" resembling stimulus-driven activity. Recent computational models of replay use noisy recurrent neural networks (RNNs) trained to path-integrate. Replay in these networks is described as Langevin sampling, but this theory alone fails to capture the environmental exploration and temporal compression observed in real biological replay. To close this gap, we re-examine spontaneous noisy RNN activity ("replay") as Langevin sampling, one timestep at a time, from a time-series distribution resembling lived experiences (unlike previous works which defined sampling as Monte Carlo inference over a static distribution). We provide a normative second-order Langevin sampling framework wherein spike-frequency adaptation drives spatial exploration at the cost of speed, and momentum via dendritic input differentiation drives temporal compression. We numerically verify our framework via simulations of noisy RNNs trained on 2D triangular and T-maze navigation activity, and others trained on place cell activity of synthetic rats. Crucially, we observe that simultaneous adaptation and momentum increases both replay exploration and speed, and provide one of the first accounts for biologically plausible temporal compression in rate-based RNN replay. Our findings encourage experiments into whether dendritic input differentiation, or other mechanisms, may support temporal compression in memory circuits like the hippocampus.
