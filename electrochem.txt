Bond-valence theory replaces the equal split of valence among bonds by $s=\exp[(R_0-d)/B]$ in the bond length $d$ and takes the sum rule, that the valences at each atom add to its formal charge, as its first axiom. Here the bond valence is defined in the electron density. Atomic basins of a first-principles density are bounded by faces, one per bond critical point, and the electric flux of that density through a face is a charge associated with the bond. The fluxes leaving a cation sum to its basin charge by Gauss's law, so on this surface the sum rule is a theorem. Across 3680 oxides, halides, chalcogenides and pnictides the flux follows the bond-valence law site by site, with a median deviation of ten percent. The softness $B$ varies nearly an order of magnitude from stiff sulfides to softer arsenides. In all cases it is set by the electrostatic flux at atomic basin faces. $R_0$ then follows from the bond lengths and their differences. Anion valences equal those of bonded cations to within 5\% for most of the oxide, fluoride, sulfide and nitride sites.
