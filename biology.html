A unified coarse-grained neural network potential for the dynamics of biomolecular complexes

Antonio Mirarchi, Stephen E. Farr, Adrià Peréz, Gianni De Fabritiis

The behavior of biomolecular complexes is governed by chemically specific interactions and often involves rare events that atomistic molecular dynamics struggle to capture within accessible timescales. Here we introduce AMARO v2.0, a coarse-grained neural network potential that retains all heavy atoms and treats solvent implicitly. Hydrogen-aware bead types and total-charge conditioning provide a shared representation for proteins, drug-like molecules, and their complexes, including ternary protein-protein-ligand systems. The model preserves folded proteins and captures key features of protein conformational free-energy landscapes. Across 262 complexes spanning 11 targets, the learned potential retains most ligand poses, although quaternary structure reorientation is observed for the HIF2A ternary complex. In targeted case studies, AMARO v2.0 distinguishes the two protonation states of trypsin-benzamidine and reaches the crystal binding pose in 3.3-fold more trajectories than all-atom molecular mechanics at a fixed estimated GPU-time budget. The same framework also recovers a reference binding pose in TYK2 and supports binding free-energy estimation for both trypsin-benzamidine and TYK2-EJM31, although the resulting values are more energetically favorable than the experimental references. SHP2 and CDK8 systems expose remaining limitations in binding-pose recovery and site specificity, respectively. Together, these results support AMARO v2.0 as a shared heavy-atom potential for biomolecular complex dynamics.

Every route counts, but some count more: a max-plus view of the C. elegans brain

Emma Reyner-Fuentes, Sebastian Rudolph, Carmen Peláez-Moreno, Francisco J. Valverde-Albacete

Brains do not simply add up every signal they receive: they select which routes dominate. Yet connectomes are usually analyzed with tools that sum over all paths and place neural states in Euclidean spaces, blurring the feedback circuits that may sustain internal states. Here we treat neural signaling as routing instead of diffusion and describe it with max-plus algebra, in which signals accumulate along a path and the strongest route prevails. We propose a model in which the functional connectome of Caenorhabditis elegans emerges from the time-varying combination of electrical, synaptic, monoaminergic, neuropeptidergic and hormonal layers, and analyze five networks within this framework. Each collapses onto a handful of dominant feedback loops, which recover well-characterized circuits and point to new candidates for experimental study. Unlike linear spectra, these structures keep the biological meaning of the data, and their order-based description suggests that brain states live in lattices rather than Euclidean spaces.

Sufficiency Theorem and Closed-Form Posterior for a Windowed Change Point Detection Task

Payton Hanks, Rishidev Chaudhuri, Timothy D. Hanks

To function effectively in a changing world, agents must detect changes and act on them in a timely fashion. We study this in the context of a change point detection task in which clicks arrive from a Poisson process whose rate changes at an unknown time. An agent must declare the change within a detection window of fixed width or the trial ends in a miss. Here we prove a window sufficiency theorem, which states that the posterior probability of a change depends only on the clicks within the most recent window, regardless of the prior on the change time. This result follows from combining survival conditioning, which rules out change times whose windows have already expired, with cancellations driven by independent increments of a known baseline process. Window sufficiency generalizes to an unknown post-change process, as long as the baseline process is known. For an exponential prior on the change time, we further derive the posterior in closed form as a function of the elapsed times since each click in the most recent window, the known rates, and the window width. These results suggest that discarding old evidence can be optimal rather than a limitation when decisions face a deadline.

Accurate structural modelling of protein-glycan recognition with GlycoWeave

Huifeng Zhao, Jintu Zhang, Yifei Xiao, Youqiao Qian, Silong Zhai et al. (21 authors)

Protein-glycan recognition underlies diverse biological processes, yet accurate prediction of protein-glycan complex structures remains challenging owing to the chemical diversity, conformational flexibility and sparse experimental structural coverage of glycans. Here we present GlycoWeave, a glycan-specific diffusion framework that integrates chemically constrained conformational sampling, multiscale molecular representations and multi-source learning. By operating in internal-coordinate space, GlycoWeave preserves glycan connectivity and stereochemistry while efficiently exploring bound conformations. A heterogeneous equivariant network couples atom-level interaction geometry with monosaccharide-level structural context, whereas multi-source, multi-head training integrates experimental complexes with structures refined using classical and quantum mechanics-informed potentials. On a benchmark of 698 protein-glycan complexes, GlycoWeave achieves a 71.9% Top-25 high-quality success rate, compared with 19.2% for AlphaFold 3, while requiring only 0.34 s per sampled pose. GlycoWeave further preserves glycan structural validity, recovers native protein-glycan interactions and reproduces linkage-specific conformational preferences, maintaining high accuracy across diverse glycan architectures and protein environments. These results establish GlycoWeave as an accurate, efficient and chemically faithful framework for modelling protein-glycan recognition. GlycoWeave is freely available at https://cadd.zju.edu.cn/glycoweave.

Local learning is all you need: brain-aligned predictive coding networks

Dirk Gütlin, Ryszard Auksztulewicz, Adrien Doerig

Predictive Coding (PC) is an influential account of how brain representations emerge through hierarchical, local, self-supervised prediction-based learning, without requiring globally propagated error signals. Despite extensive evidence supporting PC, a striking paradox persists: computational models built on these biologically grounded principles have consistently failed to match state-of-the-art Deep Neural Networks in performance and brain-alignment. Here, we show that this paradox can be overcome. We introduce Predictive Brain Networks (PBNs), a hierarchical recurrent framework that implements local PC-based learning and achieves representational alignment with the human ventral visual stream matching or exceeding established vision models. Importantly, PBN top-down predictions encode unique, brain-aligned information not captured during bottom-up feature extraction, with this contribution increasing in locally trained variants. Further, PBNs substantially outperform existing PC-inspired networks on downstream classification tasks, reproduce key signatures of PC (with higher-level predictions shaping lower-level visual responses as observed in the primate cortex), and support uncertainty estimation. PBNs therefore close a longstanding gap in computational neuroscience by combining two properties that have been largely separated: the brain-inspired learning mechanisms of predictive coding and the representational power of modern deep networks. They provide a scalable framework for studying biologically grounded learning without sacrificing the capacity to produce rich neural representations.

pyTransRate: 1 reference-free quality assessment of de novo transcriptome assemblies with current aligners and quantifiers

Matthew MacManes

Summary: The original software TransRate scores a de novo transcriptome assembly by mapping the reads it was built from back to it, with no reference required. Its last release (1.0.3) depends on Ruby 2.2 and on aligner and quantifier versions that can no longer be installed on most systems. pyTransRate is a Python reimplementation of the TransRate method built on snap-aligner 2.0.5 and salmon 2.7.0. In 5,031 paired comparisons (39 assembly-level metrics on each of 129 public assemblies), it reproduces the original sequence metrics and read counts exactly and ranks assemblies consistently with 1.0.3 (Spearman rho = 0.94 for assembly score, 0.98 for good-mapping rate), although its read-based scores are not numerically interchangeable with the original. It also reads gzip- and bgzip-compressed input directly. Availability and Implementation: pyTransRate is implemented in Python 3 and is freely available under the MIT license at https://github.com/macmanes-lab/pytransrate. It runs on Linux and macOS; snap-aligner and salmon are installed from Bioconda. Supplementary information: Supplementary data are available online. Supplementary Table S1 lists the assembly and read accessions for every assembly analyzed; Supplementary Table S2 gives per-assembly values from both implementations.

Exploring Neural Dynamics with Time Delays: A Numerical and Bifurcation Analysis Approach

Martin R Nelson, Jonathan J Crofts

The Wilson-Cowan model is a classic example of a ``neural mass model'' - a simplified yet powerful way to describe the activity of large networks of neurons. It is particularly interesting because, even in its relatively simple form, it can produce a surprising variety of behaviours, from steady rhythms to complex oscillations. A key factor influencing these dynamics is the presence of time delays, reflecting the time it takes for signals to travel between neurons. This article offers an accessible introduction to the Wilson-Cowan model, focusing on how these time delays affect its behaviour. We explore the core concepts behind the model, survey the existing literature, and introduce some numerical techniques specifically designed for analysing such delay differential equations. We also provide a practical, hands-on tutorial on using the DDE-BIFTOOL software package, demonstrating its application to the Wilson-Cowan model. Downloadable code allows the reader to reproduce our results and to explore the model's dynamics further, including MATLAB code for all computations, using DDE-BIFTOOL for the continuation, and Python versions of simulation codes.

Neuronal Identity as an Organizational Basis for Analyzing Neural Population Dynamics

Seungjae Han, Joshua Yedam You, Soi Kim, Minho Eom, Jinho Park et al. (8 authors)

Individual neurons exhibit diverse molecular, morphological, and electrophysiological properties that shape their distinct roles in neural circuits. While these multimodal features are largely shared across individuals, they remain an underutilized source of information in analyzing neural population dynamics. We posit that these shared biological profiles provide a common reference for relating neural dynamics across subjects and recording sessions. Our framework leverages this structure by routing neurons into latent groups based on their biological profiles and analyzing population dynamics through group-level representations. Because these assignments are anchored in biological properties that are consistent across subjects, the learned organization supports transfer to unseen animals without retraining or neuron-wise correspondence. We evaluate our framework on whole-brain calcium imaging in larval zebrafish and large-scale electrophysiology in the mouse visual cortex. Across both species and recording modalities, the framework delivers state-of-the-art performance in both neural decoding and manifold analysis: it achieves superior decoding of behavior and stimulus labels while better preserving the temporal consistency and local geometric structure of latent trajectories on unseen data. Our experiments demonstrate that not only does providing biological features improve baseline performance, but our proposed framework achieves even stronger results by using these properties to explicitly structure the population rather than treating them as auxiliary inputs. Together, these results suggest that organizing neural populations by their multimodal biological features offers a promising framework for generalizing population dynamics across subjects and recording sessions.

The geometry of cognition: from neural populations to neurotechnology

Lucas Bayones, Devika Narain, Roman Rossi-Pool

Cognition is implemented by distributed neural populations whose collective activity evolves through structured, low-dimensional state spaces. In this review, we survey the analytical and conceptual tools used to study latent population dynamics in biological neural networks. We begin with single-unit coding, which remains essential for interpreting neuronal selectivity, and then review population-level approaches, including population vectors, cosine similarity, principal component analysis and its mnemonic variants, multivariable linear regression axes, demixed principal component analysis, and single-trial latent-variable models, and distinguish operationally defined population subspaces from neural manifolds with reproducible geometry or topology. We use these methods to discuss sensory coding, working memory, decision-making, temporal processing, and sensorimotor transformations. Two organizing principles are emphasized throughout. First, cognitive variables can be expressed in separable or near-orthogonal population dimensions, reducing interference between sensory, mnemonic, decision-related, and motor signals. Second, such geometric separation does not require anatomically or functionally discrete cell classes; separable population readouts can coexist with a continuum of mixed-selective single-neuron responses within a shared biological substrate. We then consider recurrent neural networks as testbeds for generating and evaluating mechanistic hypotheses about how circuits produce low-dimensional dynamics, and intracortical brain-computer interfaces as a translational domain in which population geometry becomes an operational control signal. Overall, this review argues that population dynamics now provide a common language for linking neurophysiology, computational modelling, and neurotechnology.

ACOR: incremental evidence accumulation towards real-time DNA storage reconstruction

Linxuan Han, Huaming Wu, Shi Jia, Guanjin Qu

DNA offers high-density, durable storage by encoding digital information into synthetic strands and retrieving it through sequencing. Sequence reconstruction combines noisy copies of each strand before downstream decoding. Conventional offline methods typically reconstruct completed read clusters, separating reconstruction from ongoing read collection. Streaming reconstruction offers a way to overlap evidence processing with sequencing and reduce the computation remaining before downstream decoding. We present ACOR, a streaming consensus reconstruction method that records $k$-mer transitions and boundary support in a persistent ledger, then decodes through length-constrained beam search and priority-based selection at cluster closure. Evaluation on real and simulated DNA-storage datasets showed competitive recovery. On our own short-read next-generation sequencing (NGS) wet-lab library, ACOR combined near-complete recovery with second-scale reconstruction of more than ten million reads. A smaller public benchmark was completed in a fraction of a second. Parallel ACOR achieved speedups of tens to over a thousand-fold relative to the tested serial baseline configurations. Sparse storage reduced high-context memory requirements, while parallel execution accelerated reconstruction computation. ACOR is designed for streaming readout pipelines that process assigned reads while sequencing continues.
