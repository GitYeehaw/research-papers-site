Honeycomb-Surfels: Compact Hexagonal Primitives for Efficient Scene Rendering and Reconstruction

Prakhar Kaushik, Soumava Paul, Alan Yuille

Novel view synthesis and 3D reconstruction have advanced rapidly with radiance fields and splatting, yet their memory and computational demands limit their use in resource-constrained settings. We introduce Hexels: novel planar hexagonal surface elements with a first-order spatial color field for efficient 3D scene and 2D image representation. Hexels exploit locally planar regions with smoothly varying appearance using fewer, larger primitives. Their hexagonal support provides an area- and boundary-efficient domain, while the local color field captures appearance variation within each primitive. Across 34 scenes, Hexels improve PSNR over memory-matched 2DGS by 4.53-7.38 dB across datasets while requiring lower training VRAM. We further ablate Hexels by showing that per primitive, Hexels outperform 2DGS by upto 4.74 dB while using lower training VRAM and having 2.31 times faster FPS. Even in unconstrained settings, Hexels offers complementary quality-memory trade-offs. For efficient 3D surface reconstruction, Hexels reduce error by 28.9% compared with memory-matched 2DGS across 15 DTU scans. On 6 Tanks and Temples surface scenes, Hexels achieves the highest mean F1 among compared methods (34.38%, versus 31.72% for 2DGS). We further also show that Hexels, as a general primitive, improves 2D image representation over Image-GS. Hexels push the Pareto frontier on the quality-efficiency trade-off of surface-aligned primitives.

ThinkWorld: World Imagination Reinforces Chain-of-Dynamics Reasoning

Chaehyun Kim, Chi-Pin Huang, Jisoo Kim, Min-Hung Chen, Yu-Chiang Frank Wang et al. (7 authors)

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models have made substantial progress in robotic manipulation, but their generalization remains constrained by the diversity and coverage of action-labeled robot demonstrations. Human videos provide much broader behavioral and environmental experience, while learning from them faces a fundamental trade-off between scalable data and explicit action grounding. We introduce ThinkWorld, a VLA learning framework that grounds action reasoning in world dynamics and strengthens it through reinforcement learning with world imagination. ThinkWorld represents each action thought as a Chain-of-Dynamics (CoD), a sequence of discrete motion tokens that captures the intermediate dynamics connecting the current world to a future state and serves as a common action representation across embodiments. We first initialize CoD with action-labeled demonstrations, then predict the future induced by each CoD and reinforce those whose predictions better match the observed outcome. This enables videos without action annotations to provide direct supervision from observed state transitions, allowing human and robot experience to refine the same reasoning space. Experiments in simulation and on real robots demonstrate effective human-to-robot transfer and strong generalization to unseen tasks and environments.

An EVM-Cosmos Bridge for Atomic, Semantic-Preserving Transfer of Environmental Tokens

Sebastian Pandolfi, Guzmán Llambías, Laura González

Transferring semantically typed tokens between heterogeneous blockchains (e.g. EVM and Cosmos ecosystems) raises two problems: semantic mixing, the loss of a token's domainspecific meaning in transit (e.g., CO2 removals issued under different methodologies), and atomicity despite divergent consensus and timing assumptions. We propose a bridge that addresses both. A deterministic indicator identity derived from cryptographic commitments, mirrored in class registries on both chains and checked at mint time, preventing semantic mixing by construction. For atomicity, we extend hash time-locked contracts (HTLCs) from two-party exchange to single-owner transfer with an automatic callback that burns the source escrow after a destination claim, and a protocol-minimum bounty that makes the fallback burn incentive-compatible even against adversarial senders. We machine-check the protocol's invariants with TLC (supply conservation fails without the rational-monitor assumption and holds with it) and validate a bidirectional prototype on Polygon and Neutron mainnets over Axelar GMP: fully automatic transfers with a median end-to-end latency of 97 s over 50 Polygon to Neutron transfers across 7 days (all completed by the on-chain automatic callback) and 93 s in the reverse direction, and semantic-validation overhead below 0.4% of messaging cost. We also document the integration failure modes of Cosmos originated messaging, whose dominant operational risk proves to be relay eligibility rather than latency.

Can Discrete Tokens Serve as Semantically Lossless Representations of Visual Signals?

Jialv Zou, Xiaotong Li, Zhengrong Yue, Xu Huang, Yaxin Luo et al. (9 authors)

Discrete visual tokens represent vision in a discrete form analogous to language, offering a native interface for multimodal modeling under a shared next-token prediction objective. However, quantization error is widely assumed to cause unavoidable semantic loss, fundamentally limiting the semantic fidelity of discrete visual tokens and positioning continuous visual representations as an intrinsic upper bound. Using multimodal understanding as a proxy for semantic fidelity, we revisit this assumption and find quantization error unreliable, as discrete representations with substantially larger errors can achieve better understanding. Instead, the effective bit budget of discrete indices emerges as a key determinant of downstream performance. However, existing quantizers eventually saturate as additional codebooks contribute progressively less semantic information. As one approach to alleviating this saturation, we introduce Factorized Vector Quantization, which quantizes complementary, fixed-dimensional views of the visual feature with symmetric parallel codebooks. It continues to improve at larger bit budgets, ultimately matching continuous visual representations in multimodal understanding. Trained on industrial-scale multimodal data, our model achieves state-of-the-art performance among discrete-representation models, outperforming UniAR and Emu3-Chat by 22.8% and 59.2%, respectively, on a nine-metric average. To our knowledge, it is also the first discrete-representation model competitive with strong continuous-representation models such as Qwen3-VL, including on fine-grained OCR tasks. We further identify the key principles and a practical recipe enabling this competitiveness. These results and insights support the potential of discrete visual representations to underpin large-scale native multimodal foundation models. Code and models are available at https://github.com/hustvl/FVQ

When Should We Intervene at Test Time?

Jiahao Zhang, Xinling Wen, Liangliang Li, Yu Chen

Test-time adaptation helps a deployed model respond to changing inputs, but the same intervention can rescue one batch and damage the next. Updating normalization statistics, for example, can improve corrupted images yet fail when a batch contains mostly one class. Average accuracy hides these failures and cannot tell a model when to use an intervention. Target labels can guide this decision, but they are scarce and some choices are already clear. We introduce PORTAL, a controller that chooses when to deploy an intervention, which inputs to label, and when to stop querying. Each available prediction rule is a candidate, and its predictions are fixed before current feedback arrives. Every acquired label therefore evaluates all candidates on the same input. PORTAL combines these shared comparisons and directs queries toward candidates that can still overtake the current leader. It stops when the remaining labels cannot change the choice or when the possible accuracy gain no longer justifies another query. Our analysis links the required feedback to accuracy gaps and shared mistakes, explaining why some choices need much less evidence. Experiments show that this control turns an intervention that loses accuracy overall into a useful choice on selected batches. It prevents severe failures caused by changes in arrival order and preserves gains across complete corruption streams. When a fixed rule already works well, PORTAL uses little feedback to retain its performance. Periodic label checks reveal when past evidence needs refreshing and help the controller recover as conditions change. The acquired labels also train stronger candidates for future batches, and selection brings nearly all of their accuracy gains into deployment. The same feedback therefore improves both the choice made now and the predictions available to later batches.

Heads of Security: Learned Attention Gates Improve Prompt Injection Robustness

Igor Shilov, Xiaoxue Yang, Alexander Panfilov, Evgenii Kortukov, Matthieu Meeus et al. (9 authors)

We introduce Heads of Security (HoS), a prompt injection defense that enforces instruction-data separation via a small set of learned per-head attention gates. HoS learns how each head should redistribute attention between trusted and untrusted tokens, enabling models to reason over untrusted content without following injected instructions. We evaluate HoS on Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct and GPT-OSS-20B. We compare it against SecAlign++, a state-of-the-art finetuning-based defense, and ASIDE, a defense that, like ours, builds instruction-data separation into the model architecture. We show that HoS offers greater robustness to adaptive attacks, better performance on standard utility benchmarks, and matches top performance on standard agentic prompt injection benchmarks. More specifically, the black-box RL-Hammer attack reaches 97% ASR against GPT-OSS-20B and 85% against SecAlign, but only 10% against HoS. Claudini, a state-of-the-art white-box attack, strengthened against HoS through autoresearch, requires 3 times more FLOPs to reach the same ASR on HoS as it does on GPT-OSS-20B or SecAlign. HoS preserves base-model utility across standard multiple-choice benchmarks and prompting setups, whereas SecAlign and ASIDE degrade in few-shot settings. On agentic benchmarks with static attacks, HoS and SecAlign perform similarly and substantially improve the security-utility trade-off over the base model. ASIDE struggles with tool use, achieving only 10% utility. These results show that prompt injection robustness can be achieved and improved through targeted attention interventions.

Attention as Lattice Gauge Theory: Relations Interact Only Through Wilson Loops

Aakash Sarkar, Ananya Mukherjee, Motoshige Sato

Relations compose through the entity they share - Alice is Bob's mother and Bob is Carol's father, so Alice is Carol's grandmother - and the order matters, since a father's mother is not a mother's father; verifying a composed relation means closing the loop. We show that if each entity's coordinate frame may be rotated independently (per-entity frame freedom), three things follow. In the pure-gauge sector, relations that share no cycle cannot interact at all. The only gauge-invariant score of a triangle is a class function of its holonomy $H = T_{ij} T_{jk} T_{ki}$ - a Wilson loop. And the only equivariant update of an edge is $U = Ψ(H)\, T_{ij}$ with $Ψ$ conjugation-equivariant. Edge attention by Wilson loops is therefore not one design among many but the unique form the symmetry permits. Three communities hold pieces of this form: the commuting-operator embeddings of knowledge graphs are its abelian (commuting) sector; AlphaFold's triangle update and the Edge Transformer have its shape - an edge updated through the third vertex - without its constraints; and lattice QCD builds it equivariantly on lattices. We instantiate the equivariant sub-family on general relational graphs as holonomy attention. With no training and composition alone, it infers unobserved $S_4$ relations on sparse graphs at 0.88 with a fifth of the edges corrupted, at or slightly below specialist synchronization algorithms, where every commutative architecture is capped at 0.083; its holonomy score flags corrupted triangles at AUC 0.99 where commuting scores are capped at 0.76 by a second selection rule, and it separates clusters without labels.

KPPax: preprocess differentiable kinetic ODE for machine learning in atmospheric chemistry

Wenqing Peng, Michael Boy, Zhi-Song Liu

Atmospheric chemical mechanisms are commonly distributed in the language of the Kinetic PreProcessor (KPP), which generates efficient Fortran or C code but does not fit into the differentiable programming frameworks in which machine-learning models are developed. We present KPPax, a kinetic preprocessor that generates differentiable, just-in-time compiled, and vectorisable chemistry code in JAX from mechanisms written in the KPP language. KPPax evaluates the rate law with sparse operations in log space, which keeps it differentiable, and integrates the stiff system with the implicit solvers and the forward- and reverse-mode adjoints of the \texttt{diffrax} library, so that exact sensitivities are obtained without hand-derived adjoint code. We demonstrate two applications with the POLLU benchmark mechanism. In the first, the Fisher information matrix computed from an automatically differentiated Jacobian predicts, before any fit, which of the 25 rate coefficients a flow-tube-like design can identify, and noisy synthetic estimation confirms the predicted uncertainties of the nine identifiable rate coefficients to within about 30\% while revealing clusters of fast reactions of which only ratios are constrained. In the second, a neural-network correction of an emission inventory is trained offline on per-scenario 4D-Var retrievals and then fine-tuned online through the chemical model, which reduces the error of the biogenic correction by a factor of 2.4--5 and recovers information on low-temperature emissions that the individual retrievals lose. KPPax makes KPP mechanisms directly usable for parameter estimation and hybrid physics--machine-learning modelling.

Pushing Dexterity and Safety for In-Hand Object Pose Reaching That Works Remarkably Well

Ian Chuang, Hiya Shah, Vamsi Chilamkur, Iman Soltani, Claire Tomlin

Dexterous manipulation with multi-fingered hands is the next frontier of robotics, yet learned hand skills remain limited to simple grasps or basic repeated motions, such as rotating an object. As robots attempt more demanding in-hand maneuvers, safety, such as not dropping the object, becomes critical, yet it is rarely measured. We study in-hand object pose reaching, a task that demands diverse and difficult grasps: moving an object to almost any position and orientation within reach of the fingers, at any wrist orientation, through long sequences of consecutive goals. We present a recipe for training such policies for a LEAP hand in simulation without human data, built on a range of insights, from diverse resets and goals via generated grasp datasets to an adaptive friction curriculum. With careful system identification, the policies transfer zero-shot to the real hand, using either motion capture or a single wrist-mounted depth camera, and prove remarkably capable and robust. On top of the task policy, we add a learned residual safety filter with adjustable conservativeness, letting us tune the balance between performance and safety and greatly reduce object drops. In 13.3 hours of real-robot evaluation over 4,800 unseen goals across 4 objects and diverse wrist orientations, the state-based policy reaches 95.2% of goals within 2 cm and 0.4 rad, and the filter cuts its drops by 75% while still reaching 91.5% of goals. The policies can also spin a lightbulb held in an arbitrary pose, reach out-of-distribution goals, withstand fast arm motion and perturbations, and, needing only a wrist camera, serve as portable skills such as screwing in a lightbulb. We release the full recipe as a platform for studying performance, reliability, and safety in high-dimensional dexterous systems. Videos and more are available at https://ian-chuang.github.io/dexsafe/.

GEPA-Curator: Curated Learning for Self Improving Agents

Zack Yu, Nicholas Lee, Minjae Lee, Lakshya Agarwal, Luca Manolache et al. (11 authors)

Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly used in multi-turn agentic workloads that require sustained interaction with complex environments. Existing reflective prompt optimizers such as GEPA learn from failed rollouts, but agentic failures can also come from prompt weaknesses, harness limitations, stochastic execution, or noisy judges. We argue that such failures may not provide useful learning signal, and may even be harmful; and thus it is essential to curate which tasks are provided to the prompt optimizer for better performance. We introduce GEPA-Curator, a framework that augments the prompt optimizer to select which agent experiences should shape prompt optimization. In particular, GEPA-Curator consists of two components: task curation, which filters out tasks that are likely to provide noisy or unreliable learning signals, and prior success retrieval, which selectively retrieves past successful trajectories for a task and provides them as additional context to the prompt optimizer. We evaluate GEPA-Curator on GoBrowse, ALFWorld, AppWorld, and $τ$-bench, using both Gemini 3.1 Pro and Claude Opus 5 as optimizer models. GEPA-Curator matches or exceeds the mean performance of both baselines, with gains of up to 20.7% over GEPA and 21.2% over ACE.
