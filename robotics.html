Blind Precise Assembly: Proprioceptive Sim-to-Real Peg-in-Hole for Diverse Parts

Antonia Bronars, Younghyo Park, Kushal Kedia, Pulkit Agrawal

Every robot already carries high-resolution contact sensors: its own joints. Legged robots and humanoids exploit them to walk, run, and track whole-body motions without vision, but there the contacts are large and forceful. Is joint sensing also enough for precise, contact-rich assembly, where the robot must find a millimeter-scale opening, mesh gear teeth, or catch a thread through light contact? We study this question by training blind assembly policies that observe only a short history of joint positions, velocities, and their own actions, without visual, force, torque, or tactile sensing. The poses of both the fixture and the part are unknown to the policy, and the grasp is randomized substantially. The policy must therefore assemble the parts through contact, sensed exclusively through its own proprioception. Trained with reinforcement learning entirely in simulation over randomized parts, grasps, and contacts, such policies learn peg insertion, gear meshing, and nut threading. The peg insertion policy transfers zero-shot to the real world and inserts pegs of different shapes and sizes in 73% of trials. Controlled comparisons with matched visual policies in simulation show that blind policies generalize far better to unseen part shapes and sizes, though they can be slower. Training such policies needs thousands of different assemblies, each simulated with its clearance intact, so we also release an open-source generator that builds them procedurally from exact convex pieces for MuJoCo; it is more accurate and faster than convex decomposition or MuJoCo's signed-distance collision. Videos and interactive simulations are at https://younghyopark.me/blind-proprioceptive-assembly/.

StopWatch: Goal Grounding for Object-goal Navigation

Xinhao Liu, Yunsheng Tian, Yuheng Qiu, Pieter Abbeel, Xi Chen et al. (7 authors)

Pretrained VLMs bring rich world knowledge and semantic priors to object-goal navigation, and fine-tuning a VLM into a navigation policy has become a common recipe. Despite this progress, success rates remain low, and it is unclear which capability these fine-tuned policies lack. Our diagnosis identifies goal grounding as the dominant failure. These policies explore well, yet often fail to notice that the goal is already in view or to estimate how far away it is. To address this failure, we ground the goal explicitly. Explicit grounding substantially improves success and reaches state-of-the-art results on six benchmarks across three scene collections. We also correct annotation defects in these benchmarks, so that future methods are evaluated more faithfully. Our analysis on the clean benchmarks shows that, with ground-truth grounding, our policy nearly matches the expert that generated its demonstrations, leaving the headroom in perception rather than policy learning. Our work points to goal grounding as a missing piece of object-goal navigation and offers a simple and effective solution to the task.

SparseMax-dSTL: Sound Robustness with Horizon-Independent Gradient Allocation for Temporal Logic Trajectory Optimization

Sai Sandeep Damera, Ryan Matheu, John S. Baras, Calin Belta

A gradient-based solver that enforces a Signal Temporal Logic (STL) specification uses a smooth approximation of its quantitative semantics, the robustness. In safety-critical trajectory optimization, this approximation must have two properties. First, it must be sound, that is, nonnegative only on trajectories that satisfy the specification. Second, at each temporal operator, the weight of its gradient on the violating samples must stay bounded away from zero, however long the horizon. The approximations in use, including the log-sum-exp, its sound variant, and the generalized-mean robustness, do not have both. We introduce lower approximations of the minimum and the maximum whose gradients are sparsemax projections, and we prove that the robustness built from them has both. It lies below the exact robustness and is therefore sound, and its error has a bound that is independent of the number of samples and known before optimization. The gradient of each approximation is exactly zero on every sample beyond a data-dependent threshold, so the weight on violating samples a gap beyond the others has a positive lower bound at every horizon. On a planar unicycle and on a seven-joint manipulator beside a person in simulation, the log-sum-exp approximation is positive on a violating trajectory. As the horizon of the manipulator's task grows from 10 to 80 s, the gradient weight of the sound variant on the violating samples falls from 0.22 to 0.04, while that of sparsemax stays at 1. In a constrained trajectory optimization of the unicycle, the constraint on the log-sum-exp robustness returns a violating trajectory, whereas the constraint on the sparsemax robustness returns a trajectory that satisfies the specification with the required margin.

Self-Building Labs

Jiaru Bai, Yuchi Zhao, Han Hao, Miroslav Bogdanovic, Yang Cao et al. (7 authors)

Self-driving laboratories (SDLs) accelerate scientific discovery by automating experimental execution and optimization. AI agents can increasingly propose new experiments, but existing SDLs can only run the procedures they were built for. Adapting them to a new experiment still takes extensive manual engineering, which makes laboratory flexibility the bottleneck for scaling discovery. We introduce the self-building laboratory (SBL), which designs, configures, and operates its own laboratory setup given a high-level scientific goal. SBL starts from whatever already exists, from an empty room to a working SDL, and builds the missing capabilities: the lab layout, its digital twin, robot skills, and experimental workflows. We demonstrate the system working across different robots and setups, scaling from a single workstation to laboratories with several robots working together. We validate SBL's end-to-end capabilities through a real-world quantum-dot synthesis experiment. By shifting the role of human researchers from manually configuring and programming device automation to specifying scientific objectives, SBL makes scientific automation more accessible, flexible, and scalable. Project website: https://self-building-labs.github.io/

MOSS: Multimodal Origami Swamp Robot System

T. Lan, A. Babgei, L. Romanello, M. Kovac, S. F. Armanini

Robotic systems for environmental monitoring and ecological surveying in wetlands and swamps must operate across diverse terrestrial and aquatic conditions. Conventional fixed-geometry wheels cannot adapt their contact geometry to these varying conditions. This work presents MOSS (Multimodal Origami Swamp Robot System), a compact amphibious robotic platform with 3D-printed origami wheels. The compact crank mechanism switches the wheel between contracted and expanded configurations, changing its width, body diameter, and fin orientation simultaneously. The contracted configuration is better suited to efficient rolling on firmer ground, whereas the expanded configuration provides greater contact and terrain engagement on softer surfaces. MOSS was evaluated on soil, sand, mud with varying water content, and water, in addition to in-motion morphing and terrestrial-aquatic transition tests. The more energy-efficient wheel configuration depended on terrain. The contracted configuration achieved a lower cost of transport on firmer soil and mud, whereas the expanded configuration performed better on wetter mud, sand, and water, reducing the cost of transport by up to 29%. Expanded wheels also reduced slip in wet mud and reduced both slip and hub sinkage in sand. MOSS successfully completed configuration changes in $1.95 \pm 0.11$ s and demonstrated continuous locomotion across soil-mud-water transitions. Outdoor trials further demonstrated operation across natural deformable terrain and water. These results show that adaptive wheel geometry provides terrain-dependent locomotion benefits and enables a single compact platform to traverse substantially different terrestrial and aquatic conditions.

Lyapunov Exponents in Power Systems: A Survey of Theory and Applications in Stability, Control, Forecasting, and Protection

Mohammad Almomani, Venkataramana Ajjarapu

Lyapunov exponents (LEs) are fundamental metrics from nonlinear dynamical systems theory that quantify the average exponential rate of divergence or convergence of nearby trajectories in phase space. Over the past three decades, LEs have emerged as powerful tools across a wide range of power system problems, including transient and voltage stability assessment, chaos detection and mitigation, short-term load and renewable energy forecasting, and fault diagnosis. This paper presents a comprehensive review of these applications, covering more than 165 publications spanning from the early demonstrations of chaos in simplified machine models (1990s) to the most recent data-driven and machine-learning-augmented approaches applied to inverter-based resource (IBR)-dominated grids (2024--2026). The review is organized into four principal application domains: (i) stability analysis and real-time monitoring, (ii) chaos characterization and control, (iii) forecasting and predictability analysis, and (iv) protection, fault diagnosis, and condition monitoring. For each domain, we trace the evolution of methods, compare model-based and data-driven LE estimation strategies, identify key benchmarks and validation gaps, and outline the most pressing open research problems.

Parametric Resonance in Primal-Dual Learning for Slowly Non-Stationary Constrained MDPs

Maxime Baelde

Primal-dual methods for constrained Markov decision processes are analyzed under a two-timescale assumption, with non-stationarity entering through two channels. In the additive channel, where the constraint level oscillates, the dual oscillation saturates at a floor set by the moving optimum and is amplified linearly by the timescale ratio. In the parametric channel, where the constraint sensitivity oscillates, a coherent modulation carries no resonant forcing at any depth: the dual update's stiffness modulation cancels the primal update's damping modulation. Instability instead comes from a mismatch between the constraint estimate used by the primal update and the measurement used by the dual update, producing a parametric resonance at twice the pair's natural frequency. The tolerated mismatch grows with the square root of the forcing bandwidth and collapses with the square root of the dual-to-primal step-size ratio. These results hold on the tangent linear-quadratic reduction around an active saddle, for one constraint and for $K$ constraints under a common modulation and an isotropy condition. Driving both updates from the same estimate removes the resonant forcing but introduces a stationary offset equal to the estimator's bias. In a Lagrangian soft actor-critic agent, the multiplier locks onto the sub-harmonic at the timescale ratios its identified linear loop predicts and a coherent cost critic suppresses the lock-in, while a proportional (PID) term, predicted to leave the lock-in in place, suppresses most of it through the projection of the multiplier.

Not All Actions Contribute Equally: Learning Precise Progress Estimation from Imprecise Demonstrations

Huanqian Wang, Boshi Tang, Botong Xiao, Jie Zhang, Yang Yue et al. (8 authors)

Progress estimators play an essential role in providing dense reward signals for reinforcement learning in robotic manipulation, but accurately assessing task progress can be much challenging. Existing methods heavily rely on expert data and naively assume that all actions within a demonstration contribute to task success. This assumption often breaks down in real-world scenarios, especially in high-precision manipulation, where subtle geometric errors can lead to task failure. Therefore, these methods can erroneously interpret human mistakes as meaningful progress, further corrupting progress learning. We argue that a key limitation is the lack of a global prior regarding task evaluation criteria, which is crucial for distinguishing meaningful progress from human mistakes. To this end, we propose PRIME, a novel progress estimator capable of learning precise progress estimation from imprecise demonstrations by aggregating experience across trajectories. PRIME constructs a weighted graph over observed states, connecting states across trajectories based on spatial proximity and visual consistency. Edge weights encode spatial distances as transition costs, and shortest-path search approximates the geodesic distance to success. Changes in this distance naturally distinguish advancement, stagnation, and setbacks without assuming monotonic progress over time. Extensive experiments in both simulation and real world on precise manipulation tasks show that PRIME is consistently effective across diverse tasks, and robust to data with varying degrees of suboptimality. Notably, the advantage of PRIME gets more pronounced in higher-precision scenarios, yielding up to a \(5\times\) improvement in policy success rate over the SFT baselines on charger insertion.

Anytime-Valid Certification of Robustness Margins for Safe Control via E-Values

Panagiotis S. Trakas, Omid Mirzaeedodangeh, Lars Lindemann

Robust control uses the error between a known model and the true system as a robustness margin. However, this error is usually unknown: conservative estimates restrict feasibility and performance, while underestimates can result in violations of the control property. We estimate this robustness margin from online data using e-values, a statistical tool for hypothesis testing, in the setting of discrete-time control barrier functions (CBFs). Here, we measure the robustness margin as the difference between the observed CBF and the model-based CBF prediction. We then design a robust CBF controller in three steps by: (1) selecting a set of candidate margins, (2) obtaining validity certificates for these candidates via sequential tests based on e-values, and (3) updating the CBF controller with the smallest certified candidate. The deployed tests accumulate evidence against the hypothesis that an exceedance risk, the probability of the robustness margin exceeding the candidate margin, remains at or above a pre-specified failure probability $α$. Our tests are anytime valid and thus enable the use of certified candidates in the CBF controller, guaranteeing that the probability that the exceedance risk of a certified margin exceeds $α$ is at most a user-chosen confidence $δ$. We validate our approach on navigation problems across different environments with visualizations in NVIDIA Isaac Sim.

MIXSim: A multiclass first order traffic model with composition-dependent dynamics for mixed-autonomy urban networks

Muhammad Haris, Claudio Roncoli

This paper presents a novel multiclass first order macroscopic traffic model for mixed-autonomy in signalized urban networks, called MIXSim. Human driven vehicles (HDVs) and Connected and Automated Vehicles (CAVs) are represented as separate classes while origin-destination information is retained through commodity specific states. The local vehicle class composition and class specific driving behaviour characteristics determine the parameters of a composition-dependent triangular fundamental diagram. At signalized intersections, the composition of movement demand additionally determines the saturation flow rate, while the realized movement flow is constrained by demand, effective green time, and downstream receiving capacity. Numerical experiments investigate different CAV penetration levels and CAV time gaps, together with alternative signal control strategies. The numerical experiments illustrate how changes in CAV penetration and CAV time gap affect network performance under the tested conditions, and demonstrate the use of MIXSim for comparing signal control strategies across different mixed traffic regimes.
