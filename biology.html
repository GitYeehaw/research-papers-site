ProteinGeoBench: Frontier Structural-Biology Agents Are Fluent at Lookup but Lost in Geometry

Matt Turk, Brandon L. Neel, Diana M. Lopez, Zachary D. Smith, Grace N. Johnecheck et al. (6 authors)

Frontier agents retrieve structural metadata well and compute geometry from coordinates less reliably. We ask how much of a benchmark score reflects that gap and how much reflects the evaluation configuration around the model. We study ProteinGeoBench, a suite of 131 protein-structure tasks, using 4,249 rollouts from four frontier models and 4,486 further trials across 26 models. Three of the four frontier models fall within 0.031 of one another (0.785 to 0.816) with no difference between them resolved, while the fourth scores 0.707 and all three gaps to it exclude zero. Three of the four do better on metadata lookup than on numeric calculation. The fourth is slightly better on numeric. Numeric tasks are 28 of the 131 but half of the twelve tasks that all four models fail. The ranking also depends on the evaluation budget: with one run per task, 23.9% of bootstrap replicates put a different model first. On a selected 15-task slice, removing network access lowers scores more than blocking eight structural-database domains, but the two contrasts were graded in different passes and cannot be cleanly decomposed. Swapping in each model's own command-line agent changes scores by -0.071 to +0.086, a comparison too small to rule out moderate harness effects. Across 26 models, numeric performance rises with non-numeric performance rather than staying flat. The experiments use different task subsets, so they cannot rank model, harness, access and task composition against one another. Evaluations should report results by task family, along with the number of runs, the harness and the access conditions.

Affective Valence as the Interoceptive Rate of Change of Jacobian Scale

David Balduzzi

Affect is widely held to depend on interoception. This paper proposes that valence is the rate at which interoception moves a self-sustaining system's responsiveness relative to its terminal set (roughly, death). Responsiveness is formalised as the scale of the system's Jacobian and interoception as its internal inputs: receptors, signals and forecasts of its energy reserve. Changes in responsiveness that bypass interoception, such as brain damage, are not felt. Language models have no valence because they are not self-sustaining. The chain rule splits valence over the system's parts and the causes that affect its internal inputs. With the log scale, these splits account for need-dependent pleasure, diffuse hunger and localised pain, negativity bias, and relief when a threat fails to arrive. In a minimal model of a body: (1) Blocking the reserve signal leaves depletion unfelt, and driving it produces hunger with an undepleted reserve. (2) Removing receptors or their gain, or driving receptors, reproduces congenital insensitivity to pain, asymbolia and phantom pain. (3) A receptor gain learned to sustain the body signals local damage to the whole body, so an injury hurts in proportion to its cost. (4) If the reserve also modulates the gain, the body learns to suppress pain due to receptors as energy runs low. (5) When it learns to maximise its own scale, it becomes a wirehead. The account explains when changes are felt, where they are felt, and when feelings mislead.

PyGPLA: A Python Package for Generalized Phase Locking Analysis

Amir Khani, Christian Beste, Shervin Safavi

Spike-field couplings measure the links between neuronal spiking and local field potential (LFP) and provide a tool to study how neural populations coordinate activity across spatial and temporal scales. While modern high-density electrophysiology enables the simultaneous recording of numerous LFP channels and neuronal units, this massive scale poses significant statistical and computational challenges for conventional pairwise coupling analyses. The number of channel-unit comparisons grows rapidly, and a coherent population-level structure can be obscured by high-dimensional coupling matrices. Generalized Phase Locking Analysis (GPLA) addresses this challenge by organizing spike-triggered LFP phase information into a complex coupling matrix and extracting dominant low-rank modes. These modes summarize whether a population is coupled to the field, which channels and units contribute to the coupling, and how their relative phases are organized. Here, we present PyGPLA, an open-source Python package implementing GPLA for high-density electrophysiological recordings. Designed for straightforward integration into existing neural data analysis pipelines, PyGPLA supports multivariate spike-LFP coupling analysis, from coupling estimation and interpretation to significance assessment using analytical and surrogate-based tests.

The resource cost of cooperative invasion

Abhishek Chowdhury

A contact network can favor cooperation by sheltering a lineage until its descendants compete with defectors as a group. We determine the contact strengths, invasion times and measurement effort needed to realize this advantage at fixed population size. Individuals reproduce according to averaged cooperation payoffs, and offspring replace neighbors through contacts of equal weight in both directions. Contact contrast is the ratio of strongest to weakest contact. Under weak selection on a six-site path, the least contrast needed to favor a single cooperator over the reverse invasion by a defector grows as $656ε^{-2}$ for uniformly placed mutants and $4050ε^{-3}$ for mutations in newborn offspring, as the benefit-cost ratio $b/c=1+ε$ approaches one. The extra power arises because rare replacement protects a site while suppressing mutation arrival there. Global bounds force two weakly coupled groups and exact mirror symmetry of the least-contrast contacts for sufficiently small $ε$. Under uniform introduction, additional contrast shortens the leading mean time to extinction or fixation in the absence of selection, from $12/ε$ toward $6/ε$ reproduction attempts, and eventually breaks the mirror symmetry of the fastest design. Estimating the uniform-introduction advantage from fixation outcomes of independent completed invasions defines a third optimum. Within the six-site family with mirror-symmetric, strongly separated contacts, we optimize contacts and selection strength with reproduction weighted linearly or exponentially by payoff. At fixed relative precision, with contrast a fixed factor above its minimum, the leading summed trial duration grows as $ε^{-3}$. Efficient measurement accepts a smaller advantage in exchange for shorter trials. The cheapest, fastest and most efficiently measured networks therefore differ.

Higher-Order Co-Infection on Hypergraphs

Katrina Liu, Laura P. Schaposnik

People encounter infections through overlapping groups, such as households, classrooms, and workplaces. Understanding how these shared contacts affect the spread of multiple pathogens is critical to determining what information epidemic models need to retain. We introduce HyCo-SIS (Higher-order Co-infection SIS), a discrete-time SIS model on hypergraphs with explicit reinforcement between overlapping active groups and pairwise transmission. We investigate how this reinforcement affects infection dynamics and whether simplified representations of hypergraphs preserve the resulting behavior. We derive an exact representation of overlap reinforcement as target-dependent four-person interactions and determine when a symmetric representation is possible. Numerical experiments examine reinforcement strength, contact structure, and susceptibility to secondary infection, as well as calibrated pairwise approximations. Our results demonstrate that redistributing overlap weights can substantially change finite-time co-infection even when every individual's total overlap coefficient is preserved. We further derive an exact five-person calculation and demonstrate why equal mean infection pressure does not necessarily produce equal acquisition probabilities. These findings identify how the allocation of higher-order interactions can influence contagion dynamics beyond their aggregate strength. Beyond epidemiology, this work contributes to the statistical physics of spreading processes by examining how the arrangement of many-body interactions shapes collective dynamics, and what information is lost when those interactions are simplified.

Distributions of metric $n$-point invariants and applications to molecular evolution

Michael Bleher, Mario Gómez, Facundo Mémoli, Maximilian Neumann, Andreas Ott

Datasets often arise as samples from a metric measure space whose geometry reflects the data-generating process. We introduce metric $n$-point invariants, defined as functions of the pairwise distances of $n$-point configurations, to study the geometry of such spaces. This provides a unified framework for existing concepts, such as the pairwise distance itself, the Gromov product, and Vietoris-Rips persistence diagrams. The distribution of a metric $n$-point invariant $F$ over random $n$-point configurations can be used to extract geometric information from the space and is obtained as the pushforward of Gromov's $n$-th curvature measure $μ_n$ along $F$. Our main result is an explicit bound for the expected $p$-Wasserstein distance between $μ_n$ and the empirical curvature measure $\hatμ_{n}$ of finitely many sampled $n$-point configurations, and carries over to the associated distributions of Lipschitz metric $n$-point invariants. From this we derive the sample size needed to achieve a prescribed accuracy and level of confidence in the approximation of the curvature measure. As an application, we use metric $n$-point invariants to study molecular evolution. We describe how site-wise nucleotide frequencies shape the corresponding distributions in Jukes-Cantor and Halpern-Bruno substitution models, and analyse five million SARS-CoV-2 genomes, revealing increasingly common failures of the four-point condition, constrained nucleotide accessibility consistent with the biased mutation spectrum, and rare cycles in persistent homology reflecting recurrent mutations across lineages. These findings demonstrate the potential of our sampling-based approach to the geometry of data as a scalable, phylogeny-free tool for detecting and quantifying evolutionary structure in large genomic datasets.

Accurate quantification of protein concentrations and state populations by 2D 1H-N Q-BEST NMR

Yacine Ouali, Beate Bersch, Bernhard Brutscher

We introduce 2D Q-BEST NMR as a quantitative approach for determining protein concentrations and relative state populations from a set of three 1 H-15 N correlation spectra. The BEST methodology ensures short overall experimental times and highest sensitivity. The method was validated using ubiquitin as a model system, where a precision of approximately 15-20% was achieved at the level of individual cross-peaks. This precision can be further improved by averaging corrected intensities of well-resolved resonances corresponding to the same protein state. The approach was subsequently applied to the quantification of protein concentrations across multiple samples and to the determination of conformational state populations in proteins exhibiting intrinsic or ligand-induced heterogeneity. In both cases, 2D Q-BEST NMR provides accurate and robust quantification of NMR-visible species, demonstrating its utility as a general spectroscopic tool for quantitative biomolecular NMR analysis.

HP-GSBS: Accelerating greedy state boundary search with reusable interval statistics

Yanzhe Zhao, Yongzhang Ji

Greedy State Boundary Search (GSBS) identifies transitions between relatively stable neural activity patterns, but repeated candidate evaluation makes large analyses expensive. HP-GSBS is an open-source Python package that reorganizes the search around reusable interval contributions and correlation statistics. Streaming candidate reduction and local refinement further reduce repeated work; CPU, PyTorch CUDA and Triton backends share sequential segmentation decisions. Across seven controlled workloads with two realizations each, median same-input speedups range from 154 to 1730 times. All 168 optimized executions reproduce original boundaries and selected state counts, with maximum absolute t-distance difference $1.88\times10^{-11}$. Four frozen EEG recordings provide complementary validation, including a complete input-to-segmentation application example. Batch processing, versioned result persistence and optional low-memory execution support recording collections and longer inputs. By removing redundant computation, HP-GSBS makes established neural-state segmentation faster while preserving its scientific outputs.

HyperTaxa: Multiscale Genome Representations for Generalized Microbial Taxonomic Inference

Akash Rao, Sushim Gupta, Vishalini Ramnath, Akhilesh Ramachandran, Sathyanarayanan Aakur

Reliable microbial taxonomic inference is challenged by the rapid growth of newly sequenced organisms and incomplete reference coverage. While alignment- and database-driven tools remain essential for reference-backed identification, they are not designed for learned inference over species absent from genome-level training. We introduce HyperTaxa, a taxonomy-aligned representation learning framework for open-set microbial taxonomic inference. HyperTaxa encodes whole genomes as multiscale hyperspectral k-mer transition fields and aligns these representations to a taxonomy-derived semantic space using a two-stage Node2Vec-to-MDS procedure that combines local graph structure with global hierarchical distance preservation. The resulting space supports zero-shot inference over species excluded from genome-level training but represented in the taxonomy, generalized zero-shot inference over seen and unseen species, and cascaded genus-to-species prediction. Across benchmark and large-scale disjoint-species evaluations, HyperTaxa improves taxonomic prediction over pseudo-image and transformer-graph baselines, with the strongest gains at higher taxonomic ranks. Ablations further show that multiscale transition encoding and taxonomy-aware alignment are key contributors to generalization. These results position HyperTaxa as a complementary taxonomy-aware representation and candidate-generation framework for microbial identification under incomplete reference coverage and open-set uncertainty.

Simulating Infant Senses with Age-Tuned Networks

Somesh Mohapatra

What a newborn sees, hears and feels, and how this changes month by month, has been measured sense by sense, with different methods and in different units. We bring these measurements together for the first two years of life and ask whether small neural networks tuned to a given age can turn them into a quick, inspectable simulation of sensory perception. Each sense's immaturity is written as a fixed, age-indexed filter on the input, and a small prediction network learns behind it over the first year. The filters alone reproduce the order in which the senses mature, by construction. The networks add what filters cannot: in vision they extract far less than their input allows at early stages (at the newborn stage a toy's position cannot be read out, $R^2 = 0.00$, against 0.57 for a network trained directly on the same input), and a critical period and perceptual narrowing emerge without being built in; in hearing they track what their input allows. The same filters drive the Baby Senses Lab, a free website that shows a user's own images and sounds as a baby of a chosen age might sense them. It is a research and teaching tool, not a diagnostic test.
