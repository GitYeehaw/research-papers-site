$Z$-sight: Unifying Vision-Language-Action and Latent World Modeling

Junjie Ye, Yiqi Zhao, Muchen Xu, Celina Shiyu Wang, Hao Dong et al. (15 authors)

We introduce $Z$-sight, a unified robot foundation model that couples vision-language-action (VLA) and generative latent world modeling. Video-based world action models (WAMs) show the value of joint future-and-action generation for control, yet inherit the heavy cost of video generators. Recent latent-space policies avoid pixel generation, yet typically align intermediate features or rely on frozen target encoders, decoupling future prediction from the VLM's native feature space. $Z$-sight instead predicts the future directly in the visual token space in which its VLM perceives the present. Coupling a foresight expert and a continuous action expert with the VLM in a mixture-of-transformers, $Z$-sight jointly denoises future spatial tokens and actions at inference without pixel reconstruction or external encoders. Trained on human video and robot trajectories, $Z$-sight achieves 81.9% success on SIMPLE (+9.6 points over the strongest baseline) and 73.3% across six Unitree G1 tasks (+20.0 points) at 110 ms per chunk, while remaining competitive on tabletop benchmarks.

Phoenix: Learning Scene Structure Representations for Generalizable World-Action Models

Yupeng Zheng, Chaoyue Li, Linbo Wang, Xiang Li, Yuhang Zheng et al. (18 authors)

World-action models jointly predict future observations and actions, yet reconstruction-oriented visual representations do not explicitly emphasize the spatial relationships and object-state changes needed for manipulation. We introduce Phoenix, which learns representations of scene structure for world-action modeling. Specifically, Phoenix Encoder enriches DINOv3's general visual semantics through dual-teacher distillation of geometric and spatiotemporal semantic knowledge. Next, CS-VAE causally aggregates and compresses Phoenix Encoder features into generative latents while preserving scene information and spatial layout. Finally, Phoenix jointly predicts these latents, complementary appearance latents, and robot actions, linking scene evolution with action generation. To study pretraining data efficiency, we curate approximately 39,000 hours of egocentric and robot manipulation data for representation learning and use the robot demonstration subset for WAM pretraining. Following benchmark-specific post-training, Phoenix achieves state-of-the-art performance across simulation and real-robot benchmarks spanning single-arm, bimanual, and dexterous manipulation. It demonstrates strong robustness to perturbations, achieving success rates of 94.8% on LIBERO-Plus and 72.4% on RoboTwin 2.0 C2R, alongside strong cross-embodiment transfer and real-world manipulation performance on Bench2Dex and RoboDojo-RealWorld, respectively. Pretraining-scale analysis shows larger performance gains from data scaling than a video-VAE-only baseline, indicating stronger scaling benefits and more effective data utilization. Moreover, Phoenix maintains high success rates when adapted with limited downstream demonstrations, demonstrating strong few-shot adaptation. Our code, pretraining data, and models are available on the project page to help the community reproduce our results.

Grana: Compositional Reward Modeling for Long-Horizon Robot Manipulation

Zi-ang Cao, Kaiyuan Zheng, Yinzhen Xu, Haonan Duan, Jiasheng Gu et al. (17 authors)

Robot reward modeling faces two challenges: generalizing across tasks and accurately tracking progress in long-horizon manipulation. General-purpose reward models demonstrate broad task transfer, but transfer from short-task pretraining to long-horizon tasks remains underexplored. Long-horizon progress models often rely on task-specific subtask classifiers or fixed action vocabularies, limiting the reuse of learned progress knowledge when adapting to new tasks. To address these challenges, we introduce Grana, a vision-language robot reward model that learns to identify unfinished subtasks and estimate their continuous progress using open-vocabulary subtask descriptions. We train a language-conditioned model to estimate progress in short-horizon tasks and individual subtasks of long-horizon tasks. For long-horizon tasks, we combine the selected subtask's predicted progress with credit for preceding completed subtasks to estimate whole-task progress. General progress prediction requires training the model across diverse tasks with progress supervision that accounts for imperfect execution. Elapsed-time proxies can misrepresent progress when relative subtask durations vary, or failures reverse local progress. We therefore introduce an annotation protocol that converts sparse subtask boundaries and action-quality annotations into dense progress targets, without requiring annotators to assign numerical progress scores. Pretraining with this supervision on 200 diverse, mostly short-horizon manipulation tasks enables zero-shot reward prediction on unseen tasks and improves progress prediction after adaptation to multi-minute, long-horizon tasks. Experiments demonstrate (i) generalizable reward prediction, including adaptation to five long-horizon tasks, (ii) benefits from pretraining and composing subtask progress estimates, and (iii) improved downstream policy performance.

Render the Robot, Generate the World: One Video World Model Across Embodiments

Yash Jangir, Karan Mirakhor, Renos Zabounidis, Muhammad Zubair Irshad, Sergey Zakharov et al. (7 authors)

Predicting the consequences of an agent's actions requires modeling both the body's motion and the environment's response. Most video world models attempt to learn both from numerical actions, even though geometry and kinematics already specify the intended motion. We introduce Doppel, an embodiment-agnostic video world model conditioned on camera-aligned renderings of future body configurations and numerical action trajectories. By explicitly providing the intended motion, Doppel can focus on predicting object motion and interaction-driven changes in the environment. Rendered motion also provides a common visual interface across different embodiments and action spaces. To train on robot datasets without camera extrinsics, we recover camera poses and align rendered trajectories with recorded observations. We jointly train a single shared video backbone and conditioning adapter on eight datasets spanning eight robot and human embodiments, from single- and bimanual robot arms to a dexterous humanoid and human hands and bodies, using about 600 hours of video without embodiment labels. Across three robot-arm datasets, Doppel improves PSNR over action-only baselines by 1.00 dB on held-out trajectories and 1.20 dB under out-of-distribution evaluation, while predicting more consistent object motion. Training on all eight embodiments (Doppel-8) improves robot predictions over the three-robot model (Doppel-3) on four of six evaluation sets. Project page: https://doppelwm.github.io/.

AMISS: An Integrity Framework for Magnetic Navigation

Sharad C. Shankar, Prasenjit Sengupta, J. Scott Clawson, Sam Pullen

Magnetic Navigation (MagNav) is a promising alternative to traditional systems like Global Navigation Satellite Systems (GNSS), offering resistance to jamming or spoofing attacks to which the latter are vulnerable. MagNav matches scalar magnetic measurements over time against a known map of Earth's magnetic anomaly in order to position a vehicle. MagNav is therefore sensitive to the quality of these maps, especially to errors in the survey data that the maps are derived from. In order to be a reliable and mature navigation technology, MagNav must satisfy integrity requirements for mitigation of such faults. To this end, a solution-separation monitoring algorithm is developed that performs a role equivalent to Advanced Receiver Autonomous Integrity Monitoring (ARAIM) in GNSS, which detects faults as well as generates worst-case bounds (protection levels) on estimation error for faults too small to be detected. It is shown how these integrity methods can be developed in a windowed-in-time sense, where subsets of measurements over a time window are compared with each other in order to detect outliers that can potentially result from errors in the map. The applicability of the approach is demonstrated using results obtained using synthetic aircraft data with injected faults.

Teleoperated Haircutting with Delay Compensation and Passivity-Based Control for Unilateral Internet Teleoperation under Real Communication Uncertainties

Viridiana Luna-Zempoalteca, Zhendai Huang, Rosalba Galvan-Guerra, Antonio Rosales, Xiaoli Liu et al. (6 authors)

Robotic haircutting requires accurate motion execution and reliable communication to safely reproduce the operator's commands. Internet-based teleoperation is a key enabling technology to remotely perform precise manipulation tasks such as haircutting. However, communication uncertainties, including variable transmission delays and packet jitter, degrade synchronization between the master and slave systems and affect tracking performance. This paper presents a unilateral Internet teleoperation framework for robotic haircutting that takes into account experimentally measured communication uncertainties. Three delay-compensation techniques, namely Zero-Order Hold (ZOH), First-Order Hold (FOH), and Low-Pass Filtering (LPF), are evaluated together with a classical proportional-derivative (PD) controller and a passivity-based synchronization controller. Unlike conventional studies based on synthetic delay models, the proposed framework is validated using communication data acquired from a real cloud-based teleoperation platform, where both packet delay and effective delay are characterized. The results demonstrate that combining delay-compensation techniques with passivity-based synchronization improves the robustness of unilateral Internet teleoperation under realistic communication uncertainties, providing a reliable control framework for robotic haircutting applications.

Local AI Agents in Power Electronics Simulation: Deployment, Measurements and Costs

Yuzhuo Li, Pasan Gunawardena, Yunwei Li

Agentic artificial intelligence (AI) is emerging in power electronics, where it drives the simulation loop that engineers used to close largely by hand. In this loop, a converter design, or a study of the network it connects to, is built, run, measured and revised until it satisfies its specification. Such an agent can be served from a cloud API or from an inference device on the premises. For organizations whose designs, customer data or export-controlled material cannot leave the site, and whose simulators are licensed there, local deployment is the natural choice. In such a deployment, the agent becomes an electrical load on the organization's own equipment, i.e., every run consumes power and energy on a local device, generates tokens and takes time. However, existing work focuses mainly on what the agent can achieve with cloud-hosted models. What a local deployment costs in power, energy, tokens and time on real simulation tasks, and what its choices trade against one another, has received little quantitative investigation under comparable conditions. To address this gap, a systematic evaluation framework is presented in this paper. It applies locally served models to agentic power electronics tasks, from the converter level to the system level, and measures their power, energy, token and time costs. Based on these measurements, the capability boundary of a local agent on such tasks and the cost per solved task are characterized. The results are expected to provide practical insight for the use of agentic AI in power electronics and the power systems it serves.

Learning to Interact in Multi-Agent Self-Play for Controllable Autonomous Driving Scenario Generation

Beyazit Yalcinkaya, Ethan Pronovost, Maximilian Naumann, Marcell Vazquez-Chanlatte

Large-scale, multi-agent self-play trains robust driving policies effectively in simulation, but controlling these models to generate specific, safety-critical edge cases remains a key challenge. Existing goal-conditioned self-play frameworks rely primarily on static map-location goals and fail to reliably produce complex agent interactions such as aggressive tailgating or adversarial cut-offs. These agent interactions are necessary for rigorous autonomous vehicle (AV) testing. To address this, we present an extended multi-agent self-play framework that introduces agent-relative dynamic goals. By conditioning policies on this extended goal representation, our framework enables targeted, controllable scenario synthesis. We demonstrate that our approach successfully generates interactive driving behaviors that static-goal baselines cannot simulate, providing a flexible tool for long-tail scenario generation in AV testing.

NereidVLA: Learning to Understand and Navigate the Underwater World

Chenjie Zhao, Zhiliang Zhu, Quan Chen, Yuquan Xue, Xingxue Shi et al. (11 authors)

Underwater navigation requires translating visual spatial cues into motion, yet marine vision-language datasets rarely include paired robot trajectories. We present NereidVLA, a vision-language-action model that transfers marine spatial knowledge to language-conditioned 3D navigation. We first train Sea2Know on approximately two million marine examples to learn target grounding, spatial relations, and scene structure without paired robot-motion supervision. We then adapt it using expert trajectories executed under simulated underwater vehicle dynamics. Given RGB histories and a language instruction, NereidVLA predicts discrete motion tokens that decode into local 3D trajectories for closed-loop tracking. Evaluation combines an independent six-dimensional underwater navigation understanding benchmark with offline prediction and closed-loop navigation. Under matched navigation training and execution settings, marine spatial pretraining increases simulation success from 72.4% to 84.4%. Without fine-tuning on the real test environment, NereidVLA achieves 78.75% success in laboratory-pool robot trials, compared with 47.50% for InternVLA-N1. These results show that marine spatial supervision can support downstream motion learning and physical underwater navigation.

Communications-free Collective Intelligence Via Allocentric Belief

Cameron Mehlman, Arjun Mulchandani, Hovik Grigoryan, Anurag Koyyada, Gregory Falco

A team of skilled card players share no information amongst each other, and yet still are able to coordinate their play by watching the same players and reading the same tells. Autonomous systems have failed to exploit this logic, relying instead on explicit communication or complex state estimation schemes that can fail when it matters most. Without it, decentralized agents diverge in their world beliefs, and coordination collapses. Here we show that anchoring all agents to a single shared external actor, a common witness whose reactions encode team state, produces spontaneous, communication-free coordination. An allocentric belief network trained on witness behavior allows each agent to independently reconstruct full team state, keeping group beliefs consistent with no inter-agent exchange. Across five competitive environments allocentric belief alignment outperforms egocentric approaches in collective performance, and even scales to sixty-agent teams where standard methods fail. These results establish a new principle for collective intelligence: in the absence of communication, a shared gaze replaces a shared channel.
