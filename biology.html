A Control-Theoretic Approximation to Predictive Coding Dynamics

Ryan Fayyazi, Kyle Daruwalla, Mitra Javadzadeh

Backpropagation remains the dominant method for training deep neural networks, but its reliance on non-local computations has motivated the search for biologically plausible alternatives. Two prominent frameworks, predictive coding (PC) and deep feedback control (DFC), offer distinct approaches to local learning, yet their relationship remains unclear. Here, we show that DFC arises as a local approximation to predictive coding dynamics near fixed points, with exact equivalence in shallow linear networks and systematic deviations arising with depth, nonlinearity, and distance from equilibrium. We characterize how this approximation, which we call Approx PC, deviates from PC in its activity dynamics, and show that these deviations can be mitigated by relinearizing the feedback controller and approaching the output target through a sequence of intermediate nudges. In networks trained on MNIST and FashionMNIST, Approx PC reaches task performance comparable to PC trained with the same nudges, and weights learned with it transfer directly to PC inference. Our results unify energy-based and control-theoretic perspectives on learning and open the door to applying tools from control theory to the training of predictive coding networks.

Temporal Contrastive Alignment of EEG and Naturalistic Video Only Beats Linear Decoding On In-Sample Stimuli

Yuanyuan Yao, Simon Geirnaert, Alexander Bertrand

Neural tracking, the temporal alignment between a continuous stimulus and the elicited brain response, enables decoding of attention from electroencephalography (EEG), yet its application to naturalistic video remains largely unexplored and mainly relies on linear canonical correlation analysis (CCA). We investigate whether a temporal contrastive model, pairing pretrained VideoMAE features with a lightweight EEG encoder trained on a correlation-based InfoNCE objective, can strengthen EEG-video alignment. Benchmarked against CCA under three generalization regimes, the model outperforms CCA across subjects (leave-one-subject-out), matches it within the stimulus manifold (leave-video-segment-out), and falls behind CCA when extrapolating to entirely novel videos (leave-one-video-out). Because this advantage appears only for seen stimuli, we probe its basis: a sham control shows that a high accuracy does not certify genuine alignment, as a synthesized correspondence fit to the seen stimuli can reproduce it without genuine temporal alignment in the training pairs. This does not preclude practical use: on a downstream attention-decoding task with seen stimuli, the model remains better than CCA since the decoding only requires a strong statistical coupling, regardless of whether that coupling is physiologically meaningful. Using a group-averaged EEG reference, we further show that genuine, generalizable coupling is nonetheless learned, only buried in the noise of single-subject EEG. These results indicate that progress hinges on data scale, diversity, and single-subject denoising.

Revealing Pathway Interactions in Network Computations with Complete Path Contribution

Shenghua Liu, Youssef Faragalla, Surya Ganguli, Stephen A. Baccus

Networks pass signals through many layers of interconnected units, so explaining what they compute means tracing how information travels from input to output and how pathways interact. Most existing approaches analyze only pairwise interactions between components such as units, layers or attention heads. Even when these measurements are combined post hoc into a circuit, they do not capture higher-order interactions along full causal chains (paths) from input to output, and so cannot address how exponentially many paths interact to construct network computation. This leaves a large set of mechanistic questions unanswerable, for example whether inhibitory pathways veto excitatory ones, and how the two combine to yield network decisions. To address this gap, we introduce Complete Path Contribution, a method to decompose the network output into mathematically complete contributions of individual paths to study path-level interactions directly and at scale. Partial summation then induces definitions of contributions for any circuit at any granularity level, with completeness guaranteed at every level. We further present an efficient algorithm that identifies the $k$ strongest paths without examining all of them, with runtime empirically linear in $k$ rather than exponential in depth. By applying Path Contribution to benchmark image classification networks, we quantify excitatory-inhibitory interactions and show how they collectively construct network decisions at a level of detail previously unmeasurable. Furthermore, in an interpretable model of the mouse retina, it reveals how dynamic receptive fields emerge from excitatory-inhibitory interaction motifs among pathways between different cell types in response to time-varying natural stimuli. Overall, Path Contribution reveals how a network's causal pathways interact at scale to transform input to output.

Does the Workspace Do the Work? A Causal Protocol for Verifying Global-Workspace Computation in a Language Model

Diaoxuan He, Linqian Wu

Global Workspace Theory (GWT), a leading theory of consciousness, holds that information becomes conscious when it is broadcast to specialized processes that use it. Building a workspace into a network does not reveal what the trained network computes with it: a module that reports an answer may have combined a broadcast message with its own knowledge, or merely forwarded an answer computed elsewhere, and task accuracy and ablation cannot tell these apart. We introduce a causal protocol that identifies who computes when modules communicate. Its central test transplants a message from another input, chosen so that ignoring, relaying and recombining it predict three different answers. On networks built to relay or to recombine, the test assigns the right computation in every case. Among trained workspace models it finds all three outcomes: one leaves its broadcast nearly unused, and of two models that differ only in their training data, one relays and the other recombines. In AFGW, a language model whose modules communicate only through a learned workspace, a transplanted message yields the answer that recombination predicts in 97% of cases, and its content acts by redirecting what the receiver retrieves from its own memory. The tests carry over to other communication designs and to a known interface of a pretrained Transformer, turning GWT's central claim into a testable property of trained models.

Regime-dependent reconstruction of structural connectivity: an in silico study of spatially constrained modular neuronal networks

Laia Barjuan, Hideaki Yamamoto, M. Ángeles Serrano, Jordi Soriano

Inferring structural connectivity from neuronal activity is a major challenge in neuroscience, yet the conditions under which inferred interactions faithfully reflect anatomical connections remain unclear. Here, we investigate in silico how spatial constraints in a neuronal network and its dynamical regime jointly shape structural recoverability. We construct modular networks with varying average axonal lengths and simulate spontaneous activity using the Izhikevich model with synaptic depression across a broad range of excitatory strengths and background noise levels. Using Generalized Transfer Entropy, we infer effective connectivity and compare it with the underlying structural ground-truth at the levels of individual connections and global topology. We observe that the richness in the repertoire of activity patterns is highest in an intermediate regime between globally synchronized and noise-dominated activity, reflecting a balance between global excitation and stochasticity. Optimal structural reconstruction generally occurs under these same conditions, although this correspondence depends on network architecture and is weaker in highly modular networks. Together, these results show that structural recoverability depends jointly on network organization and dynamical regime. More broadly, our findings highlight the risks of treating inferred effective connectivity as a direct surrogate for structural connectivity.

An Explainable and Scalable Hierarchical Tensor Framework for Integrating Multi-Omics and Digital Pathomics

Ahmad Hussein, Ali Anaissi, Mukesh Prasad, Ali Braytee

Background: Precision oncology requires integration of heterogeneous molecular and pathology data, yet many fusion models offer limited biologically grounded interpretability and struggle with missing modalities. Methods: We present Hierarchical Interpretable Tensor Fusion (HIT-Fuse), integrating six modalities: somatic copy number variation, DNA methylation, RNA sequencing, microRNA expression, RPPA protein abundance, and vision transformer-derived pathomics across four TCGA cohorts (glioma, lung adenocarcinoma, breast, and kidney cancer). Modality-specific autoencoders compress high-dimensional inputs, followed by two-stage CP tensor decomposition. Missing latent modalities are reconstructed using clinical covariates and observed omics to expand cohorts and retrain downstream models. A mathematical traceback mechanism enables global and local xAI attributions. Results: HIT-Fuse identifies distinct latent subgroups with significant survival stratification in glioma, breast, and kidney cohorts, both with complete and reconstructed modalities. Cohort expansion yields cohort-specific gains, including a 56.4% relative increase in weighted F1 for glioma. Biological validation against DepMap CRISPR screens, miRTarBase and RPPA annotations supports decoded drivers across all four cohorts. Essential gene rates range from 6.2% in glioma to 7.6% in lung cancer; example drivers include ANAPC11, SNRPD1, PLK1 and RPL14. Of decoded miRNAs, 71-92% have miRTarBase regulatory support, while 77-84% of selected genes are covered in DepMap. Conclusions: HIT-Fuse combines hierarchical tensor fusion, missing-modality reconstruction and traceable attributions to support interpretable multi-omics and digital pathology-based cancer prognosis across heterogeneous cohorts.

Dynamical Variational Theory of Stochastic Reaction Networks

Moshir Harsh, Peter Sollich

Discrete stochastic population dynamics describe systems ranging from biochemical reaction networks and gene regulation to epidemic spreading, ecology, and population genetics, where finite populations generate strong intrinsic fluctuations. Predicting their dynamics, including mean populations, fluctuations, time-lagged correlations, response functions, and steady states, remains challenging: the chemical master equation admits no general analytical solution, numerical solutions scale poorly with system size, and standard approximations fail at low copy numbers. Using a field-theoretic representation of the reaction dynamics, we develop a dynamical variational approximation for stochastic reaction networks based on the Plefka expansion from spin-glass physics. For reaction systems with general stoichiometry and time-dependent rates, the expansion yields tractable non-Markovian effective dynamics with self-consistent memory. We further introduce a generalized self-consistent bubble resummation that provides closed predictions for means, correlations, and response functions. The formulation applies broadly to discrete stochastic population dynamics on networks, and we provide a numerical implementation with adaptive time stepping for user-defined systems. We benchmark the method on stochastic gene regulation, enzyme kinetics, and infection dynamics, where it accurately captures both time-dependent behaviour and steady states, including fluctuation-driven effects missed by conventional approximations. Together, these results establish a general analytical framework for stochastic reaction dynamics across transient and stationary regimes in strongly fluctuating low-copy-number systems.

Fractional Order or False Memory: Identifiability of Caputo SIR Models from Incidence Data

Md Babul Hossain, Mohammad Shah Alam

When a fractional order improves the fit of an epidemic model, it is usually taken as evidence of memory. We ask whether the order of the Caputo susceptible-infectious-removed (SIR) model can actually be recovered from incidence data. If the transmission rate is allowed to vary freely in time, as in hybrid forecasting models, every admissible order, removal rate and initial count reproduces the data exactly. With constant transmission and exact data the order is identifiable in principle, but the dominant term of early linearized growth depends on only two parameter combinations: the growth rate r = (beta - gamma)^(1/alpha) and the amplitude beta*I0/alpha. What is left is transient, and it is limited. We prove that parameter sets sharing these two combinations give Poisson count distributions whose Kullback-Leibler divergence stays bounded no matter how long the linear phase is observed, and that the nonlinear model inherits this bound as the population grows. In simulations, wrong orders were rejected in at most 5% of replicates when the data ended two weeks or more before the epidemic peak, and in 95-100% once the peak was included; separation began only a few days before the peak. With mild overdispersion the order was identified only once about 60% of the population had been infected, and with stronger overdispersion not even then. Fits that the early data could not tell apart predicted peaks that differed by 75%, and plug-in forecasts that ignored parameter uncertainty were badly overconfident. Data generated without any memory produced fitted orders below one when transmission changed and when the fitted model was discretized in daily steps. In early COVID-19 data from Bangladesh and the United States, orders from 0.6 to 1 gave almost the same growth rate, whatever value we assumed for the removal rate.

From Sequence Uncertainty to Phenotype Hypotheses: Uncertainty-Conditioned Inference over Biomedical Knowledge Graphs

Huixin Zhan, Jason H. Moore

Genomic foundation models provide variant-level evidence, but connecting that evidence to phenotype hypotheses requires accounting for uncertainty throughout structured reasoning. We introduce an uncertainty-conditioned path-energy framework that converts sequence predictions and calibrated predictive uncertainty into explicit distributions over graph-supported phenotype hypotheses, without patient-level phenotype supervision. An interpretable learned energy scores admissible gene--disease--phenotype routes, query uncertainty controls the target temperature, and path probabilities are aggregated into phenotype hypotheses. This formulation makes evidence quality an explicit determinant of hypothesis breadth while retaining traceable multi-path support. Exact normalization over enumerable candidate sets provides a reference for evaluating a Hastings-corrected learned revision operator. On AlzKB and Hetionet, phenotype-level entropy tracks upstream uncertainty, with Spearman correlations of 0.951 and 0.878, respectively; ablations identify the contribution of uncertainty conditioning. In masked-edge recovery, the model exceeds chance and degree-matched probability-permutation controls. Training the energy under the uncertainty-dependent temperature yields higher held-out recall@10 than training under a constant temperature of comparable magnitude. Under gene hold-out, recall@10 exceeds that of a trained DistMult embedding on 24 of 30 graph masks (0.183 versus 0.123); this difference is attributable mainly to three genes with many variants and is not established for all genes. These results establish an uncertainty-aware interface from genomic predictions to graph-supported phenotype hypotheses that combines interpretable probability allocation with held-out association recovery of explicitly stated scope.

When Should Deployed BCI Decoders Adapt? A Resource-Priced Ladder for Frozen EEG Trunks

Sergey Musienko

When should a deployed MI decoder spend adaptation resources-not only how to adapt under shift? We study a thin control layer outside a frozen encoder: a resource ladder (factory -> Bayesian head -> affine map, with full fine-tune as an escape hatch) governed by regime-aware gating under a utility that prices accuracy, fit time, and label cost. The head is Bayesian in the conjugate sense: a Normal-Inverse-Wishart prior over the class conditional Gaussians in embedding space, updated in closed form (no gradients) as labelled trials arrive. Full estimators, constants, and the reanalysis code are in the companion paper and the ancillary bundle of this submission. Thesis: adaptation should be gated and capped, not always-on and not blindly fine-tuned. Evidence: (i) utility-max gating over-adapts on clean data that already carries session drift; a clean-referenced gate restores low false-entry rates (e.g. dropping the adaptation rate from 0.56 to 0.11 on field-like sessions, with the cascade staying on the factory decoder for ~87% of clean cells). (ii) Under severe synthetic stress, a lightweight LDA head matches or exceeds the frozen trunk's accuracy across five encoder architectures (n=155 subject x trunk cells, pooled across shared subjects), at ~23x lower fit time than full warm-start fine-tuning. Always-on baselines favor capping the default below full fine-tune. (iii) In two no-perturbation BNCI session checks the factory model transferred without intervention (no reliable head gain), so the control layer's value under natural drift is cost-efficiency and clean-safety rather than demonstrated accuracy gains. (iv) Adaptation severity does not follow a universal ordinal ladder across datasets, highlighting the need for dynamic gating over fixed adaptation policies.
