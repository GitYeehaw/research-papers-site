SafeDriveVLA: Navigation-Conditioned World Model Dreaming for Conflict-Aware End-to-End Autonomous Driving

Shaoyuan Xie, Zihan Zhang, Jingxuan Wang, Jiashu Qu, Xiaoqing Liang et al. (9 authors)

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models are gaining attention in autonomous driving, inheriting commonsense priors from large language models (LLMs) and widening the navigation interface from a fixed command set to free-form natural-language instructions. However, this open-ended interface exposes a new vulnerability surface: an inattentive driver may issue instructions that are unsafe given the current scene, and a malicious user may issue dangerous ones on purpose. We present the first benchmark suite that evaluates navigation following and conflict awareness together, along with adversarial robustness. Existing VLAs fall short on both: SimLingo completes no language-instructed lane change in closed loop, and all models degrade sharply under conflicting signals. We propose SafeDriveVLA, the first driving VLA that (1) explicitly predicts a three-level navigation-compliance mode to decouple conflict reasoning from action generation, and (2) uses navigation-conditioned world model dreaming to inform this prediction. Unlike prior world models that roll out expert or planned actions, ours dreams of strictly following the navigation signal, showing when following is safe. SafeDriveVLA reaches 88.16 Driving Score (DS) and 68.64% Success Rate (SR) on Bench2Drive, lifts closed-loop language following by 49.3% and reduces collisions under unsafe instructions from 60 to 38 compared to the best SimLingo variant. Video demonstrations are available on our project page.

Modeling and Understanding Neural Weights of Humanoid Policies with Diffusion

Huixuan Cheng, Bike Zhang, Shankar Sastry, Koushil Sreenath

Recent advances in robot learning have driven rapid progress in humanoid control, enabling increasingly agile behaviors in the real world. However, humanoid control networks are typically obtained through conventional training procedures, while comparatively little attention has been given to directly modeling the distribution of their learned weights and generating functional networks in weight space. In this work, we investigate whether high-performing humanoid control networks can be generatively modeled directly through their weights. We adopt a latent diffusion framework that couples a linear autoencoder with a Diffusion Transformer. The autoencoder compresses actor network parameters into a compact latent space, and the Diffusion Transformer models the distribution of these latents. Trained on networks collected from reinforcement learning for the Unitree G1 robot, our approach generates deployable networks that perform various motions in the real world, including walking, dancing, and spin kick. Beyond generation, we further study the learned weight space to better understand the structure captured by these humanoid control networks. We identify a structural principle in the first-layer weights that consistently reflects the robot's kinematic prior. Notably, this kinematic prior emerges under both Reinforcement Learning (RL) and Imitation Learning (IL) training, suggesting that it is not specific to a particular optimization process but may instead reflect common knowledge associated with humanoid embodiment. Together, these results demonstrate the feasibility of generative modeling in humanoid network weight space, while providing initial insights into the information encoded by their learned weights. Additional results are available on our project website: https://humanoid-weight-diffusion.github.io/

Hierarchical Residual Policy Learning for Real-World Mobile Manipulation with Sparse Human Guidance

Zhefei Gong, Hefeifei Jiang, Zehao Wang, Jiachen Li

Mobile Manipulation (MoMa) is a cornerstone of autonomous robotic systems, yet real-world fine-tuning remains a formidable challenge because of high-dimensional action spaces and intricate coupling between heterogeneous modules. In this paper, we propose HiRe-MoMa (Hierarchical Residual Mobile Manipulation), a systematic framework for stable and sample-efficient real-world fine-tuning of complex MoMa systems. At its core, HiRe-MoMa adopts a Hierarchical Residual Architecture that fine-tunes a frozen base policy. A Policy-Agnostic Residual learns additive corrections atop the base policy, while Hierarchical Decoupling splits the residual into mobile-base and manipulator-arm heads with unidirectional conditioning, mitigating gradient interference and aligning corrections with the platform's physical hierarchy. To improve sample efficiency in data-expensive real-world settings, we propose Expert-Guided Value Alignment, which leverages sparse human interventions with under 20% training coverage to reshape the critic's value landscape, effectively guiding autonomous exploration through expert priors. Extensive real-world experiments on the TIAGo++ platform demonstrate that HiRe-MoMa consistently enhances task performance with only around 30 minutes of online interaction, regardless of the base policy architecture. To our knowledge, this is among the first frameworks to enable efficient on-robot fine-tuning for mobile manipulation in the real world, providing a practical paradigm for deploying complex robotic systems in dynamic, human-centric environments. Further details can be found on our project website: https://hire-moma.github.io/

CoWear: Towards Collaborative Wearable Inertial Localization with Multiple Smart Devices

Zhuoyuan She, Zhanpeng Zhang, Yapeng Chen, Jiale Wang, Xin Xie et al. (6 authors)

Consumer smart devices observe the same human motion from different body locations, yet existing inertial-localization benchmarks rarely provide synchronized heterogeneous wearables with device-specific motion ground truth. We present CoWear, a dataset and reference framework for collaborative inertial localization using a smartphone, smartwatch, and smart glasses. The dataset contains over 30 participants, over 300 walking sessions, and more than 30 hours and 100 km of aggregate device trajectories, with each device individually tracked by motion capture. To enable collaboration across distinct body motions, we train role-specific estimators to predict the displacement of a common reference device over synchronized temporal windows. Their predictions are transformed to the world frame using gyroscope integration and fused according to learned uncertainty. Experiments using phone, glasses, and watch as reference targets show that multi-device fusion consistently improves both displacement and trajectory rollout accuracy over individual-device estimation. CoWear provides a dataset and reproducible baseline for studying collaborative inertial localization across heterogeneous consumer wearables.

PingpongDuo: Agile Humanoid Table Tennis Doubles with Safe Team Coordination

Haoran Jiang, Yichao Zhong, Kai Zhang, Cheng Liu, Yixuan Pan et al. (9 authors)

Unlike singles, humanoid table tennis doubles requires teammates to strike in strict alternation while sharing a confined workspace. Rapid repositioning can compromise striking accuracy, while uncoordinated teammate movements introduce collision risks. We present PingpongDuo, a hierarchical framework for agile humanoid doubles with safe team coordination. At the team level, a planner jointly selects positioning targets and uses learned risk estimates to filter risky proposals, preferentially preserving the current striker's target. At the individual level, separate footwork and striking skills combine agile movement with precise racket control. The skills are guided by human motion references, including footwork collected from human doubles play, and trained from states produced by one another to support rapid transitions. A racket-arm residual further refines strikes after movement using return feedback from a simulator calibrated with real ball-flight and contact measurements. In simulation, PingpongDuo improves the ball-return success rate by 13.2% over the state-of-the-art baseline, while achieving approximately 2 cm positioning error during clearance. In real-world experiments, a robot completes a 2.15 m clearance-and-recovery cycle in 2.4 s with a peak speed of 2.23 m/s, while two robots sustain more than 10 consecutive strikes in strict alternation.

KinoGen: Customizable Humanoid Loco-Manipulation References via Kinematically Grounded Video Generation

Qi Wu, Lulu Zhao, Sanghyun Hahn, Rishi Yennu, Emmy Yang et al. (9 authors)

We present KinoGen, a framework that synthesizes customizable humanoid loco-manipulation references through kinematically grounded video generation. KinoGen conditions a pretrained video generator on calibrated renders of the target robot and object in a simulated task instance and recovers the reference by fitting the same models to the generated two-view video, so that the reference lies in the robot configuration space by construction without a human body proxy or retargeting. Body, hand, and object priors initialize the reconstruction, and direct optimization against cross-view evidence refines it. Since an edited keyframe is simply another rendered state, a user can prescribe a goal object pose, regenerate the video around the edited keyframe, and obtain a reference that satisfies the pose exactly. Across eight loco-manipulation tasks, KinoGen yields lower penetration and foot skating than video-based baselines and preserves over 90% of annotated contacts, and its customized references satisfy every prescribed target with substantially less penetration than direct interpolation. Tracking policies trained on the synthesized references are deployed on a Unitree G1 humanoid with dexterous hands. Project webpage: https://kinogen-humanoid.github.io/.

EarlyCommit: Calibrated Word-by-Word Commitment for Robots Acting Before You Finish Speaking

Muhammad Haris Khan

Human listeners can often identify the intended object several words before an instruction ends. Robots, however, are typically designed to wait until the speaker has finished before deciding how to act. We present EarlyCommit, an open-source tool that lets a robot commit to the intended object while the instruction is being spoken. After every recognized word, a fine-tuned text encoder scores each object in the scene. The robot acts when the calibrated probability of the best object reaches a threshold tau set by the user, so 1 - tau is the accepted risk of choosing the wrong object. Otherwise, it waits or asks. The tool includes a streaming speech front end, a scene and instruction generator with exact word-by-word ground truth, and scripts to train, calibrate, and evaluate. On instructions containing words never seen in training, EarlyCommit commits correctly 1.6 to 2 times as often as keyword matching, is right at least 92 percent of the time when it commits at tau = 0.9, and needs 31 to 35 ms per word on consumer GPUs.

FedRIC: Heterogeneity-Aware Federated Learning with Hard Deadline Constraints in Open RAN

Adhwaa Al-Chaab, Ayman Younis, Dario Pompili

Open Radio Access Networks (O-RANs) bring intelligence to disaggregated RANs through virtualized platforms and open interfaces, but the stringent latency budgets of the RAN Intelligent Controller (RIC) and heterogeneous traffic conditions make real-time deployment costly. Centralized learning is ill-suited to the RIC due to its computational burden, timing constraints, and communication overhead, motivating Federated Learning (FL) as a distributed alternative. We formulate FL in O-RAN as a Mixed-Integer Nonlinear Program (MINLP) that minimizes communication cost and learning time under heterogeneity in User Equipment (UE) compute, traffic, and channel conditions, and decompose it into a UE Assignment (UA) subproblem and a Computation and Bandwidth Allocation (CBA) subproblem. We then propose FedRIC, a heterogeneity-aware FL framework combining compressed gradient descent, slice-aware resource allocation, and multilevel hierarchical aggregation. Experiments show that FedRIC outperforms FedCOM, FedAvg, and QFedAvg in convergence rate, communication cost, and latency.

The Physics Is the Network: Fast, Training-Free Neural Emulation

Luka Todorovic, Kaushal Gianchandani, Sai Ravela

Neural emulation need not require learning a substitute for known physics. We investigate PolyNets that couple a continuous-time neural representation of polynomial dynamics with a simple recurrent network for discrete-time integration. When the governing coefficients are known, the resulting tensor graph requires no training and preserves the prescribed numerical scheme while enabling efficient hardware execution and parallel simulation. Using the one-dimensional Korteweg-de Vries equation, we compare PolyNet with CPU and GPU finite-difference Runge-Kutta solvers and three trained neural models: a state-map emulator, a physics-informed predictor, and a learned-tendency integrator. Analytical soliton solutions provide a reference for amplitude and phase errors across grid resolutions, 10^6 dispersively scaled time steps, ensembles of up to 10^4 trajectories, and varying initial amplitudes. PolyNet achieves accuracy comparable to the numerical solvers, including at amplitudes outside the learned models' training conditions. It combines this fidelity with high GPU throughput, approximately constant over the tested resolution range, and favorable ensemble scaling.

Physical Time as a Missing Dimension of Robot Policy Memory Dynamics

Yushen Liang, Yue Peng, Baosheng Jin, Tianluo Zhang, Xinyu Zhang et al. (9 authors)

Existing robot policy memories typically evolve with observation sequences, implicitly treating each update as the same amount of physical time. Yet identical observation histories can span different durations and require different actions, exposing physical time as a missing dimension of policy memory. We introduce Temporal Flow Policy (TFP), which learns memory dynamics to retain past events and evolve the memory state over measured time intervals. Observations and prior memory determine the content and time scales of each transition; elapsed time determines how far the state advances. TFP conditions diffusion and flow-matching policies through this memory state and is trained with their native imitation objectives. We evaluate TFP with Diffusion Policy and $π_{0.5}$ through controlled comparisons of memory designs. These experiments show that successful timing depends on both measured intervals and retained history. TFP achieves higher average success than the recurrent and state-space memories we equip with the same intervals and than a variant that feeds the interval to a learned gate. Memory readouts recover the time since an event and motion information after occlusion. Changing the supplied intervals systematically delays or advances actions, linking time inputs to behavior. Real-robot tasks requiring timing or memory, together with LIBERO evaluations, demonstrate benefits in physical and general manipulation. These results suggest viewing robot memory not as stored observation history, but as a state that retains past events and evolves with physical time.
