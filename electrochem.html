Prediction and Screening of $η$-carbide-type Nitrides for Ammonia Synthesis

Luka H. Mikek, Nicholas D. Humphrey, Andrea Ambrosini, Matthew D. Witman, Dallas R. Trinkle

Synthesis of ammonia is of critical importance to applications in energy, industrial chemistry, and especially agriculture, and is accomplished industrially using the carbon- and energy-intensive Haber-Bosch process. η-carbide-type nitrides such as Co3Mo3N have gained attention in recent years as alternative ammonia synthesis catalysts with the potential to lower these energy demands. Co3Mo3N is experimentally observed to undergo a reversible phase transition from Co3Mo3N to Co6Mo6N, offering a chemical looping-based NH3 synthesis route that has not been fully explored. In this work, we present a first-principles exploration of phase change in the Co3Mo3N/Co6Mo6N system as a prototype η-carbide-type nitride and fit a cluster expansion to predict the energy of Co3Mo3Nx systems. We use our results to identify the phase energy difference as a useful screening criterion for the phase change process. We then apply this methodology to screen a broad range of η-carbide-type nitrides that combine group 4-6 transition metals with Cr, Fe, Co, Ni, or Zn. We report strong periodic trends in phase energy difference, which increases with decreasing period and group number of the group 4-6 transition metal and with distance from group 9 for the other metal. We identify Co3W3N, Fe3W3N, and several quaternary nitrides as promising candidates for NH3 synthesis via a phase change mechanism.

Toward high-throughput, low-latency structural biology using light, Part I: Protein-ligand pose determination

Brian K. Petkov, David R. Klug

We demonstrate for the first time that an optical spectroscopy - electron-vibration-vibration two-dimensional infrared spectroscopy (EVV 2DIR) - has sufficient structural competence to enable the reconstruction of the three-dimensional geometries of drug-protein binding at the level needed for practical drug discovery applications. Klug et al. previously demonstrated experimental detection of drug binding to a kinase using EVV 2DIR. Here we use a quantum mechanical approach to calculate and assign elements of EVV 2DIR spectra for the spectral region 1200-1800 cm$^{-1}$ / 1100-1800 cm$^{-1}$ of 92 protein-ligand complexes. This dataset includes ligand-target poses drawn from existing crystal structures and ligand-target poses created by docking and cofolding models. We find that binding-dependent spectral features are robust, interpretable, and consistent across poses in a given binding mode, and distinct for each unique hydrogen bonding geometry. We construct a spectral distance metric to distinguish poses that conform to the ligand-target hydrogen-bonding pattern of the crystal structure from those that do not. These results show that, given a set of predicted ligand-target poses containing the correct geometry, the hydrogen-bonding pattern of the valid pose may be rapidly identified by comparing simulated and experimental EVV 2DIR spectra. Finally, we discuss the practical implications for a new, high throughput structural biology for drug discovery that will be the subject of forthcoming manuscripts, noting that the base technologies to perform EVV 2DIR quickly and efficiently already exist and will enable a novel structure to be determined in only minutes for each sample. Moreover, this can be achieved without the need for crystallization, isotopic labeling, or low temperatures, non-destructively using picogram quantities of material.

Quantum Simulation of First-Quantised Chemistry with a Sum-of-Squares Representation

Aleksei V. Ivanov, Christoph Sünderhauf, Jon Kunjummen, Nick S. Blunt, Róbert Izsák

Calculating molecular energies is expected to be an important application of quantum computers, but the most efficient algorithms to achieve this typically have significant space requirements. Scaling quantum processors to thousands of logical qubits is a daunting engineering challenge, so developing algorithms that run with reduced qubit requirements is of particular importance. Moreover, most algorithms are benchmarked on active spaces with about as many orbitals as electrons, missing dynamical correlation which is necessary to describe real chemistry. Capturing this correlation requires several times more orbitals than electrons, even in compact atomic-orbital basis sets. This low-filling regime favours first quantisation, where each electron is represented by a number of qubits that grows only logarithmically with the number of orbitals. Whether this approach translates into a practical advantage in orbital basis sets of realistic size, however, has been unclear. Here, we develop a first-quantised phase estimation algorithm for generic orbital basis sets, based on a non-convex sum-of-squares representation of the Hamiltonian and spectral-gap amplification. We compare it with the state-of-the-art second-quantised algorithm, compiling both with the same techniques at the logical level. For six molecules with 4 to 24 electrons in 5 to 14 times as many orbitals, our first-quantised method needs up to 16 times fewer Toffoli gates than the second-quantised algorithm in the low-qubit regime, where only a few hundred logical qubits are available. It stays cheaper up to between several hundred and about 1\,400 qubits, and its smallest qubit count is 1.4 to 2 times lower than in second quantisation. In the low-qubit and low-filling regime, our algorithm is therefore the method of choice for chemistry in orbital basis sets.

xTBloom: GPU Batching for High-Throughput GFN-xTB Calculations

Zitian Lu, Shengrui Xu, Tong Zhu, Qiangqiang Gu, Jinzhe Zeng

Conformational searches and multiwindow free-energy sampling require large numbers of GFN-xTB energy and force evaluations across molecular conformations and sampling windows. Processing each system separately on a GPU makes it difficult to amortize memory management, host-device data transfer, and kernel-launch overhead. Here, we present xTBloom, a GPU batching library for GFN-xTB energy and force evaluations. xTBloom sorts evaluation queues by atomic orbital (AO) count before forming batches, accommodates size differences through a native ragged layout, maintains independent self-consistent charge (SCC) and failure states for each system, and selects execution paths according to data residency. We focus the quantitative evaluation on GFN2-xTB through cross-program comparisons on QM9 and OMol25, CPU-CUDA comparisons, finite-difference force checks, and controlled failure tests. On a near-size OMol25 workload, GPU execution achieved a 2.9-fold speedup over xTBloom's single-core CPU backend. A companion LAMMPS plugin integrates cross-window batching with periodic quantum mechanical/molecular mechanical (QM/MM) simulation. In a short synchronized test with 48 windows on one GPU, the complete coupled workflow achieved an aggregate speedup of approximately 14 over sequential execution of the same windows. These results demonstrate the throughput benefits of batched GFN-xTB evaluation for repeated electronic-structure calculations and multiwindow sampling.

Design and Modeling of Graded Base III-Nitride HBTs for High-Frequency Applications

Md Saklain Morshed, Chandan Joishi, Ijaas Mohamed, Siddharth Rajan

This work analyzes the high-frequency performance constraints of AlGaN/GaN heterojunction bipolar transistors (HBTs) and proposes a novel band-engineered graded base N-polar AlGaN/GaN HBT and evaluates the RF performances. Unlike conventional p-GaN base designs, we propose a graded base in the N-polar orientation which offers polarization-induced hole accumulation with quasi-electric field-assisted minority carrier transport. The proposed design simultaneously mitigates the longstanding current crowding, Webster effect, and conventional base resistance limitations. Using well-calibrated two-dimensional TCAD simulations, the proposed compositionally graded base HBT projects peak f_T/f_{max} of 190/200 GHz with a power density (V_{br}I_{max}/8 > 20 W/mm), offering a promising pathway toward simultaneous high-frequency and high-power GaN millimeter-wave electronics.

4D-TEMPO: Differentiable 4D-STEM ptychography via fast Physics Optimizers

Ramani Duraiswami, Yichao Zhang

Electron ptychography recovers atomic structure from 4D-STEM data: diffraction patterns at multiple positions of a scanned probe. Current codes reconstruct an image and read atoms off it, or fit atoms under an $L_1$ loss; neither gives per-atom uncertainty. We propose 4D-TEMPO, a differentiable physical renderer that fits the atoms, the microscope, and the detector directly to the recorded electron counts under a Poisson likelihood. The output is a list of atoms, each a Gaussian primitive with a 3D position, a thermal width, and a model-conditional error bar from the Fisher information. The Fisher information also conditions the solver, gives the dose (electrons per Å$^2$) each parameter needs, and separates calibration from imaging. On a simulated WSe$_2$ bilayer, starting from the published reconstruction, fitted atoms cut in-plane error by a third (3.2 vs. 4.8 pm) and depth error by 40-61% against its 3D peaks. Depth comes from multiple scattering into the dark field, the faint counts outside the direct beam. The two layers, $10\times$ closer than the depth of field, separate; per-atom depth then follows the layer geometry. When the model is well specified, the bars are calibrated (69% of position coordinates within $1σ$). On experimental WSe$_2$, fitted atoms predict held-out patterns better than the three published codes we test. With free probe modes where atoms are incomplete, the same likelihood predicts best on all three held-out benchmarks, matches every WSe$_2$ baseline in 5 min against up to 20.5 h, and passes torchslice at its released setting 180$\times$ sooner on PrScO$_3$. Per-atom error bars, planned doses, and fast reconstruction point to low-dose, real-time 3D imaging of beam-sensitive and dynamic materials.

Characterizing adsorption thermodynamics in carbon capture materials: an efficient first-principles approach

Anna T. Bui, Grant M. Rotskoff, Thomas E. Markland

Porous materials offer a vast design space for CO$_2$ capture, whose exploration hinges on efficient and accurate first-principles predictions of the thermodynamics governing adsorption. However, simulating isotherms and heats of adsorption using grand canonical molecular simulations is extremely computationally expensive when combined with accurate ab initio treatments of the molecular interactions, limiting screening to low-pressure uptake encoded in the Henry constant. Identifying and designing the best CO$_2$ capture materials requires a strategy that can efficiently calculate their adsorption properties using ab initio potential energy surfaces such as those now available from foundational machine-learned interatomic potentials (MLIPs). Here we develop a neural classical density functional (cDFT) framework for carbon capture processes that allows direct calculation of adsorption isotherms from low to high loading limits of metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) without grand canonical simulations. We demonstrate the success of our approach by calculating the ab initio adsorption isotherms for over 5,000 experimentally reported MOFs. From our screening, we show how these materials can be characterized using four isotherm shapes, arising from their pore size and binding sites, which the Henry constant cannot distinguish. We show that these shapes, rather than the binding strength alone, decide which MOFs suit which process: small pores with strong binding sites for vacuum and temperature swings at low pressure, large pores with weakly binding walls for pressure swings. Finally, we identify the most promising candidates by weighing their working capacity against the energy needed for regeneration.

Grain-size distribution characterization from single-shot x-ray diffraction using hierarchical Bayesian multi-emitter analysis

Hyun Sang Park, Gannon Murray, Rohit Berlia, David Elbert, Timothy P. Weihs et al. (6 authors)

The distribution of grain sizes in a polycrystalline material can be determined from a 2D x-ray diffraction pattern by counting and characterizing diffraction spots, so long as the spots are discrete. This approach breaks down if there is substantial overlap of the reflections due to either a large number of grains in the scattering volume or crowding of spots caused by crystallographic texture. As a result, the spot-counting approach to grain size determination is infeasible for many situations of practical interest. We address this limitation by applying hierarchical probabilistic learning using a reversible-jump Markov chain Monte Carlo framework to jointly infer diffraction spot populations and their intensity distributions. Using synthetic diffraction patterns, we show how this hierarchical model allows recovery of diffraction peak intensities across a wide variety of grain-size distributions and crystallographic textures. From the recovered peak intensities we infer grain-size distributions themselves and characterize the regimes of grain size and texture for which this approach is practical. Finally, we demonstrate the application of our technique by comparing grain-size distributions in Cu-Ti alloy specimens obtained from 2D x-ray diffraction patterns with those from electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD). This method of grain-size distribution determination is expected to be particularly useful in the context of high-throughput experimentation, where the minimal sample preparation requirements and short exposure times of single-shot x-ray diffraction are substantial advantages relative to microscopy techniques.

Atomic-scale assembly of sulfur vacancy complexes for tailored in-gap states in monolayer WS$_{2}$

Daniel Jansen, Guangyao Miao, Jan-Heinrich Keienburg, Jeison Fischer, Thomas Michely et al. (7 authors)

Atomic-scale defects in two-dimensional semiconductors are quasi-isolated quantum systems with promise for emerging technologies. Although their potential as spin qubits, quantum sensors and single-photon emitters has been demonstrated experimentally, creating them deterministically and identifying them chemically remains challenging, which limits control over their electronic states. In this work we assemble sulfur vacancies in monolayer WS$_{2}$ into complexes of different sizes and geometries using the tip of a scanning tunneling microscope. We study their electronic properties by a combination of scanning tunneling spectroscopy and density functional theory, revealing the intricate influences of geometry, underlying point group symmetries and spin-orbit coupling on hybridized states. Among the realized sulfur vacancy complexes, we characterize two configurations that are candidates for bright single photon emitters. Our findings demonstrate an atomic-scale method for engineering quantum states within the band gap of two-dimensional semiconductors.

GenerativeQC: A Generative, GPU-Accelerated Quantum Chemistry Program

Shengrui Xu, Longhan Shen, Wenxi Zhai, Tong Zhu, Jinzhe Zeng

GenerativeQC is an open-source, GPU-accelerated quantum chemistry program that computes energies and analytic nuclear forces from Hartree-Fock (HF) and density functional theory (DFT) to closed-shell density-fitted CCSD(T) and an MP2-assisted CCSD(T)/complete-basis-set (CBS) composite. Its scientific compiler translates symbolic equations into C++ and CUDA code and uses automatic differentiation to generate the derivative and response programs that analytic forces require. A native runtime executes these programs together with hand-written iterative solvers and integral routines. Across HF and DFT benchmarks for eight methods on water clusters of up to 96 atoms, GenerativeQC is faster than GPU4PySCF in all 128 comparisons on an RTX 5090 GPU and in all but two comparisons on an H100 GPU. For correlated wave functions, compiler-generated derivative contractions and transpose Jacobian operators enable complete density-fitted CCSD(T) and CCSD(T)/CBS analytic forces on GPUs, completing a CCSD(T)/CBS energy-and-force calculation for heptane (23 atoms) in 4.5 min on a single H100 GPU (17 min on RTX 5090).
