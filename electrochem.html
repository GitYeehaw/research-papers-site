Magnetic Johnson noise from arbitrarily shaped conductors: experimental validation of the fluctuation-dissipation finite-element method

S. M. Clayton, Y. J. Kim, T. E. Chupp, A. Epstein, J. M. Feltman et al. (11 authors)

Magnetic Johnson noise--magnetic noise caused by thermal current fluctuations in conducting materials--can interfere with measurements and limit their sensitivity. As the sensitivity of experiments increases, it becomes increasingly important to accurately estimate the magnetic Johnson noise from experimental components with complex shapes during both the design and analysis phases. A particularly powerful approach is to combine the fluctuation-dissipation theorem with finite-element calculations. This versatile approach can be used to calculate the magnetic Johnson noise from conductors of arbitrary geometry for a wide range of frequencies and for magnetic field detectors with general geometries, such as a finite-sized loop or a gradiometer. Using a SQUID gradiometer array, we measured the magnetic Johnson noise from conductors with complex geometries and compared the results with calculations performed using this method. The calculations reproduce both the measured noise amplitude and its frequency dependence, providing an experimental validation of this method. We also performed a detailed analysis of how the geometry and material parameters affect the frequency dependence of magnetic Johnson noise.

Quantum 3xplorer: AI-Native Engine for Next-Generation Quantum Materials Modeling

Vsevolod Biryukov, Sergey V. Barabash, Pranab Kumar Das, Jack Deslippe, Mauro Del Ben et al. (15 authors)

Quantum-mechanical approaches, such as density functional theory (DFT), underpin much of modern computational materials science and are the primary source of data for AI-driven discovery. Yet the established electronic-structure codes today represent decades-old software architectures, making new physical models slow and costly to implement, validate, and deploy on emerging hardware. We present Quantum 3xplorer (Q3, pronounced "Quantum Explorer"), an AI-native quantum simulation engine written from scratch by AI coding agents under the direction and supervision of domain scientists. As a first and demanding validation case, we implement plane-wave pseudopotential DFT end to end: norm-conserving, ultrasoft, and projector-augmented-wave formalisms, spin polarization, DFT+U, and dispersion corrections, together with forces, stresses, structural relaxation, and electronic-structure analysis. Q3 reproduces established reference implementations, runs faster on a single core in representative benchmarks, and scales across GPU-accelerated supercomputers. It was developed in weeks rather than years. Its layered, physics-first architecture separates abstract quantum-mechanical concepts from their numerical representations, making its framework extendable beyond plane waves to other basis sets and levels of theory. Its inputs and outputs follow structured, schema-validated data standards, making every result AI-ready. Q3 is open source under Apache 2.0 and built to be reused, extended, and improved by the research community. Q3 points to a paradigm shift in how computational quantum science is done. Scientists define the physics, formulate the models, and set the criteria for validation; AI agents write, test, and optimize the code. The decades-long path from a new theory to a trusted, high-performance implementation can thus collapse to days, significantly accelerating scientific discovery.

Field-resolved observation and quantification of the instantaneous Kerr response in semiconductors

Haifeng Lin, Hannes Kempf, Hinduja Bhuvanendran, Aitor De Andrés, Markus Ludwig et al. (6 authors)

The optical Kerr effect, whereby the refractive index of a material changes with light intensity, underpins nonlinear photonics and emerging mid-infrared technologies. Accurate determination of the nonlinear refractive index is therefore essential, yet conventional intensity-based techniques infer the nonlinear phase shift indirectly from changes in beam propagation or optical spectra. Moreover, methods such as the $z$-scan cannot resolve the temporal evolution of the nonlinear response, making it difficult to distinguish instantaneous Kerr nonlinearities from delayed contributions, including free-carrier effects. Here, we investigate the nonlinear optical response of germanium at $5 μm$ using field-resolved degenerate pump-probe spectroscopy. By measuring the transmitted probe electric field as a function of pump-probe delay, we directly retrieve the pump-induced phase shift and its temporal evolution while retaining sensitivity to changes in absorption. We observe cross-phase modulation that follows the pump intensity envelope and vanishes when the pump and probe no longer overlap, consistent with an instantaneous Kerr response. The phase shift scales linearly with pump intensity, yielding a nonlinear refractive index of $n_2=(0.87\pm0.16)\times10^{-17} m^2/W$. Our results establish the instantaneous Kerr effect as the dominant contribution to the measured nonlinear phase shift under our experimental conditions and demonstrate field-resolved pump-probe spectroscopy as a direct, time-resolved method for determining the nonlinear refractive index and distinguishing instantaneous from delayed optical responses.

Drying kinematics selects open honeycomb fold networks on solvent-extracted viscoelastic thin films and gels

Fan Zhao, Surjyasish Mitra, Sunandan Tandon, Zoya Leonenko, Boxin Zhao et al. (6 authors)

Solvent extraction and drying of substrate-supported viscoelastic films and gels produce quasiperiodic networks of three-armed surface folds. In this Letter, we examine the physical mechanisms governing their formation, length scales, and geometric organization. We observed that swelling-induced compression in the pinned film drives localized folding of a stiffening, diffusively thickening surface skin over a drained underlayer. Combining experiments and theory, we reveal that both the pattern wavelength and fold-arm length scale with the dried-film thickness following a power-law with an exponent 5/8, with remarkably weak dependence on elastic modulus. The folds form 120-degree junctions on a triangular lattice, defining an open honeycomb network whose pitch satisfies p = sqrt(3)(aL + g), where g is the junction gap. Notably, the folds accommodate less than 0.1% of the stored compressive strain, implicating elastocapillary arrest, rather than strain relaxation, in limiting their amplitude. These findings establish distinct geometric, kinetic, and interfacial constraints governing pattern selection in drying soft solids.

Current-driven transient dynamics of magnetic-octupole domain walls in noncollinear antiferromagnets

Myoung-Woo Yoo, Axel Hoffmann

Current-driven magnetic-octupole domain walls in noncollinear antiferromagnets are promising for fast spintronic devices, yet their transient dynamics are not yet fully understood. Here, we investigate spin-orbit-torque-driven domain-wall motion in Mn$_3$Sn using a multipole micromagnetic framework. The three symmetry-related 60° walls exhibit equivalent current-driven dynamics, unlike their field-driven motion. For a representative 60° wall, the post-pulse motion is governed by relaxation of the current-induced rotation of the surrounding octupole domains, with a characteristic time of approximately 33 ps quantitatively captured by a uniform-domain analytic model. By resolving spatially nonuniform texture dynamics beyond this analytic description, the simulations further reveal an additional slow relaxation in a composite 180° wall associated with the relative motion of its constituent walls. The post-pulse response also varies systematically with the exchange stiffness and magnetic anisotropies. These results identify distinct domain and internal-coordinate relaxation channels and establish the intrinsic time scales governing pulse-controlled positioning of magnetic-octupole domain walls in antiferromagnetic spintronic devices.

Generation and spectroscopic characterization of continuous-wave 148.4-nm light in strontium tetraborate

Ming Guan, Yang Wang, Heng Tu, Kaifgeng Zhou, Zhengrong Xiao et al. (11 authors)

Continuous-wave (CW) radiation at 148.4 nm is required to drive the $^{229}$Th isomeric transition of a solid-state nuclear clock, and frequency doubling of 296.8-nm light is its most direct source. We generate CW 148.4-nm light by random quasi-phase matching in strontium tetraborate and characterize it with a compact prism spectrometer built for the purpose. The spectrometer separates the harmonic from the pump on a CaF$_2$ prism, reads the wavelength from the rotation angle of a solar-blind photomultiplier, resolves the two lines with $δλ\approx 0.6 nm$, and yields an absolute power calibration. At room temperature the source delivers $1.9 nW$ at 148.4 nm, rising to 5.7 nW at the optimum crystal temperature. The conversion efficiency is also strongly structured in temperature: between 30 degree and 50 degree it shows several pronounced maxima, with neighbouring extrema only a few degrees apart, so that a small temperature excursion moves the output from a maximum to a minimum. These results provide practical guidance for optimizing and stabilizing 148.4-nm sources for solid-state $^{229}$Th nuclear clocks.

Tensor cluster expansion as an efficient surrogate for multi-ACE

Jacob Jeffries, Enrique Martinez, Eva Zarkadoula

In this work, we generalize an on-lattice tensor cluster expansion (TCE) framework to an arbitrary correlation order. We show that these generalized tensor cluster expansion features span a subspace of MACE features, suggesting that TCE features can sufficiently pre-screen MACE features for chemical diversity in alloyed transition metal dichalcogenide (TMD) monolayers. We show that, using maximum-entropy subset sampling, we can empirically recover chemical diversity from MACE features using a cheaper TCE representation. We then fine-tune a multi-ACE (MACE) potential with both subsets, and use this potential to predict temperature-dependent vibrational properties in alloyed TMD monolayers with varying chemical ordering. In our approach, TCE features can be used as cheap surrogate features for pre-screening chemically rich configurations using MACE features, allowing for the more efficient finetuning of atomic cluster expansion (ACE) potentials, thus achieving a tractable prediction of finite-temperature alloy thermodynamics on modest hardware.

Shock Loading Accesses Reaction Pathways beyond Accelerated Thermolysis

Mingjie Wen, Jiahe Han, Zihan Zhou, Hao Wang, Xueyong Guo et al. (7 authors)

Thermolysis and shock loading can produce similar major stable products in energetic materials, despite different chemical evolution and reaction pathways. Here, we develop EMFF-MACE, a reactive machine-learning potential for C/H/N/O energetic materials, enabling thermolysis simulations of 20 materials and shock simulations of 10 on a common potential energy surface. Comparisons at matched normalized reaction progress reveal divergent chemical states and local structural environments. Mean reaction-state similarity decreases from 94.0% at 20% progress to 25.9% at trajectory endpoints, while 47.1-92.6% of shock local environments lie outside corresponding material-specific thermolysis reference domains. Structural and reaction-network analyses reveal enhanced intermolecular reorganization and additional association and rearrangement branches under shock loading, despite partially shared early decomposition chemistry. These findings show that loading history influences the structural states and subsequent reaction pathways accessed during decomposition, rather than merely the rate of thermolysis.

Generative Design and Graph Neural Network Screening of Novel Bimetallic Alloy Catalysts for the Hydrogen Evolution Reaction

Vipin Kurian Elavunkel, Sylvain Le Tonquesse, Hadrien Vroylandt

Identifying earth-abundant catalysts for the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) is a central challenge in scalable green hydrogen production. We present a computational pipeline that couples diffusion-based generative modelling, machine-learning force-field relaxation, and graph neural network (GNN) property prediction to discover bimetallic HER catalysts beyond the structures and stoichiometries covered by existing adsorption databases. A generative model (MatterGen) is fine-tuned on bulk alloy structures labelled with their minimum hydrogen adsorption energy and steered towards adsorption energies near zero. From the generated pool we retain the stable structures that are novel bimetallic alloys: binary compositions built from the training-set metals but at stoichiometries or structures absent from that database. Each candidate's low-index facets are ranked by a machine-learning-force-field (MatterSim) surface-energy screen, and only the most stable terminations are converted into explicit slab models with hydrogen adsorbed at all high-symmetry surface sites and relaxed with the same force field. Two GNN surrogates (MEGNet and ALIGNN), trained on a high-throughput density functional theory (DFT) dataset, then screen the resulting slab candidates. Because the two models encode the local environment through different inductive biases, we retain only the alloys on which both agree, reducing the influence of any single model. This consensus screening, combined with a MatterSim energy-difference triangulation, resulted in a shortlist of near-thermoneutral bimetallic candidates. Eight are validated with plane-wave DFT, and four of them, Pd5Au, Pt5Rh, Cu5Pt3, and Pd13Ru3, bind hydrogen within the thermoneutral window. All eight alloys are dynamically stable by phonon calculations. These four alloys are highlighted as DFT-validated bimetallic HER candidates for experimental follow-up.

Second-order Bethe-Salpeter kernels: the interplay of vertex-corrected quasiparticle energies and the screened interaction

Paul van Hoegaerden, Arno Förster

We study second-order Bethe-Salpeter kernels generated by truncating an iterative formulation of Hedin's equations after the second cycle. Starting from the Hartree-Fock or the $GW$ self-energy, whose first cycles correspond to TDHF and $GW$+BSE, we obtain the second-cycle kernel by differentiating the first-cycle correlation self-energy with the interacting correlation function replaced by the non-interacting one, and evaluate it in the static limit of the effective kernel. Using fixed restricted Hartree-Fock orbitals, we benchmark 30 singlet and 28 triplet excitation energies for seven small molecules (six for the triplets) against the theoretical best estimates from QUEST v1.2. The second cycle lowers the mean absolute error (MAE) in both sequences. For the $GW$ sequence, the MAE decreases from 0.63 to 0.29 eV (singlets), on par with ADC(2) (0.30 eV), and from 0.43 to 0.38 eV (triplets). BSE$^{(2)}$@HF removes the triplet instabilities of TDHF and yields MAEs of 0.43 and 0.38 eV, or 0.31 and 0.30 eV when the Tamm-Dancoff approximation is applied in all cycles. Assigning the statically screened interaction $W_0$ to the exchange part of the $GW$ self-energy counts second-order screening twice in the second-cycle kernel and strongly blueshifts valence excitations. Without this double counting, dressing the exchange-type terms with $W_0$ lowers the singlet MAE from 0.37 to 0.29 eV but leaves the triplets nearly unchanged. Replacing second-order Green's function (GF2) quasiparticle energies by vertex-corrected ones in the same kernel raises excitation energies by about 0.7 eV, improving Rydberg but degrading valence excitations. A perturbative dynamical correction of the kernel blueshifts valence excitations by 0.3-0.4 eV and increases the errors of all full-matrix BSE$^{(2)}$ variants. The consistent improvement over the first cycle suggests that higher cycles are worth pursuing.
