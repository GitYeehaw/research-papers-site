Hierarchical World Models with Implicit Dynamics

Gaoyue Zhou, Yvonne Wu, Zichen Jeff Cui, Nicolas Ballas, Mido Assran et al. (7 authors)

Planning with world models enables test-time adaptation for embodied agents, offering superior generalization over direct policy learning. However, long-horizon tasks remain hindered by cumulative errors and search complexity. Existing hierarchical methods often use rigid macro-actions or latent skills, leading to brittle representations and limited fine-grained control. We introduce ID-WM (Implicit Dynamics World Models), a hierarchical world model that decouples state-space reachability from local control. Our framework utilizes a high-level model to sample feasible transitions across the state manifold and a low-level world model as an inverse dynamics solver to ground these transitions into precise actions. This allows the planner to identify reachable subgoals without being constrained by low-level execution details. By prioritizing local physical feasibility over global behavioral patterns, ID-WM synthesizes novel long-horizon plans from short segments, transcending the specific behavioral modes seen during training. We demonstrate its effectiveness across three navigation and manipulation suites, achieving a 38.5% improvement over state-of-the-art policy and diffusion-based planners. Crucially, by leveraging this hierarchical structure to mitigate compounding errors, our approach yields a 135% performance gain over single-level world model MPC, particularly in complex, unseen configurations for long-horizon tasks. Videos can be viewed at https://id-wm.github.io

RoboArchitect: Automating the Design of Robotic Agents with Persistent Memory

Shengguang Wu, Suning Huang, Cherie Ho, Ria Doshi, Hao Zhu et al. (6 authors)

Recent work shows that a frozen robot policy performs far better inside an agent harness, where control code decides when to query the policy, consult vision tools, or command the arm directly. Yet today these harnesses are built by hand and redesigned for each new robot or benchmark. We show that harness design can be automated, offering a strong alternative to finetuning on a novel setup. In RoboArchitect, a coding model observes a robot's rollouts, diagnoses failures, and iteratively revises the harness around the frozen policy. The harness evolves on a set of episodes for its optimization and is evaluated on held-out scenes and tasks. Without updating any model weights, RoboArchitect raises average success from 9.7% to 43.3% on unseen scenes (4.5x) and from 12.7% to 40.2% on unseen tasks (3.2x), across multiple benchmarks and policies. The gains are largest where the benchmark is far from the policy's training data: wrapping a general-purpose policy, the evolved harness yields a specialist that often outperforms even the finetuned version. Intuitive control strategies are automatically designed, such as lifting a jammed arm clear or reaching in horizontally when an obstacle blocks the way from above. The harness also evolves its own persistent memory, carrying learned experience, such as effective grasp depths, into later decisions. These results suggest that substantial headroom in robotic manipulation lies in the agent harness, and its design can be effectively automated and optimized without weight updates.

ROOF: Learning Humanoid Fall Recovery under Overhead Constraints

Yidan Lu, Jiayuan Yu, Zongrui Cai, Jingwen Sun, Jinrui Han et al. (8 authors)

A fallen humanoid beneath an overhead obstacle faces a coupled recovery problem. It must create clearance while organizing support for a stable rise, since attempting to stand before making room can further increase obstruction. We introduce a reinforcement learning method with contact-supported geometric escape guidance, which rewards incremental reduction of robot-obstacle overlap without prescribing an escape path. To achieve this, a learned motion prior, together with posture-dependent support and joint-stall costs, is proposed to shape a single policy that learns when to clear the obstacle, how to establish support, and when to complete the rise, all using proprioception alone. In simulation, it recovers in 88.5% of trials beneath plates that can only be lifted, compared with 18.9% without geometric guidance, and in 98.6% of trials beneath free plates with randomized size, mass and placement. On a Unitree G1, the deployed policy recovered in 95 of 150 attempts across seven real-world obstacle scenes, including furniture and multi-object arrangements absent from training. Project page: https://lu-yidan.github.io/ROOF/

CausalWAM: Learning the Causes of Actions in World Action Models

Juntao Cheng, Jinzhou Tang, Fan Feng, Henrik I. Christensen, Yujia Zheng

World action models (WAMs) learn latent variables from observations and use these to both predict the evolution of the scene and to generate actions. The prediction has to be of the whole scene, whereas only a portion of the scene causes actions. WAMs fitted to demonstrations do not, in general, guarantee that they will generate actions solely from the latent causes of the demonstrated actions (the action-relevant latent variables), as the model could just as well learn to imitate in this way whilst entangling the action-relevant latent variables with the nuisance variables. We therefore separate out what causes action from what prediction depends on. We prove that under a sparsity constraint the latent variables used to generate actions are an invertible function of the action-relevant latent variables alone and the true structure is identified up to a permutation of the latent variables. This requires neither sufficient temporal dependence nor any assumptions on how the unobserved regimes evolve and holds even when the action-relevant latent variables are statistically dependent on the rest. We therefore propose CWAM (Causal WAM), which incorporates the constraint into a pretrained WAM by adding a sparse routing path to its action head, with the world modeling objective remaining unchanged. Experiments on both synthetic data and real-world robots validate the theory as well as showcase the improved efficiency in closed-loop success.

Measurement-Based Assessment of Decadal Inertia and Primary Frequency Response Trends in the US Eastern Interconnection

Saurav Dulal, Mark Baldwin, Narges Ghiasi, Mohammed Olama, Ali R. Ekti et al. (7 authors)

Changes in the generation mix of the U.S. Eastern Interconnection (EI) raise questions about long-term frequency performance, particularly inertia and primary frequency response (PFR). This paper jointly examines these characteristics using synchronized FNET/GridEye frequency measurements and confirmed generation-trip events from 2013 to 2025. A representative EI frequency trajectory is constructed using the median of geographically distributed measurements. Effective inertia is estimated quarterly from the early post-disturbance rate of change of frequency (RoCoF), using a 3-second window and least-squares fitting. PFR is evaluated using the beta metric, which measures aggregate frequency response from disturbance size and the A-to-B frequency deviation. Annual beta estimates are obtained after filtering events by starting frequency and disturbance size to improve comparability across years. Results show that EI effective inertia varies seasonally and follows a non-monotonic trend rather than a uniform long-term decline. Over the same period, coal generation decreased while natural gas, wind, and solar generation increased. Changes in synchronous generator commitment and operating conditions may have offset some expected inertia reductions. Filtered beta generally increased from the mid-2010s to the early 2020s, with year-to-year variability thereafter. The different trends show that early inertial behavior and broader aggregate frequency response have not evolved in parallel. Joint analysis of inertia and beta offers a more complete measurement-based assessment of EI frequency performance during the generation transition.

REA: A Routed Embodied Agent Runtime for Asynchronous Brain-Cerebellum Control

Pengfei Zhou, Yixing Ma, Zhengfeiyang Zhang, Haoyuan Chen, Zhenglin Wan et al. (8 authors)

Foundation models are becoming remarkably reliable at high-level reasoning, yet they remain too slow and costly to serve as fine-grained controllers. Conversely, fast policies like VLAs and whole-body controllers offer more efficient reflexes, but struggle to generalize. While pairing a deliberative brain (foundation models) with a reactive cerebellum (fast policies) seems natural for embodied systems, two obstacles remain: methods are built and scored on incompatible stacks, and their asynchronous coordination is difficult. For example, an agent that calls the VLA in the agentic loop can stall the fast execution, while a VLA that calls the agent on errors tends to re-trigger fallbacks repeatedly or completely miss silent failures. To address this, we propose REA (Routed Embodied Agent), a unified runtime where a deliberative System 2, an embodied router System 1 and reactive System 0 policies cooperate under a single golden rule - no running policy waits on the brain. Instead of querying the brain at each failure, REA employs a route memory that stores each policy's measured success ahead of time, a boundary-triggered judge gate that consults the brain on policy execution, and a shared-budget escalation ladder for bounded recovery. Evaluated on 3,000 test episodes across 60 LIBERO-Pro tasks, REA reaches 95.9% success and beats the best single-agent baseline in its own policy pool (94.7%) while reducing control steps by 25% and control time by 17%. In a separate 100-episode RoboTwin 2.0 study, REA achieves 84% success with VLA policies and 86% with VLA+code, compared with 80% for the strongest single VLA baseline MEDex. These results demonstrate learned and programmatic policies operating within the same runtime. REA also transfers to BEHAVIOR-1K and a real Unitree G1 humanoid, achieving the highest observed success among the compared methods.

A Hybrid Path Planning Framework for Assistive Wheelchair Navigation in Dynamic Pedestrian Environments

Damla Leblebicioğlu, Rachel Barume, Taşkın Padır

Autonomous wheelchairs can improve the quality of life for individuals with mobility impairments by providing greater independence. However, reliable navigation especially in human environments is a major challenge due to factors such as dynamic obstacles, unpredictable pedestrian behavior, and the users' need to complete multiple stops along the way before reaching their final destination. This paper presents a hybrid path planning framework for multi-goal navigation in dynamic environments. The framework integrates the Traveling Salesman Problem (TSP) for global task ordering with the Potential Field (PF) method and heuristic local planners such as Rapidly-exploring Random Tree (RRT) and A-star (A*) to achieve collision-free navigation. Within this hybrid framework, TSP determines the visiting order of intermediate stations, PF transfers global path information, and local planners avoid obstacles while guiding the robot to the stations. The local planners generate feasible paths that allow the robot to follow the global trajectory and reroute to avoid static and dynamic obstacles. For this purpose, two algorithms have been developed: (i) TSP-PF-RRT and (ii) TSP-PF-A*. The performance of the proposed algorithms are evaluated using Monte Carlo simulations in crowded and extra-crowded scenarios that contain heterogeneous dynamic and static obstacles. Experimental results demonstrate that the proposed hybrid framework enables efficient multi-goal navigation while maintaining safe operation in dynamic environments with humans, with TSP-PF-RRT achieving fewer or no collisions and shorter travel distances.

System-Level Factors Influencing AGC Cyber-Attack Success and RAS Vulnerability in Multi-Area Grids

Abdulmannan Ajabnoor, Zhi Jin Zhang, Maryam Saeedifard, Dominic Groß

Automatic generation control (AGC) stabilizes frequency and scheduled power exchanges among interconnected areas, yet its reliance on communication networks introduces cyber-vulnerabilities. This work examines coordinated AGC cyber-attacks in the presence of remedial action schemes (RAS) and identifies operating conditions under which an attacker can drive grid frequency beyond safe limits while evading RAS. A feedback-based attack policy is used to manipulate AGC signals and steer system frequency toward unsafe trajectories. Then, a large nested-loop parametric sweep---spanning attacked-area inertia relative to its interconnected neighboring area, AGC capacity versus maximum tie-line transfer, AGC update rate, load damping, and bi-directional power transfers---generates over 1.45 million scenarios. These are classified using a decision tree to establish guidelines to distinguish successful from unsuccessful attacks based on frequency deviations. The resulting vulnerability guidelines are validated on a three-area system using phasor-domain simulations with IEEE 9-bus area models, detailed turbine--governor dynamics, and under/over-frequency load and generation shedding. Results show that specific combinations of AGC capacity, inertia distribution, and pre-attack tie-line loading increase the likelihood of successful attacks, offering operators actionable criteria for identifying vulnerable operating conditions.

Proof-Carrying Evolution: Black-Box Search, White-Box Flight

Amit Jain, Sergio Sanchez-Hurtado, Victor Rodriguez-Fernandez, Richard Linares

We study whether a language model can help design controllers whose behavior can be formally checked. In proof-carrying evolution (PCE), a search proposes controllers together with their certificates. Their mathematical forms are fixed; a proposer chooses their numerical parameters together. Certificate checks guide selection, and only a certified candidate can be returned. The deployed artifact is a control law and its certificate; the language model is used only during design. We compare four language models with classical search and synthesis on docking under a speed limit, pursuit of a maneuvering target, and attitude control on SO(3), using paired runs and shared proposal allowances. Certificates are checked in exact rational arithmetic during search on the linear problems. On SO(3), the search uses floating-point checks, followed by exact re-verification of every reported champion. The best run of each method also receives Lean 4 proofs of its stated control properties. On the linear problems, the strongest models beat the standard classical baselines on every run, but a classical optimizer that chooses certificates for the same objective closes most of that lead. Among the cited pursuit champions, the ranking does not survive a change from Euler to zero-order-hold dynamics. On SO(3), no model detectably outperforms CMA-ES when mixed with classical mutation. When models write every proposal, one tested model improves on CMA-ES and the other performs worse than its mixed variant. All search medians remain below the hand-tuned law. In a docking control with the objective and mutation operator held fixed, retaining certified candidates yields new certified designs in every run; removing this rule yields none.

A Neural Network Approach towards Real-Time Knot Point Density Estimates for the Nonlinear Quadrotor

Jasmine Ratchford, Raffaele Romagnoli, Mark H. Klein

Spline representations provide a compact way to approximate continuous trajectories using a finite number of parameters. When approximating trajectories generated by dynamical systems, the efficiency of a spline representation depends strongly on the placement of knot points. Uniform knot distributions often lead to inefficient representations, requiring either unnecessary control points or producing poor approximations in regions where the trajectory varies rapidly. In this work we propose a learning-based method for adaptive knot placement in B-spline approximations of trajectories generated by ordinary differential equations. A neural network learns a mapping from the initial condition of the system to quantities that characterize the local variation of the resulting trajectory. These estimates are used to construct nonuniform knot distributions that approximately equidistribute a local error indicator, reducing the number of control points needed to meet a prescribed accuracy.
