Generic finiteness of solutions to parametrized systems of generalized polynomial inequalities

Stefan Müller

We study parametrized systems of $\mathit{generalized}$ polynomial inequalities, $(c \circ x^B) \in C$, for a positive variable vector $x \in \mathbb{R}^n_>$, a $\mathit{real}$ exponent matrix $B \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}$, a positive parameter vector $c \in \mathbb{R}^m_>$, and a positive, blunt coefficient cone $C \subset \mathbb{R}^m_>$. This setting includes (i) generalized polynomial equations, $A \, (c \circ x^B) = 0$, arising e.g. from power-law dynamical systems with real exponents (and involving a real coefficient matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n' \times m}$), and (ii) generalized semi-algebraic sets. Our main result guarantees the generic finiteness of solutions modulo unavoidable exponential fibers. Building on a framework for parametrized $\mathit{polynomial}$ systems developed by Müller and Regensburger, we reformulate the problem as a system of $\mathit{binomial}$ equations on the coefficient set $P = C \cap Δ\subseteq \mathbb{R}^m_>$, where $Δ$ is a product of (standard) simplices. Solvability is governed by the monomial-dependency subspace $D$. Using elementary arguments from analysis and topology, including a logarithmic boundary analysis and Sard's theorem, we control the accumulation of solutions on the boundary and in the interior of $P$. We show that, if $\dim P \le \dim D$, then generically in both the measure-theoretic and topological sense, there are finitely many solutions on $P$ (and hence finitely many exponential fibers of solutions in $\mathbb{R}^n_>$). As an application, we prove generic finiteness of steady states (within forward-invariant sets) for parametrized power-law dynamical systems with real exponents.

What I Disbelieve: Quantifying Belief With Negative Ranking Functions

Hanbin Go, Britt Anderson

A challenge in exploring the psychology of human uncertainty is its measurement and quantification. Prior work has focused almost exclusively on probability, while the formal theories developed in philosophy are rarely tested. We tested Wolfgang Spohn's ranking theory, a normative account in which belief is graded by ordinal ranks of disbelief that combine by minimization rather than addition, as a descriptive model of human belief. Across six experiments, participants assigned ranks of disbelief to propositions involving personal experience, chance devices, sequential learning, and an Ellsberg urn. Ranks were reliable and, through the theory's two-sided rank function, distinguished belief, disbelief, and suspension of judgment. Participants respected the Law of Negation, never disbelieving both a proposition and its complement, in every task except a lottery. Disjunctions did not follow the theory's Law of Disjunction. Instead of taking the minimum rank of the disjuncts, participants averaged them. In a learning task, ranks aligned more closely than did subjective probabilities with objective probabilities. In an Ellsberg Urn scenario, the paradoxical behavior seen with betting was markedly reduced by ranks. We conclude that human belief has the bipolar, negation-respecting structure that ranking theory proposes, but combines disjuncts by averaging rather than by minimization. As a clear account of human belief dynamics with metrics that can be reliably measured, Ranking Theory provides a valuable framework for investigating and contrasting probabilistic and non-probabilistic accounts of human belief.

An exact coexistence criterion for stochastic Lotka--Volterra systems with Volterra--Lyapunov stable interactions

Jorge Ramirez Osorio

We consider stochastic Lotka-Volterra systems $dX=Diag(X)(α+AX)dt+Diag(X)ΓdB$ with nondegenerate noise and a Volterra-Lyapunov stable interaction matrix $A$, that is, $DA+A^\top D\prec 0$ for some positive diagonal matrix $D$. This class covers every self-limited predator-prey food web that is conservative in the sense of Volterra, in particular every such web whose interaction graph is a forest. We give a necessary and sufficient condition for invariant coexistence: the process has an invariant probability measure on $(0,\infty)^n$, that is, a stationary distribution under which all species have positive densities, if and only if $m=-A^{-1}β$ is positive, where $β=α-\frac{1}{2}diag(ΓΓ^\top)$ is the vector of noise-corrected growth rates. This measure is then unique and ergodic, has mean $m$, and attracts every interior initial condition exponentially fast in total variation. Thus environmental noise affects coexistence only through the Itô shift $α\mapstoβ$, and the criterion is the exact counterpart of the feasibility criterion for the deterministic system $\dot x=Diag(x)(β+Ax)$. Both directions rest on the fact that, for Lotka-Volterra systems, invasion rates are affine in the mean of the underlying measure. Necessity follows from an exact formula for the means of invariant measures supported on faces of the orthant. Sufficiency follows from the persistence theorem of Hening and Nguyen, for which the explicit weights $Dm$ give a stochastic counterpart of Hofbauer's average Lyapunov function, so that no enumeration of the invariant measures on the boundary is required. Applied to subcommunities, the result also characterizes every stationary regime in which some species are absent.

BARRR: Bidimensional Augmented Reduced Rank Regression for Multi-View Multi-Cohort Data

Jiuzhou Wang, Eric F. Lock

Bidimensional data integration across multiple cohorts (i.e., sample groups) and views (i.e., sources or modalities) is crucial in biomedical research to derive holistic insights from heterogeneous datasets. However, most existing integrative methods focus on a single dimension, such as multi-view integration across distinct sources or multi-cohort integration across distinct sample groups. Existing approaches for bidimensional integration are scarce and predominantly unsupervised, overlooking the valuable insights offered by covariates. To address this gap, we introduce the Bidimensional Augmented Reduced Rank Regression (baRRR) method. Our approach models covariate-related and unrelated effects concurrently, while also decomposing effect levels (global, partially shared, and individual) across both dimensions. Extensive simulations demonstrate the advantages of our method with respect to signal recovery, outcome prediction, and missing data imputation. We apply our approach to data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), using somatic mutations as covariates to inform patterns of variability across different omics platforms and cancer types. These studies highlight its potential as a robust and adaptable tool for integrative analysis in biomedical research.

Reading Between the Reads: A Decade On, Revisiting Sequencing Protocol Annotation in the Sequence Read Archive

Jamie J. Alnasir

Background: Experimental metadata are essential for interpreting archived sequencing data. In 2015, Alnasir and Shanahan examined the annotation of fragmentation, adapter ligation and enrichment in the Sequence Read Archive (SRA). This study revisits those measurements using the original SQL keyword definitions on a September 2026 NCBI metadata snapshot reconstructed in the historical SRAdb schema. Results: The contemporary database contained 41,593,302 experiments, compared with 419,620 in 2013. In library_construction_protocol, the proportion matching all three historical keyword groups declined from 4.06% to 1.42%. The protocol field was NULL in 67.65% of contemporary experiments. Searching design_description alongside the protocol field increased combined three-step keyword detection to 1,197,367 experiments (2.88%), with most additional matches occurring where the protocol field was NULL. Coverage varied across sequencing library strategies, ranging from 0.26% for AMPLICON to 7.69% for RNA-Seq. Independent comparison of 150 sampled experiment records against source XML confirmed agreement in all historical keyword classifications. Conclusions: The original criteria identify a smaller proportion of experiments in the designated protocol field than a decade earlier. Additional preparation-related terminology is detectable in general experiment descriptions, exposing the limitations of searches restricted to one metadata field. Differences among sequencing strategies complicate historical comparisons as preparation methods evolve. The findings support more consistent protocol annotation and the need to distinguish keyword occurrence from the adequacy of experimental documentation.

Does a Flux-Defined Markov Blanket Screen Self-Replicating Gray--Scott Spots? A Computational Test of a Blanket Condition from the Free Energy Principle

John E Pearson

Friston's free energy principle holds that a system separated from its environment by a Markov blanket---a boundary whose states statistically screen its interior from its exterior---can be treated as performing approximate Bayesian inference, and that such blankets arise wherever coupling is short-range. We test this blanket condition in a concrete nonlinear pattern-forming system: self-replicating spots in a reaction-diffusion model that persist, compete, and reproduce. Directed boundary exchange and timescale separation hold up reasonably well, with qualifications. A flux-defined blanket does not screen interior from exterior. Flux removes only part of the linear dependence, and a nonlinear test shows that conditioning on flux increases the dependence, consistent with conditioning on a collider, since flux is a joint function of concentrations on both sides of the boundary. The dependence is concentrated within about one organism radius of the boundary. A band of the surrounding field that thick screens the interior from the exterior beyond it, as any short-range field would, which is not evidence of inference.

Magnolia: A Persistent-Memory Agent Framework for Computational Chemistry

Tao Jiang, Benjamin Navet, Yousouf Taghzouti, Fabien Gandon, Louis-Félix Nothias et al. (6 authors)

Chemistry has recently entered a high-data regime as molecular databases from high-throughput platforms or computational studies have become available. Increasing automation within the chemical sciences leads to a more digital and precision chemistry. Dedicated scientific agents are becoming central to guiding the chemist through an ever-increasing amount of data and findings, including negative ones. Here, we introduce Magnolia, a persistent-memory artificial intelligence agent framework that integrates computational protocols, from docking to quantum chemistry, lab notebooks and chat logs relying on large language models (LLMs). It relies on a three-tier memory system: session-level observations are automatically captured and distilled into structured learnings, confirmed and elevated to project-tier entries and, when human-validated, promoted to cross-project rules that actively guide subsequent work and provide direct chemical feedback. We illustrate Magnolia on four case studies: quantum-chemical evaluation of aromatic systems (COMPAS database), molecular dynamics of butane and of modified DNA, and a protein-peptide docking campaign. Across these projects, Magnolia tracked 317 runs and accumulated 116 project-tier entries, from which four cross-project rules were promoted; in controlled replays of four tasks, runs with memory passed the checks that runs without it failed (for example, ten of ten docking-configuration checks, against one to four of ten). By combining automation, reproducibility, and human-in-the-loop control, our Magnolia framework streamlines docking, molecular dynamics and quantum chemistry calculations with direct analysis and LLM-augmented feedback, offering accelerated access to computational (bio)chemistry.

Caterpillar trees are asymmetric, balanced trees are symmetric

Kalle Leppälä

Two conjectures of Allman, Long and Rhodes are proven. The ultrametric exchangeable model captures the symmetries in allele distributions of ultrametric mechanistic models of coalescence and substitution in mathematical phylogeny. The dimension of this model with respect to the species tree leaf count is shown to be greatest for caterpillar trees and least for balanced trees.

Cross-disorder Learning of Imaging-derived Joint Mophometric Markers in Alzheimer's and Parkinson's Diseases

Yuji Zhao, Boris Gutman

As two most prevalent neurodegenerative diseases (NDDs), Alzheimer's disease (AD) and Parkinson's disease (PD) shared commonalities both in symptomology and pathophysiology. However, to what extent and in what way these two distinct disorders overlap remain underexplored. In this study, we propose an explicit, interpretable and spatially adaptive transfer learning framework to elucidate the morphometric biomarkers linked to AD and PD. We applied such high-dimensional machine learning model to two most popular open-source neuroimaging datasets, i.e., Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) and Parkinson's Progression Markers Initiative (PPMI). On the one hand, our cross-disorder classifier revealed neuro-anatomical alteration patterns well aligning with previous neuroimaging research findings and underscoring patients' clinical presentations. On the other hand, the spatially adaptive trained classifier using shared prior achieved statistically superior diagnostic performance compared to respective individual models, which further highlights the cross-disorder framework's efficacy on modeling limited-size datasets across disease domains.

Representing short-term movement to reveal animal identity and learning progress

Ping Zhang, Quang-Huy Nguyen, Yingtie Lei, Sameera Shridhar, Zachary Leveroni et al. (8 authors)

How learning progresses over time and varies across individuals is a longstanding question in cognitive and behavioral science. However, a major challenge is identifying observable behavioral measures that faithfully capture learning progression. Existing approaches commonly rely on predefined behavioral features or task-level performance, while fine-scale posture and movement in freely behaving animals remain less explored. In this paper, we ask: can short-term movement trajectories reveal learning progress and individual identity? We introduce a self-supervised representation learning framework built on a simple hypothesis: behavioral characteristics associated with identity and learning remain relatively stable over nearby time intervals. Using temporal proximity as supervision, our framework learns compact representations directly from movement trajectories without manually defining behavioral states or kinematic features. Using behavior data from freely behaving rats, we show that the learned representations not only improve prediction of identity and learning-related readouts over existing methods, but also provide a tool for investigating broader scientific questions about when and how these signals appear in behavior. Specifically, trajectories as short as one second contain informative learning and identity signals. Egocentric pose dynamics are more predictive of identity, whereas allocentric motion is more predictive of learning-related variation, and both signals are strongest at decision junctions in the alternation cohort. We further use a representation-conditioned diffusion model to inspect movement patterns associated with identity and learning progress. Our findings establish short-term movement as an informative behavioral "fingerprint" for studying learning and individual differences. Our code is available at https://github.com/pizhn/TRAIL.
