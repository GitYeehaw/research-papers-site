Deep LLM layers expose passage-level context in EEG during silent reading

Ingo Marquardt, Anthilia Alchanat, Priyanka Jain

In a previous report we showed that a decoder can recover open-vocabulary word-level information from 19-channel EEG recorded during silent reading, by aligning short EEG windows with hidden-state embeddings of the presented word taken from a large language model (LLM). That analysis contrasted two of the model's 33 layers: the context-free input embedding and one mid-depth layer. Here we ask how EEG-decodable structure is distributed over the full depth of the language model. We trained one decoder per layer of Llama-3.1-8B, with and without a causal transformer over the sequence of EEG windows. We distinguish word-level decodability from a context-tracking component. Word-level decodability peaks in early layers and declines steadily towards the last layer. In contrast, the context-tracking component of the retrieval gain rises with depth, both with and without a causal transformer. Unexpectedly, a decoder that sees a single 0.5-s window at a time tracks the text passage too. Two new controls dissect this component. A target-geometry control, a synthetic predictor made of the true target plus noise calibrated to each decoder's word-level accuracy, shows that context tracking is not a by-product of word decoding and target geometry. A control that permutes predictions across runs from either the same or from different sessions shows that most of the observed context-tracking is not caused by an EEG recording session signature. Instead, context tracking mostly follows the text. A single EEG window therefore carries passage-level information that a contextual target can absorb. The results locate word identity in the early layers of the language model, discourse state in the deeper layers, and make the choice of target layer a design dial for EEG language decoders.

Distributional signatures of computation: inferring mechanisms from computation time distributions

Céline van Valkenhoef, Catherine Schuman, Nikolaus Kriegeskorte, Philip Walther

Computational complexity theory provides a framework for analysing abstract models of computation, whereas computational systems operate as finite physical implementations whose behaviour is shaped by their architecture and physical substrate. In such systems, computational organisation cannot be directly characterised from algorithms or internal representations. Building on the use of reaction time distributions to constrain mechanisms in cognitive science, we introduce an empirical framework for investigating computation in physical systems by inferring computational mechanisms from observable computation time distributions. The framework combines empirical benchmarking, which characterises computational efficiency through the scaling of computation time with problem size, with distributional analysis, which treats computation time distributions as signatures of the stochastic dynamics generated by different computational mechanisms. We demonstrate the framework using a scalable visual search paradigm, comparing human performance with three computational models that employ distinct mechanisms of evidence accumulation. Benchmarking reveals broadly similar scaling behaviour for the models and human observers, whereas distributional analysis distinguishes between these models. We show that computation time distributions provide an empirical level of description linking observable computational behaviour to underlying computational mechanisms. This framework complements computational complexity theory by extending the empirical study of computation from measuring computational performance to characterising computational organisation. It provides an empirical approach for comparing finite computational systems across diverse physical implementations, including biological, classical, neuromorphic, and quantum systems, without requiring access to their internal algorithms or architectures.

Heterogeneity among and competition between life stages together extend the stability boundary of complex ecological communities

Grace Garmire, James P. O'Dwyer

Classical models of pairwise species interactions consistently predict that large, complex communities are inherently unstable, yet the observed persistence of diverse communities highlights a central contradiction in theoretical ecology. While foundational frameworks often model species as homogeneous populations, one theoretical resolution lies in including internal species structure. In this work, we incorporate explicit demographic stage structure into a generalized Lotka-Volterra framework to evaluate how ontogenetic changes throughout the life cycle of a species can affect whole-community stability. We show that stage-dependence of inter-specific interactions can significantly expand the stable parameter space for a species network. Furthermore, this enhanced stability requires strong competition between life stages of a species. Our study illustrates that stage structure with population heterogeneity expands classical stability criteria, providing a mathematical foundation for understanding how ontogeny modifies the classically derived complexity boundaries for local asymptotic stability.

Multiplayer games on regular graphs under aspiration-driven imitation: Fixation probabilities and the dilution principle

Dhaker Kroumi

We investigate how cooperation evolves in multiplayer games on regular graphs of degree $k$ when individuals base their decisions on both their own aspiration levels and the success of their neighbors. We calculate explicit first-order formulas for fixation probabilities using pair approximation and diffusion methods under weak selection. These formulas separate aspiration effects from payoff effects filtered by the graph. We show that increasing the graph degree attenuates the selection effects driven by payoffs. In contrast, the contribution of the aspiration gap $Δα=α_D-α_C$ is independent of graph degree at first order. We apply the framework to public goods, stag-hunt, and snowdrift games and obtain corresponding conditions for the evolutionary success of cooperation. In the public goods and stag-hunt games, the conditions at large degree retain cost terms, so cooperation requires a sufficiently positive aspiration gap to compensate for the cost of contributing. In the snowdrift game, both benefit and cost contributions vanish in the locally tree-like regime at large degree, leaving the aspiration gap as the leading first-order contribution. Monte Carlo simulations on connected random regular graphs of degree $k$ are consistent with the first-order predictions within their formal regime of validity, while simulations outside this regime are interpreted only as qualitative checks of the predicted trends.

The nearest neighbor interchange proximity measure for the tree-basedness of rooted binary phylogenetic networks

Martin Frohn, Takatora Suzuki

Tree-based networks are a broad graph class in phylogenetics which posit a model of evolution composed of tree-like processes and hybridization events. Hence, for any non-tree-based phylogenetic network, it is meaningful to study the magnitude of violation of the tree-based model assumptions. For rooted networks, we introduce the proximity measure delta_rNNI, defined by the minimum number of rooted nearest neighbor interchanges (NNIs) required to transform a network into a tree-based network. For binary networks, we prove that delta_rNNI is pairwise distinct from all previously known proximity measures in the literature and that no approximation guarantees between delta_rNNI and other known proximity measures holds. In addition, we characterize a family of networks for which a linear time lower bound on delta_rNNI is tight. Moreover, we analyze upper bounds and give an exact solution algorithm to compute delta_rNNI.

Stationary analysis of adaptive aspiration dynamics in graph-structured games

Dhaker Kroumi

Aspiration dynamics can favor cooperation on finite graphs through a purely local satisfaction mechanism. In the model studied here, individuals compare their payoffs with adaptive aspiration levels and revise their strategies through a self-switching Fermi rule. Although the aspirations are continuous and endogenous, the resulting finite-population process has a unique stationary distribution for every finite selection intensity and every finite connected graph under degree-normalized average payoffs when restricted to the compact forward-invariant state space in which aspirations lie in the payoff interval. In the weak-selection regime, the first-order stationary abundance shift is positive when \(R+S>T+P\), negative when \(R+ST+P\) means that, under neutrality, the expected payoff of a cooperator exceeds that of a defector. The stationary variance reveals a different effect whose first-order correction is governed by the payoff-range contrast \((R-S)-(T-P)\). This contrast compares how strongly cooperator and defector payoffs respond to changes in local neighborhood composition and therefore measures the relative sensitivity of their payoffs to the local environment. Under degree-normalized average payoffs, these first-order results hold on every finite connected graph and are independent of degree sequence, topology, and aspiration learning rate. Simulations on regular and heterogeneous graphs compare averaged and accumulated payoffs and illustrate that graph heterogeneity and the aspiration learning rate can affect stationary behavior away from weak selection. The first-order coefficients are derived from the finite stochastic process and do not use pair approximation, mean-field closure, or a large-population limit.

Fundamental Bounds on the Oscillation Efficiency of Markov Networks

Chen Jia

Stochastic oscillations are commonly identified through peaks in their power spectra, yet the fundamental limits on their strength and energetic cost remain unclear. Here we establish universal dynamical and thermodynamic bounds on oscillation efficiency in Markov networks, showing how finite state spaces constrain spectral peak enhancement. In particular, we demonstrate that networks maximizing modal coherence need not maximize oscillation efficiency. We further derive dissipation bounds that connect oscillation efficiency to the relaxation speed and stationary probabilities, revealing an unavoidable energetic cost of maintaining pronounced oscillations at a specified relaxation timescale. These bounds remain strictly positive in regimes where existing explicit spectral dissipation estimates vanish, with stronger constraints for normal networks and uniform rings. Our results distinguish spectral oscillatory strength from modal coherence and reveal how network dynamics and thermodynamic dissipation jointly limit stochastic oscillations.

EEG Lempel-Ziv Complexity vis-à-vis Autocorrelation Function

Pedro Montoya, Ignacio Cifre, José Luis Rosselló Sanz, Dante R. Chialvo

Measures of signal complexity have become increasingly popular in electroencephalography (EEG), where they are often interpreted as indicators of neural information processing, dynamical richness, or conscious state. In contrast, classical measures of temporal dependence, such as the autocorrelation function (ACF), remain more directly linked to known physiological mechanisms and are easier to interpret. The present work revisits, for the EEG practicioner, the relation between normalized Lempel-Ziv complexity (LZC) and temporal autocorrelation in both synthetic and physiological EEG signals. First, synthetic time series exhibiting a broad range of temporal structure and complexity levels were generated using stochastic processes with varying degrees of temporal dependence. LZC and autocorrelation measures were then computed and compared. Across simulated signals, a robust inverse relationship was observed between normalized LZC and the first autocorrelation coefficient, indicating that signals with stronger temporal correlations exhibit lower complexity estimates. This relationship also holds in resting-state EEG recordings from healthy adult participants across channels and subjects, as well as dynamically during eyes open/close maneuvers. Overall, these results show that the information captured by LZC reflects temporal redundancy already quantified by conventional autocorrelation measures. Because autocorrelation measures relate more directly to established neurophysiological mechanisms, including oscillatory persistence, network synchronization, and temporal integration, these results suggest that interpreting complexity-based EEG findings should explicitly account for their dependence on conventional temporal statistics, which may provide a more transparent framework for mechanistic inferences.

Multiscale Quantum-Classical Simulation of Protein Autoproteolysis in a Million-Atom Membrane Environment

Alexander Orlov, Alexis Ralli, Tim Weaving, Michael Williams de la Bastida, Reuben Martin et al. (14 authors)

Predicting chemical reactions in biomolecules is challenging because reactive centers are embedded within complex environments that strongly influence their energetics and mechanisms. Here, we present a hybrid quantum-classical workflow that combines molecular dynamics, density functional theory, and correlated wavefunction methods, tightly integrating CPUs, GPUs, and quantum processing units (QPUs) to enable simulations across multiple length and accuracy scales. We apply this approach to ADGRG1 autoproteolysis, a maturation reaction in an adhesion G protein-coupled receptor that has not previously been studied using correlated electronic-structure methods. Our simulations treat a biologically realistic system containing more than one million atoms, including the full-length uncleaved receptor embedded in an endoplasmic-reticulum membrane, while incorporating a 54-qubit IQM quantum processor and up to 1,200 NVIDIA H100 GPUs. These results demonstrate how quantum computers can serve as accelerators within scalable scientific workflows, extending correlated electronic-structure methods to biologically relevant systems.

What Shapes Brain Alignment of Large Language Models during Dialogue? Insights from Language Production and Comprehension

Yuko Nakagi, Yuya Chiba, Yasue Kishino

Brain alignment of large language models (LLMs) offers a way to investigate representational similarities between LLMs and the human brain during language processing. While previous studies have mainly focused on passive language comprehension, little is known about such alignment during dialogue, in which speakers engage in both language production and comprehension. In this study, using fMRI data collected during dialogue, we examine brain alignment during language production and comprehension across LLM layers and brain regions. We assess its association with the intrinsic dimension (ID) of LLM representations and the contributions of several interpretable linguistic and communicative features. Our results reveal distinct patterns of brain alignment between production and comprehension, as well as strong associations with ID in brain regions associated with each processing mode. Furthermore, we show that the contributions of linguistic and communicative features also vary across processing modes, LLM layers, and brain regions.
