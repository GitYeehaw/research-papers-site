Agentic World Action Models

Zhuoyang Liu, Dantong Niu, Botong Zhang, Boning Shao, Jing Wang et al. (18 authors)

Recent world action models provide visual foresight and future prediction for robot manipulation, but lack explicit mechanisms for compositional skill reuse. Agentic robotics combines code-based planning and reasoning with reusable skills, but often lacks predictive grounding in physical dynamics. We introduce AW-0, an agentic world action model framework that integrates code-based planning with joint video and action prediction. We jointly model code, future video, and robot actions as three coupled generative streams within a unified mixture-of-transformers architecture. Within this architecture a code expert generates parameterized skill routines and control logic while video and action experts generate future visual states and low-level motor commands. Code serves two complementary roles: it structures multi-step prediction and control within the world model and represents skills as reusable programs for subsequent agentic execution. We further introduce an automated curation pipeline that labels robot and human video trajectories with aligned programs and consolidates recurring routines into reusable skill libraries. Evaluations on RoboTwin 2.0 and physical bimanual manipulation tasks show improved task performance over VLA and WAM baselines, alongside gains in language following, semantic reasoning and robustness, demonstrating that agentic code enables connection between planning, prediction and execution within WAMs.

PhysiCalWorld: In-Context Physics Calibration for World Models

Yunhao Luo, Wentao He, Yinpei Dai, Hyelin Nam, Hongze Fu et al. (8 authors)

Existing world models typically perceive a scene through current visual frame or a narrow history window. However, these observations may not reveal the physics properties that determine transition dynamics, such as an object's mass distribution, joint damping, or surface friction. Without calibrating these physics factors for world models, predictions tend to regress toward an empirical prior and induce severe drift in novel test scenes. To this end, we propose PhysiCalWorld, which calibrates action-conditioned world models to the instance-specific dynamics via in-context prompting, without fine-tuning or analytical physics modeling. Our method encodes an exploratory interaction clip collected within the test scene into a compact physics descriptor token, which grounds subsequent predictions in the scene's actual physical behavior. PhysiCalWorld is trained end-to-end with the latent prediction loss alone, without physics labels. At inference time, the descriptor token is extracted once and can be reused across rollouts with negligible compute overhead. We evaluate PhysiCalWorld across four environments covering diverse physics variations and show that it substantially improves rollout prediction accuracy and visual planning success rate. The learned descriptors also form a physically structured latent space and generalize beyond the physics ranges seen during training.

CW-WAM: A Structured Contact-Wrench World Action Model for Force-Sensitive Manipulation

Houzhu Ding, Xinda Qi, Luqi Wang, Ming Yang

Force-sensitive manipulation requires contact and load information that is not provided explicitly by visual observations. We present CW-WAM, a world action model that jointly predicts video, motion, and contact wrench. Inspired by hybrid motion-force control, it contains a unified interaction module that treats both motion and contact wrench as modeling targets and predicts time-aligned motion-wrench chunks as control references. A pair-causal attention mechanism structures the motion-wrench dependencies, while a long fixed-length wrench memory retains past interaction information. The predicted wrench chunk acts as a force reference for a 20-Hz outer-loop admittance controller within a slow-fast control system that maintains force feedback and smooth motion between model queries. CW-WAM improves contact prediction and increases the insertion success rate from 25% to 70% for square pegs with 1-mm clearance per side and from 40% to 95% for power adapters, compared with WAM without wrench input. It also improves lifting-height accuracy across different loads. Our framework with an admittance controller reduces force-reference RMSE from 4.8 to 2.7 N for peg insertion and from 2.0 to 1.3 N for wiping, respectively, with smaller force fluctuations during successful execution. Multimodal Embodied Agents: A Unified Capability-centric Perspective from Computer-Use to Robot-Use

Yanzhe Chen, Ziyi Yang, Jifeng Zhu, Qiming Huang, Ruihe An et al. (17 authors)

Multimodal agents (MMAs) sustain interaction through reasoning, memory, tools, and feedback, most visibly as computer-use agents, while robotic systems (RSs) couple sensing and actuation under physical dynamics. We define multimodal embodied agents (MMEAs) as goal-directed systems that couple multimodal task reasoning with physical action and revise decisions from the resulting feedback. This raises a central question: what changes when a multimodal agent moves from computer-use to robot-use? We introduce PAPAV, a unified capability-centric framework of five recurring functions: Perceive the current state, Anticipate action effects, Plan a feasible course, Act through an interface or body, and Verify the outcome. Defined by function rather than architecture, these capabilities let PAPAV identify what all three share and then compare MMEAs with MMAs and with RSs. Five physical constraints, from partial observability to unverifiable outcomes, leave fewer choices fixed in advance and less room to reverse errors. Across 64 benchmarks, explicit evaluation centers on Act; only 5 assess Anticipate and 3 assess Verify, leaving both largely hidden behind task success. These constraints also frame the open challenges we identify. PAPAV therefore provides a common basis for designing reliable physical agents and measuring progress across all five capabilities rather than task success alone.

Asymmetric On-Policy Distillation Learns In-Context Exploration

Sriyash Poddar, Yanda Bao, Jacob Krantz, Matthew Chang, Xavier Puig et al. (8 authors)

Sequential decision-making under partial observability is difficult because agents must explore to infer hidden, task-relevant information before acting effectively. Learning such information-gathering behaviors tabula rasa via online reinforcement learning is sample-inefficient, whereas offline imitation averages expert actions across hidden states and fails to learn exploration. In this work, we propose ASTEROID, a simple and effective student--teacher supervised learning framework that can learn test-time exploration. Although a privileged expert never needs to explore, we demonstrate that a history-conditioned student can learn to do so when trained with on-policy contexts: the student first collects on-policy context under partial observability, and the privileged expert provides action labels conditioned on that context. By distilling expert actions directly conditioned on the student's on-policy history, the student learns to systematically resolve partial observability, i.e., search while uncertain and exploit once the hidden state is revealed. This enables efficient test-time exploration (similar to Bayesian posterior sampling) without incurring the impractical training-data costs of reinforcement learning. Across a diverse range of eleven different environments, including discrete exploration benchmarks, robotic manipulation, navigation, and Procgen environments, ASTEROID achieves ~2x higher returns under fixed exploration budgets, uses ~100x fewer interactions than RL baselines, and outperforms frontier models at exploration. Finally, our method transfers policies trained in simulation to vision-denied real-world robot manipulation, exhibiting efficient exploration and increasing success by +40% over standard distillation. Overall, it provides a simple yet surprisingly effective framework to learn efficient test-time exploration.

Implementation of Dynamic Microsegmentation for a Multi-Robot Network

Vishwam Raval, Jaewon Kim, Ravi Pattabhi, Sandip Roy

This report describes a laboratory instantiation of a dynamic microsegmentation function for an Internet-of-Things network, specifically a multi-robot network, using a commercial software-defined microsegmentation tool. Fine-grained dynamic segmentation policies are defined, where segments are adapted based on asset functional and health tags. Automated implementation of the dynamic policies in software is also undertaken. The microsegmentation solution is shown to automatically isolate an indicated threat in real time, while maintaining unaffected operations. The demonstration indicates that microsegmentation can be implemented in IoT networks, in a way that allows for robust operation in the face of threats and disruptions.

Machine Learning-Enabled Wireless Power Transfer: A Task-Oriented Review of Structural Optimization, State and Parameter Estimation, and Adaptive Control

Denghao Luo, Yiheng Jiang, Jiaqi Zhao, Yuan Gao, Changsong Cai et al. (7 authors)

Wireless power transfer (WPT) systems must maintain efficient and safe energy delivery under variations in geometry, coupling, load, alignment, and operating conditions. Machine-learning methods are increasingly applied to accelerate structural design, estimate operating parameters and states, and support adaptive control and scheduling. This review develops a task-oriented framework organized around three interconnected domains: structural design and optimization, parameter and state estimation, and control and optimization. Supervised learning, reinforcement learning, and hybrid or physics-informed methods are compared in terms of data requirements, dependence on physical models, information sources, computational requirements, accuracy, online response, application scope, and optimization objectives. Supervised learning provides rapid surrogate modeling, regression, and classification when representative labeled data are available. Reinforcement learning addresses sequential control and scheduling but introduces challenges in reward design, safe exploration, and simulation-to-reality transfer. Hybrid methods integrate physical knowledge with data-driven models to improve data efficiency and physical consistency. The principal barriers to engineering deployment include limited and biased datasets, weak out-of-distribution generalization, insufficient physical consistency, unsafe online adaptation, embedded-resource constraints, and the absence of standardized benchmarks. Future research should prioritize uncertainty-aware and physics-informed learning, safe simulation-to-reality transfer, lightweight edge deployment, distributed coordination, reproducible evaluation, and digital-twin-assisted development.

AtomicRL: Atomic Reinforcement Learning for Long-Horizon Robotic Manipulation

Likui Zhang, Zhe Zhu, Zhihao Zhan, Yibo Bai, Hanlin Wang et al. (10 authors)

Vision-language-action (VLA) models have shown promise in robotic manipulation, but imitation learning (IL) alone often struggles to master complex long-horizon skills. Reinforcement learning (RL) offers an effective avenue for further improving VLA policies. However, existing VLA-RL methods typically suffer from delayed and biased credit assignment due to trajectory-level sparse rewards, while imbalanced subtask mastery creates bottlenecks that limit overall performance. To address these challenges, we propose AtomicRL, an RL post-training framework that leverages atomic task structures for reliable credit assignment and adaptive exploration. AtomicRL introduces Semantic-Aware Policy Optimization (SAPO), which uses semantic-aware gating to constrain TD residuals from failed atomic subtasks from propagating across task boundaries, mitigating credit contamination while preserving intra-subtask credit assignment and value bootstrapping. In addition, Bottleneck-Adaptive Target Exploration (BATE) dynamically coordinates task sampling and local exploration incentives based on the mastery of individual atomic subtasks, directing learning toward the remaining bottlenecks. Finally, we develop a Real2Sim2Real pipeline that bridges online RL in simulation with real-world deployment, facilitating practical transfer to physical robots. On LIBERO-LONG, AtomicRL achieves absolute performance gains of 3.6% and 4.0% over PPO across two VLA architectures. On CALVIN ABC-D, it improves full-task performance by up to 9.5% over PPO. Combined with the Real2Sim2Real pipeline, AtomicRL further improves real-world task performance by an average of 21.3%. These results demonstrate that AtomicRL effectively improves the learning efficiency and manipulation performance of VLA policies on long-horizon tasks.

Why the Swing Parameters Are Sloppy

M Parimi, Shashank Varma, Sunny Kumar, S R Wagh, Navdeep Singh

Evangelou, Stanković, Kevrekidis and Transtrum compared analytic and data-driven assessments of parameter identifiability for the infinite-bus synchronous generator. Fisher information with the manifold boundary approximation method (MBAM), and output diffusion maps with geometric harmonics, report the same six identifiable parameters on the late window $t \in [3, 5] s$, and both place the inertia $H$ and the damping $D$ among the unidentifiable ones. We explain this result from the geometry of the dynamics rather than from that of the parameter-to-data map. The rotor swing is a fast fibre that has collapsed onto a two-dimensional normally attracting invariant manifold (NAIM) of the transient fluxes before the window opens. The zeroth-order NAIM is exactly independent of $H$, $D$, $T_{d0}''$ and $T_{q0}''$, four of the five MBAM limits. We prove, under stated smoothness and rank conditions, that each of the four limits is a boundary of the model manifold at a distance equal, to first order in the parameter, to its current profiled speed. The same analysis predicts how identifiability changes with the experiment: neither a larger initial speed deviation nor a load change restores $H$ or $D$ in the late window, whereas the early window $[0, 2] s$ makes $H$ the best-determined parameter. Evaluated in a physical metric fixed by synchrophasor accuracy limits, the late window identifies five parameters rather than six and the early window nine. The spiked-covariance structure of the linearised trajectory map explains why the principal components of whitened, sampled trajectories reproduce the prior-scaled Fisher spectrum, and regression on those components attains the Fisher-predicted recovery error in 113 of 114 parameter--scenario pairs.

Do Earth-Trained Humanoid Controllers Walk on the Moon? Gravity, Terrain, and Soil Transfer

Guillem Casadesus Vila, Sri Aditya Deevi, Grace X. Gao

The Moon changes gravity, terrain, and soil at once. Learned locomotion has studied reduced gravity on rigid ground and granular terrain mostly at Earth gravity, but not humanoids under both, generalist-controller transfer, or the Earth cost of lunar adaptation. On a simulated Unitree G1, we train 12 policies with one recipe, varying gravity, terrain geometry, and ground contact (rigid or three-dimensional resistive force theory, 3D RFT), and including Earth-to-Moon fine-tuning and gravity randomization. We test them at both gravities on rigid ground and 3D RFT, and at their training gravity on three held-out soil models. We also test the frozen SONIC, a released generalist humanoid controller, at both gravities on rigid ground only. Gravity transfer is asymmetric. Earth-trained policies keep 0.64 to 0.75 survival at the Moon on flat rigid ground, Moon-trained and fine-tuned policies cannot stand at Earth, and SONIC survives at most 0.10 at the Moon. Rough training lifts combined-tile survival from 0.53 to 0.98 at Earth and 0.80 to 0.99 at the Moon. In 3D RFT, soil training helps on Earth sand, on tiles and for rough-trained policies also on flat sand. On the stiffer lunar soil it helps only flat-trained Earth policies at Earth and changes the survival of Moon-trained policies by at most a few hundredths. In the sampled rollouts of the held-out soil models, Moon-trained policies keep most of their rigid-ground progress on lunar regolith at the low G1 foot pressure, with per-seed progress ratios of 0.84 to 1.39 apart from one seed with a short rigid reference. A gravity-randomized policy trained on rough 3D RFT terrain has a seed-mean survival of at least 0.84 at Earth and 0.96 at the Moon on every key test cell up to 20 degrees. Lunar results are simulation predictions conditional on the contact model and soil stiffness.
