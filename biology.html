How many mutations does it take to evolve a new function?

Nida Farheen, Zachary Boroda, Ethan Levien, Jané Kondev

Biochemical and genetic networks in cells evolve to produce new functions. Evolution is guided by the genotype to phenotype map which describes how mutations at the molecular scale lead to changes in network function at the cellular scale. The number of genotypes is astronomically large which makes these maps very high dimensional. In this context, the simple question: How many mutations does it take to evolve a new function?, is usually hard to answer. Here we consider this question in the context of a simple Boolean-network model of a genotype-phenotype map. The nodes and edges of the Boolean network represent the genotype while the Boolean function the network computes is the phenotype. The network evolves by rewiring of its edges, while keeping the number of nodes fixed. We compute the number of mutations needed to achieve a new Boolean function as a function of the number of nodes in the network. We show that the number of mutations scales with the number of nodes of the network as a power-law with an exponent determined by local geometrical features of the genotype-phenotype map. Our theoretical study emphasizes the role of the genotype-phenotype map in determining the time to evolve a new function.

Advancing Clinical Genomic Analysis with a Skill-Centred Agent

Qiaoyu Zheng, Weike Zhao, Yanjie Fan, Ye Cao, Mengqi Jiang et al. (13 authors)

Clinical genomic analysis requires experts to integrate evolving guidelines, databases, and computational evidence. As sequencing has become increasingly accessible, this complex and labor-intensive process has become a major bottleneck to the timely use of genomic information in clinical decision-making. To fill this gap, we developed ClinGeX, a large-language-model agent that composes 105 curated executable skills through task-specific retrieval and dependency-aware orchestration. On 223 temporally held-out benchmark cases, ClinGeX achieved 71.7\% macro-averaged task-level concordance with expert reference classifications, compared with 45.7\% for the strongest comparator. In a three-condition, counterbalanced human-AI collaboration study, 45 clinical genomic professionals assessed 135 clinically derived cases under routine (no AI assistance), DeepSeek-assisted and ClinGeX-assisted conditions. ClinGeX assistance increased raw case-level concordance across criterion-level variant analysis, genomic interpretation in rare disease and molecular oncology decision-making (51.1\%, 57.8\%, 64.4\% versus 15.6\%, 31.1\%, 20.0\% in comparator conditions) and reduced reported interpretation time in all scenarios. During an exploratory one-month real-world non-interventional assessment, 11 experienced professionals submitted feedback on 96 self-selected analysis episodes and reported frequent conclusion adoption and time savings. These findings support executable domain skills as a promising approach to augment specialist genomic interpretation.

A Low-Rank Space Captures Linear Alignment Between LLM and Human Neural Activity During Naturalistic Language Listening

Zach Paris, Emily Finn

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used as computational models of human language processing, yet it remains unclear which components of their representations drive alignment with neural activity. Here, we use ridge reduced-rank regression to identify the brain-predictive subspace of LLM representations: the representational directions that support linear prediction of neural responses. Across three naturalistic language-listening datasets spanning intracranial electrophysiology and fMRI, and six model conditions spanning autoregressive, bidirectional masked, and randomly initialized language models, we find that full linear encoding performance can be recovered using only 1-3% of the original embedding dimensionality. These low-dimensional subspaces generalize across held-out participants and, to a lesser extent, across datasets, suggesting that neural predictivity is concentrated in a compact and partially shared representational space. We next compare these brain-predictive directions with the principal axes of variation in the embeddings. Principal component analysis requires substantially more dimensions to recover neural prediction, demonstrating that the directions most useful for predicting neural activity are distinct from those capturing the most overall variance within LLM representations themselves. Finally, characterizing the learned subspaces allows us to surface interpretable features that are overrepresented in neural activity above and beyond the LLM representations. Together, these results show that LLM-brain alignment is supported by a small, structured subset of model representations and provides a framework for better understanding exactly why these representations align. Code can be found at: https://github.com/thefinnlab/low-rank-alignment

Latent Prediction Yields Cross-Subject Alignable Representations in Sensory-Evoked fMRI

Serwan Jassim, Victoria Bosch, Daniel Anthes, Elia Bruni, Tim Kietzmann

Decoding sensory-evoked functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) data is a central analytical tool in cognitive neuroscience. Here we ask whether self-supervised learning (SSL), which has been scarcely explored in this domain, can yield representations that support strong downstream performance. To do so, we adapt a joint embedding predictive architecture (JEPA) to train participant-specific encoders on brain data alone. The encoders never see the stimulus images or any representation derived from them. Applied to the Natural Scenes Dataset (NSD), we evaluate our learned brain embeddings on the downstream tasks of brain captioning and image reconstruction, as well as cross-participant generalization. Our self-supervised approach achieves competitive performance with parameter-rich supervised pipelines that are trained end-to-end with access to image and language data. We further find that encoders trained independently for different participants can be aligned fully unsupervised, using neither images nor stimuli shared between them. After alignment, a neural decoding head trained on one participant can be applied to another without fine-tuning and retains most of its within-subject performance on image reconstruction and brain captioning. By removing the need for images and shared stimuli, our approach is a step towards pooling fMRI data across participants and, eventually, across datasets.

Counting approximate breakpoint medians of random genomes

Poly H. da Silva, Arash Jamshidpey, David Sankoff

Unsigned linear unichromosomal genomes with genes labeled 1, . . . , n are represented by permutations of these labels, whose adjacencies are the unordered pairs of genes occurring consecutively. The breakpoint distance counts the adjacencies of one genome that are absent from the other, and a breakpoint median minimizes the total breakpoint distance to a given set of genomes. Deciding whether a permutation is a median often requires a minimization over the whole symmetric group, which makes the median set resistant to exact counting. We replace this global condition by a local one: a c-approximate median is a genome having at most c adjacencies absent from all the input genomes. For a fixed genome we introduce its approximate median inverse, the set of ordered k-tuples of genomes ad- mitting it as a c-approximate median, and we construct and count this set explicitly. For k genomes chosen uniformly and independently at random, this gives the probability that a given genome is a c-approximate median and the expected number of such approximate medians. The relaxation is not only technical: approximate medians and near-optimal genomes co- incide up to a shift of the tolerance that remains bounded in probability as the number of genes grows. Finally, we derive an explicit upper bound on the probability that a given genome is an exact median, and on the expected number of exact breakpoint medians.

AI-driven structural informatics reveals diverse glycosylation machinery across double-stranded DNA viruses

Lan Na

Glycosylation is widespread in biology and shapes molecular recognition, yet the diversity and biosynthetic organization of viral glycosyltransferases (GTs) remain poorly understood. Here we integrate structural-alphabet-guided searches and structure similarity networks with genomic-neighbourhood and biosynthetic gene cluster embedding analyses to examine viral GT-A proteins. This approach reveals extensive structural diversity overlooked by sequence-based annotation, including previously uncharacterized viral GT lineages. Most strikingly, we identified a jumbo-phage locus encoding five tandem glycosyltransferases alongside hydroxymethyl-pyrimidine biosynthetic and sulfation-associated enzymes, suggesting a previously unrecognized form of viral DNA modification involving a sulfated pentasaccharide. Beyond these examples, predicted saccharide-associated loci span divergent double-stranded DNA viruses, from giant viruses to tailed phages, and occupy multiple regions of the reference biosynthetic landscape. Together, our findings uncover an underappreciated repertoire of virus-encoded glycosylation machinery and suggest that viral glycan biosynthesis encompasses diverse molecular contexts and potential biological functions.

Frequency-induced Tipping: Resonance-Driven Tipping in a Threshold Predator-Prey System

Shan Gao, Silas Poloni, Hao Wang, Mark Lewis

Tipping points refer to abrupt, substantial, and often irreversible transitions in dynamic systems that can be triggered by minor perturbations. Existing tipping mechanisms are usually classified by the nature of the trigger: bifurcation-induced tipping occurs when a parameter crosses a critical threshold, noise-induced tipping is caused by random perturbations, rate-induced tipping occurs when a system fails to track a moving attractor, and phase-induced tipping is triggered by timed perturbations relative to an oscillatory state. In this paper, we identify a new mechanism for tipping: frequency-induced tipping, in which periodic forcing drives a transition through resonant amplification. This occurs by destabilization of an equilibrium via subthreshold perturbations of the appropriate frequency. Filippov analysis of a piecewise-smooth model identifies admissible crossings that lead either to predator loss or to extinction of predator and prey, and numerical simulations show that tipping persists after smoothing of the thresholds. These results extend the current tipping framework to include critical transitions under recurrent environmental forcing.

What to randomize matters: a controlled study of volume, axis and representation in low-data molecular generation

Neerav Kaushal, Alisa Teige, Ajay MNV Penmatsa

Chemical language models can struggle to generate new molecules when trained on small datasets, where a model may reproduce training examples rather than learn a useful generative distribution. Randomized SMILES augmentation varies atom traversal, whereas Sequential Attachment-based Fragment Embedding (SAFE) also allows the order of fragment blocks to vary. How these distinct sources of representational variation affect low-data molecular generation has not been clearly studied. Here, using 1,000 seed molecules to train an autoregressive sequence model, we compare canonical SAFE with randomized SAFE at increasing corpus sizes, isolate atom-order and fragment-order axes of SAFE randomization, and compare fully randomized SAFE with randomized SMILES at molecule-by-molecule matched training corpora. Three findings emerge. First, randomization of SAFE alone raises the genuine yield of novel molecules more than 450-fold, turning a model that merely reproduces its training set into a practical molecular generator. Second, the two axes of randomization act jointly rather than additively: atom order alone reaches a tenth, and fragment order alone a seventh, of the genuine yield obtained when both vary, which is four times their combined contribution. Third, at matched corpus size and compute, randomized SAFE delivers 2.8 times the genuine yield of randomized SMILES. Welch tests with Games-Howell post-hoc comparisons resolve every difference on the primary outcomes. In the low-data regime, thus, what one randomizes rather than only how much determines whether augmentation produces new molecules or merely new spellings of the training set.

Spontaneous Spirals in Spatial Systems

Wim Hordijk

Several chemical and biological systems produce spontaneous spirals in their dynamical behavior, such as the Belousov-Zhabotinsky reaction in chemistry and the slime mold Dictyostelium in biology. In a previous study, such spiral wave formation was accurately replicated with a computational spatial system known as the Ideal Storage Cellular Automaton Model (ISCAM). Here, a more detailed investigation is presented of the ability of this model to generate spontaneous spirals depending on its parameter values. A dynamical systems analysis is performed, and bifurcation diagrams, phase space diagrams, and snapshots of the model's spatial dynamics are presented for a range of parameter values. This analysis reveals that spiral waves can only be sustained in the system when the parameter values of the model fall well within the regime of a so-called Neimark-Sacker bifurcation.

Recognition of auditory objects from continuous and ultra-sparse spike streams in neuromorphic devices

Leonardo E. S. Tavares, Kevin D. Sargent, Jon T. Sakata, Dezhe Z. Jin

Energy consumption is a major concern for modern artificial intelligence (AI) applications. The brain is thought to achieve energy-efficient computation through sparse coding schemes. By emulating the spiking dynamics of the brain, neuromorphic chips hold the potential to realize energy-efficient AI. In this work, we use birdsong to demonstrate that auditory object recognition can be achieved using ultra-sparse coding. Feature-detecting neurons tuned to short temporal features ($\sim$37 ms), sampled from exemplar syllables, transform continuous auditory signals into streams of ultra-sparse spikes that mark the onsets and offsets of features within the signal. Spike patterns elicited by the exemplars are stored as templates. During recognition, template matching is performed using axonal delays between feature-detecting and template-detecting neurons. Recognition is further enhanced by an additional group of specialized binary neurons trained to discriminate between syllable categories with subtle differences. When tested on Bengalese finch songs, our approach achieved error rates below 5% using 10 to 40 exemplars per syllable type. We then implemented this recognition algorithm using spiking neural networks on Intel's neuromorphic chip, Loihi, and found that response latencies remain constant (approximately 17 ms per syllable), regardless of the number of templates used. Our results demonstrate the potential of neuromorphic chips for low-latency, low-energy recognition of complex sounds, including speech.
