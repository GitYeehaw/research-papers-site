Learning Time-Varying Connectomes for Stable Behavior Across Development

Christof Fehrman, Jacob J. Morra, Eva A. Naumann

Neural circuits must maintain functionality to drive effective behavior while their size, topology, geometry, and intrinsic dynamics change across development. To model developing visuomotor circuits and behavior, we investigated whether a single compact generative encoding could produce functional recurrent controllers across such changes. Building on the HyperNEAT algorithm, we represented this program as a compositional pattern-producing network that received developmental time as an input to generate age-specific connectivity. The developmental substrate prescribed neuronal birth, position, sensory organization, and intrinsic time constants. We tested this approach using two geometrically distinct developmental substrates and two visuomotor tasks: optomotor responses and phototaxis. Controllers optimized at only the initial or mature substrate generally failed when evaluated at other ages. In contrast, controllers optimized across multiple developmental snapshots maintained substantially higher and more consistent performance across trained and unseen ages. Optimization at only three developmental snapshots was often sufficient to produce effective behavior across the tested developmental interval. The resulting connectomes changed substantially with age and did not converge on a single trajectory of sparsity or connection-weight statistics. Controllers also differed in whether they relied on neurons born later in development. These results demonstrate that a shared spatiotemporal time-varying connectivity program can produce recurrent neural networks that preserve behavior across substantial developmental changes. Together, these results provide a framework for studying how functional stability can emerge without requiring structural or dynamical stability.

Cell-type-specific modularity in E/I networks shapes dynamical regimes and computational capabilities

Emmanouil Giannakakis, Victor Buendía, Oleg Vinogradov, Anna Levina

Structural modularity is a ubiquitous feature of brain networks that shapes neural dynamics and information processing. Yet excitatory and inhibitory populations can differ in their modular organization, and it remains unclear how this asymmetry affects network dynamics and computation. Here, using dynamical mean-field theory and simulations of networks performing complex temporal tasks, we show that independently tuning excitatory and inhibitory modularity controls the dynamical regimes of balanced E/I networks. In particular, such tuning can generate a dominant spectral outlier despite overall E/I balance, producing a topology-controlled transition between bulk- and low-rank-dominated regimes, with different dynamical bifurcations leading to chaos and bistability, respectively. In chaotic time-series prediction tasks, computational performance is enhanced near these dynamical boundaries, including both the onset of chaos and the transition to bistability. Favorable configurations exhibit strongly modular excitation and comparatively homogeneous inhibition, similar to what is observed in cortical connectivity.

PINR-ESI: Physics-Informed Self-Supervised Implicit Neural Representation Framework for EEG Extended-Source Imaging

Zhiyong Zhou, Sheng Liao, Youshen Xiao, Xiaolin Li, Yakang Dai

EEG extended-source imaging aims to localize active cortical regions and characterize their spatial extent, yet supervised inverse networks typically depend on synthetic EEG-source pairs and may be sensitive to synthetic-to-real mismatch. We propose a physics-informed self-supervised implicit neural representation (INR) framework for spatial source reconstruction without paired source labels or simulation-based supervised pretraining. The lead field matrix encodes the source-to-sensor volume-conduction model and constrains the INR through agreement between predicted and measured EEG. Cortical current density is modeled as a continuous coordinate-conditioned field. A classical inverse estimate provides warm initialization and spatial constraints on source activity, while patch-integrated projection maps the field to discrete source coefficients for forward modeling. Simulations with varying noise levels, source counts, and source extents showed the clearest advantage in identifying active cortical regions, particularly for multiple and larger sources. Quantitative and qualitative evaluations using real EEG recordings from epilepsy patients and intracranial stimulation experiments further supported the feasibility of the framework for source imaging. These findings suggest the potential of physics-informed self-supervised INR for extended-source imaging.

Synchronized Hearts, Desynchronized Brains: A Nonlinear Dynamics Perspective for Function and Failure

Irene Pellini, Simon Bauer, Seba Contreras, Ruben Fernández Busnadiego, Anushka Deb et al. (19 authors)

Both the heart and the brain are built from electrically excitable cells, yet the emergent dynamics of these organs could not differ more. In the myocardium, a coherent traveling wave of action potentials ensures coordinated contraction; in the cerebral cortex, distributed, irregular spiking underlies efficient information processing and the generation of behavior. Here, we illuminate the principles that underlie the emergence of such distinct nonlinear dynamics using concepts from complex systems. We trace these principles across scales from ion channels and cellular action potential generation, through cellular coupling mechanisms and network architecture, to organ level dynamics and clinical phenotypes. Designed to support either synchronized waves or desynchronized information processing, the heart and the brain can thus be understood as operating on opposite sides of a synchronization phase transition. Crossing this transition implies pathological dynamics in both organs: Loss of synchrony in the heart produces arrhythmia; excess synchrony in the brain leads to epileptic seizures. We discuss how adaptation mechanisms, including plasticity, modulation, remodeling, and learning, maintain the appropriate level of (de)synchronization. Overall, we provide a unified excitable-system perspective, together with an educational notebook, to foster cross-disciplinary insights into cardiac and neural function and pathology.

Time-Division Multiplexing: The Operational Rhythm and Temporal Organization Layer of Digital Life

Haibin Tong, Luming Guo, Nana Cui, Jiang Yu

Continual agents must handle urgent interaction while retaining alternatives, replaying errors, and committing safe long-term changes. A previous digital-life framework distinguishes online (busy) and offline (idle) channels, but leaves open whether these are separate programs or phases of a common process and how their operations should be ordered. Motivated by recent observations of rapidly alternating perceptual representations in primate cortex, we develop a temporal organization layer with phase-tagged micro-epochs, an observable duty cycle, independent content and resource controls, and three nested clocks separating candidate exploration from durable commitment. We give a projected duty-cycle update, state its deterministic descent guarantee and the additional noise term required for a stochastic guarantee, and specify coupled busy/idle execution contracts. Five synthetic experiments (64 seeds each) examine descent and noise, objective misalignment, gain asymmetry, resource coupling, and dropped replay logs. In the gain/noise model the best tested gain ratio is 1.55; decoupled scheduling yields 26.6% versus 11.1% success on a constructed urgent/high-load subset. A frozen-backbone Qwen3-8B prototype further checks orchestration, but its proxy outcomes are not task-benchmark evidence. The results establish a testable scheduling design and identify its conditions of validity, not machine consciousness or general-purpose agent superiority.

Modeling Gene Expression Noise: From Transcriptional Dynamics to Cell Growth and Division

Augustinas Sukys, Lucy Ham, Kaan Öcal, James Holehouse, Ramon Grima

Gene expression is inherently noisy, leading to substantial cell-to-cell variation in mRNA and protein levels. The telegraph model and the simpler bursty model provide good fits to single-cell count distributions and have been used extensively to understand transcriptional kinetics and estimate the kinetic parameters. However, these models all describe a static cell. Here, we review models of stochastic gene expression in growing and dividing cells which aim to understand how a cell's own life history, from its birth, through copying its genome, to its division, leaves a distinct fluctuation signature on molecule numbers. We discuss the biochemical and biophysical processes captured by these models, their analytical tractability, the concept of cyclo-stationary behavior, when this is well approximated by the steady-state of static models and how the distributions of molecular numbers measured under these conditions sensitively depend on the experimental setup. This is followed by a discussion of how these models are fitted to single-cell measurements, how biological variability can be separated from measurement noise, and which computational advances make this feasible for thousands of genes. We finish by highlighting the open problems in the field.

Mind Control of LLMs via Closed-Loop Stimulation: Out-of-Distribution Robustness

Kasra Fallah, Rudramani Singha, Hannah Ghanei, Paul Sajda, James Anderson et al. (6 authors)

Brains and large language models need not be considered equivalent systems to reveal an interpretable commonality: both exhibit high-dimensional, nonlinear dynamics whose behavior can only be modeled imperfectly. This creates a shared challenge whenever we seek to intervene in their dynamics. A controller may either rely on a model identified around a particular operating regime and risk failure as the system moves away from that regime, or explicitly account for uncertainty and a changing internal state. Neurostimulation has confronted an analogous problem for decades. The limitations of fixed, open-loop stimulation have motivated closed-loop and state-dependent approaches that condition intervention on the ongoing state of the neural system. Inspired by this principle, we formulate LLM steering as a feedback-control problem in which the network's internal state, rather than a fixed intervention alone, determines how the system is perturbed. We further account for the mismatch between tractable dynamical models and the nonlinear transformer they approximate through robust control. As one principled realization of this framework, we develop an $H_\infty$-based steering controller that limits sensitivity to disturbances and linearization mismatches. Across multiple steering benchmarks, the controller improves performance over existing baselines, with particularly pronounced gains under out-of-distribution conditions and varying length complexities. Beyond controller performance, we introduce robust steerability, $S_{rob}$, a pre-deployment measure of tolerance to dynamics mismatch; across model-task pairs, it exhibits task-specific structure and varies systematically with model scale. Beyond robust LLM steering, our measure asks not merely whether control is possible, but how much uncertainty it can survive; a principle that may span artificial and biological neural systems.

An Exact Poisson Mixture Representation of Siler Remaining Life Expectancy

Silvio C. Patricio

The Siler model describes mortality from birth to old age as the sum of a declining immaturity hazard, a constant background hazard, and an increasing senescence hazard. No standard closed form is available for its remaining life expectancy, which must therefore be evaluated by numerical integration. We show that Siler remaining life expectancy at age $x$ is a Poisson mixture of Gompertz-Makeham remaining life expectancies, that is, a Poisson-weighted average of life expectancies in models with only a constant background hazard and an exponentially increasing senescence hazard. All components share the senescence hazard. Their background hazards increase from one component to the next by the rate at which immaturity mortality declines. The Poisson mean is the immaturity hazard that remains to be accumulated after age $x$. This representation yields an exact series of generalized exponential integrals with a closed-form bound on the truncation error. Replacing the background hazard by its discounted counterpart also gives the Laplace transform of the remaining lifetime. The mixture bounds the life expectancy lost to immaturity mortality: up to a term below the reciprocal of $a_2+b_1$, this loss equals the probability of an immaturity death times the life expectancy with the immaturity hazard removed. In annual Siler fits to Swedish female mortality, we estimate that the loss at birth fell from 4.3 years in 1900 to less than 0.01 years in 2020, which accounts for 15% of the gain in life expectancy at birth over the period.

Discrete Backbone Elasticity and Curvature-Dependent Torsion Statistics in Proteins

Yiquan Wang, Mingyu Bian, Jiayuan Ma

Coarse-grained models of protein conformational dynamics use $C_α$ geometry to describe the probabilities of regular helices, ordered turns and disordered conformations. To test how much of this order arises from local chain elasticity, we connect discrete Hasimoto frame transport to neighboring bending mismatch, deriving a curvature-product torsion interaction and a six-parameter generalized von Mises density. Fitted on 194 chains from the ATLAS molecular dynamics database and tested on 1744 sequence-nonredundant proteins, this baseline reduces negative log-density loss from 1.490 to 1.160 nats, torsion-sign error from 12.26 to 2.15 percentage points, and helix-fraction error from 21.84 to 10.41 points. Ablations and circular-moment projections against reference generative models motivate extensions to anisotropic stiffness and two partition-function-weighted elastic states, further lowering test loss to 0.790 nats and helix error to 1.65 points. The fixed hierarchy also improves predictions across 72 trajectories of peptides $Fs$ and $(AAQAA)_3$ and miniprotein $HP35$ spanning varied starting conformations and force fields. Remaining angular errors in these trajectories are associated with atomic context at shared bends, as bend-matched peptide frames adopt opposite torsion signs with distinct hydrogen-bond patterns and a proline-containing $HP35$ turn remains at negative torsion. Introducing a bilinearly coupled $C_α$--$N$ orientation angle largely reparameterizes the existing bend-dependent harmonics, with an empirical $L^2$ Gram projection placing 96.7\% to 99.6\% of its added coefficient-function squared norm within the original six-function span. These results link discrete Hasimoto elasticity to statistical backbone potentials and clarify the scope and limits of local $C_α$ geometry.

A minimal E/I circuit model explains differential recovery from amblyopia under TTX and reverse occlusion

Lawrence Udeigwe

Recovery from amblyopia beyond the critical period remains a major challenge in visual neuroscience. Experimental studies show that transient inactivation of the fellow eye with tetrodotoxin (TTX) produces stronger and more persistent recovery than reverse occlusion (RO), despite both interventions reducing competition from the fellow eye. The circuit mechanisms underlying this difference remain unclear. We develop a minimal cortical circuit model combining excitatory-inhibitory population dynamics, BCM-like metaplasticity, homeostatic regulation, and eye-specific synaptic plasticity. Monocular deprivation drives the network into an amblyopic operating regime with reduced excitatory activity and fellow-eye dominance. Phase-plane and operating-regime analyses show that TTX and RO subsequently place the circuit in distinct dynamical states. TTX lowers inhibitory activity while maintaining excitatory activity above the BCM modification threshold, creating a larger plasticity-permissive regime than RO. These differences produce stronger recovery of amblyopic-eye responsiveness and greater correction of ocular dominance under TTX. A local robustness analysis across 300 perturbed parameter sets shows that the TTX advantage is not dependent on a narrowly tuned parameter choice. These results provide a mechanistic account of the differential efficacy of fellow-eye inactivation and suggest that recovery from sensory deprivation depends not only on changes in sensory input, but also on the dynamical operating regime of the cortical circuit.
