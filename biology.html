When Does the Real Wiring Win? Testing connectome structure-function claims against unmeasured physiology

Yeyuan Liu

Connectome-constrained models are often used to argue that a measured wiring diagram outperforms shuffled or rewired wiring, and hence that structure shapes function. Such comparisons can confound wiring with how the models are tuned: a connectome fixes who connects to whom, not the excitability, gains or plasticity rates the model also needs, and modellers often tune these until the real-wiring model works. We separate three estimands: physiology tuned on the real wiring and transferred to nulls (P1), each wiring tuned on its own (P2), and P2 restricted to physiology consistent with independent data (P3). The biases involved are known in machine learning (under-tuned baselines, inherited weights, winner's curse); we measure what they do to biological conclusions, using synthetic ground truth, placebo nulls and three Drosophila circuit models. Some claims survive every protocol we test: segregated reward and punishment dopamine improves learning, and head-direction topography is required for a persistent bump. Others do not. In the mushroom body, the real Kenyon-cell output wiring beats a cross-subtype identity shuffle by 0.09 under P1 and P2 but loses to it by 0.09 under P3 (95% CIs exclude zero; held-out runs confirm). The verdict is set by the share of alpha'beta' cells among active Kenyon cells, which P1 selects low; at the share implied by imaging (about 44%) the real-wiring model fails to learn, exposing a model inadequacy rather than a deficit of the fly's wiring. In flyvis, P1 penalises a mirrored connectome, which has a constructed solution within 0.07 (median) of the real error, more than a spatially shuffled one in 50 of 50 members; the ranking survives ten-fold longer retraining but depends on the checkpoint at fifty-fold. A credible structural claim needs matched optimisation, data-constrained physiology and a model that still works there.

Emergent Biological Capabilities in a Foundation Model for Molecular Interactions

Adi Mashiach

Foundation models trained on large unlabeled corpora develop emergent capabilities, tasks that go beyond the training objective. We extend this paradigm to multimodal biology with Bonbon, a foundation model for molecular interactions trained on approximately 3.2 trillion tokens of paired protein-ligand sequences. From a single representation space, Bonbon exhibits three zero-shot emergent capabilities at hierarchical levels of resolution: functional (mechanism of action), residue (binding site localization, including orthosteric/allosteric discrimination), and bond (covalent versus non-covalent engagement). All three capabilities generalize to out-of-distribution complexes. Bonbon was trained on protein-ligand sequences alone, without any task-specific supervision and without structural coordinates. We conducted controlled scaling experiments across six model scales from 42M to 1.6B parameters; every capability evaluated shows a sharp jump at the largest scale on continuous metrics, even as pretraining loss decreases smoothly. This demonstrates that loss-based scaling laws alone are insufficient to predict when zero-shot emergent capabilities become available. The capabilities reported here emerge after training at a 1:2,000 parameter-to-token ratio to accommodate the sparsity of paired biological interaction data. The same frozen representations achieve state-of-the-art binding and affinity prediction at five orders of magnitude greater speed than co-folding methods. In prospective wet-lab validation across nine disease targets spanning seven protein families, Bonbon achieves a 61.5% dose-response hit rate on de novo compounds, yielding structurally novel molecules not previously reported to inhibit these proteins, including for out-of-distribution targets.

Mutual Linearity in Nonlinear Population Dynamics

Shiling Liang

Mutual Linearity in Nonlinear Population DynamicsMutual linearity, the persistence of affine relations among stationary observables under arbitrarily strong changes of one transition rate, is known for Markov jump processes and chemical reaction networks. We show that generalized Lotka--Volterra dynamics form a new class of mutually linear systems, even though species can go extinct and attractors can be chaotic. Any change to one species' equation acts on the others as one effective press and keeps all abundances affinely related: piecewise across extinctions, and as time averages on chaotic attractors.

Measuring Robustness and Efficiency in a Connectome-Constrained Fly Visual System Model: Linking an Adversarial and a Perceptual Illusion

Alexandre Garcia-Duran, Alejandro Rodriguez-Garcia, Manuel Molano-Mazón, Alexandre Hyafil, Srikanth Ramaswamy et al. (6 authors)

Biological visual systems compute reliably under tight metabolic and wiring constraints, whereas artificial vision systems consume far more energy and can be sensitive to adversarial perturbations. Whether networks with brain-like wiring are more robust at lower computational cost is unclear. We address this by benchmarking a task-optimized, continuous-time recurrent neural network of the fly visual system constrained by its synaptic connectome (FlyNet) against artificial neural networks. We compare it against CNNs, RAFT, and continuous-time recurrent neural networks without detailed connectome constraints (sCTRNN, CTRNN) on a collision-detection task within a differentiable 3D rendering pipeline. FlyNet is adversarially vulnerable but, relative to its compute, more robust to common video corruptions than RAFT, while using up to five orders of magnitude fewer FLOPs. The CTRNNs remain more robust than FlyNet across almost all tested perturbations, suggesting that connectome constraints benefit efficiency, through their low edge count, over robustness. Recurrence improves robustness to white-box attacks in RAFT and temporal context improves robustness to common corruptions. FlyNet's position and velocity representations degrade under adversarial textures but recover under counterfactual textures that restore correct classification while staying equally far from training, ruling out unfamiliar textures as the cause of failure. The reverse-phi illusion similarly disrupts these representations and inverts the same low-level motion statistic as the adversarial textures. Optimizing this input statistic alone, without access to the network, suffices to induce classification failure, attributing both perturbations to the same circuit vulnerability. Connectome-constrained models can thus surface adversarial failure mechanisms as testable hypotheses about biological perceptual vulnerabilities.

Bayesian inverse problems and epidemic-threshold uncertainty for a heterogeneous SIS reaction-diffusion model

Duc-Lam Duong

We study Bayesian inference of persistence from early observations of a heterogeneous susceptible--infected--susceptible (SIS) reaction--diffusion model with standard incidence. We show that the leading prevalence dynamics depend on the unknown coefficients only through the difference between the transmission and recovery rates and the infected diffusivity. The same pair determines the principal eigenvalue whose sign governs persistence, so early prevalence can inform the threshold even when the individual rates remain uncertain. We prove global weak well-posedness and quantitative parameter stability for the SIS system and use the resulting observation-map estimates to establish Bayesian well-posedness. For an initial infection of size $\varepsilon$, we derive a uniform second-order expansion that describes how the separate rates enter prevalence. Incidence depends explicitly on transmission at leading order. When the noise standard deviation is proportional to $\varepsilon$, we prove $O(\varepsilon)$ convergence in Hellinger distance of the nonlinear posteriors to their low-prevalence limits for both prevalence alone and joint prevalence and incidence data. We bound the principal eigenvalue using the growth rate of the infected $L^2$ norm between two times. This rate gives a lower bound for the full nonlinear system, and the upper bound separates spatial transients from nonlinear saturation. Uniform spectral estimates and bounds on infection size and measurement error give sufficient conditions for persistence and extinction. We also bound the error in posterior persistence probabilities under forward and eigenvalue approximation, accounting for posterior mass near the threshold. Numerical examples illustrate threshold inference and the predicted \(O(\varepsilon)\) convergence of the nonlinear posteriors to their low-prevalence limits in Hellinger distance.

Cotangent-lift inference of stochastic microbial dynamics

José A. Capitán, Alberto Megías, Cristina Sardón

We study the parameter estimation for the stochastic logistic and Lotka--Volterra diffusions from discrete, irregularly spaced observations corrupted by measurement noise, a setting typical of microbiome time series. For the logistic equation, the growth rate acts as a time change and does not enter the stationary law, so it can be identified only from the temporal structure of the data. We prove that the maximum-likelihood estimator of the Euler scheme, with the observations taken as the states, coincides with the regression of log-increments on abundances used in the literature, and we characterise what this estimator cannot see under measurement noise: for the linearised dynamics, the law of consecutive pairs of observations is invariant along an explicit one-parameter family of relaxation rates and noise levels, and the regression converges to a limit set by the noise. Treating the true abundances as latent, we compute the likelihood with a Gaussian moment filter and show that measurement noise is precisely what couples the filter states across samples. The exact gradient is then the backward recursion of the discrete cotangent lift, whose cost, two to three likelihood evaluations, does not depend on the number of parameters, and we give the covector map of each filter step in closed form. In simulations, the latent estimator recovers the relaxation times, noise levels and interaction strengths that the regression biases, while larger communities require prior information on the interactions. In gut microbiome time series it attributes most of the variability of a dominant genus to measurement noise and estimates its relaxation time at about $64$ hours, with a large standard error.

Optimization principles, revisited

William Bialek

The brain solves a wide range of problems, and some of these solutions are very good indeed. It is an old idea that, in some cases, these solutions may be as good as possible given the relevant constraints. If this is true we can promote this notion of optimization to a principle from which aspects of function and mechanism can be derived, ideally with no free parameters. Optimization principles are powerful and have had many successes, but they remain controversial. We start with a reminder of a classic example---diffraction limited optics in insect eyes---and then a summary of why you should worry about optimization as a more general principle. Next we turn to recent progress on the theory of optimal motion estimation in fly vision, and finally summarize recent results on the landscape for optimization, arguing that (contrary to widely held intuitions) optimal performance need not require the underlying mechanisms to have finely tuned parameters. I hope to convince you that this is a moment to take optimization seriously as the basis for theories of neural coding and computation. This is based on a rather informal lecture at the summer school LASCON X in Sao Paulo, and I have tried to preserve that tone here; pointers are given to more detailed and technical discussions.

ANTIOCH-VS: From Sequence to Hits with Modular, Open-Source Ensemble Virtual Screening

Hale S. Hasdemir, Venkata Sai Sreyas Adury, Pratyush Tiwary

Structure-based virtual screening needs a receptor conformation that fits the ligand class, but for most targets no ligand-bound crystal is available -- and a single predicted model or an apo structure often misses the binding-competent state. We present ANTIOCH-VS, an open-source framework that runs an entire in silico screening campaign, from a protein sequence to a ranked shortlist of candidate binders, as one plug-and-play workflow. It first builds a conformational ensemble by reduced-MSA subsampling with ColabFold, then uses AlphaFold2-RAVE (AF2RAVE) -- short molecular-dynamics simulations with State Predictive Information Bottleneck analysis -- to pick out docking-ready, Boltzmann-weighted metastable states. It then detects the binding pocket, prepares receptors and ligands, docks the library across the ensemble with DOCK6 or AutoDock Vina, builds a consensus ranking, and triages the top hits by physics-based metadynamics (CTMD). We benchmark it on two very different targets, JAK2 kinase and ADRB2 GPCR, using their DUD-E benchmark sets of known binders and property-matched decoys. Starting from sequence alone -- no ligand-bound crystal, no manual intervention -- the AF2RAVE ensembles gave top-20 shortlists strongly enriched for true binders: a 20% hit rate for JAK2 and 60% for ADRB2 (random ~1-2%), on par with ligand-bound crystals and, for the kinase, above single AF2 models and an apo crystal. The full pipeline runs end to end on a workstation or HPC from a single command, or each module can be run on its own with user-supplied inputs. ANTIOCH-VS is freely available at https://github.com/tiwarylab/antioch-vs

Mesoscopic derivation of predator-prey dynamics via diffusive fast-reaction asymptotics: cross-diffusion and fear effects

Marzia Bisi, Andrea Bondesan, Maria Groppi, Cinzia Soresina

We propose a kinetic derivation of a spatial predator-prey model with cross-diffusion interactions and a Beddington-DeAngelis functional response. At the mesoscopic level, we divide the two populations into distinct behavioral states: prey can be active or hidden in a refuge, while predators can be either searching or handling. The microscopic state of each subpopulation is described in terms of group size and a continuous directional velocity variable, with state transitions governed by suitable Boltzmann-type interaction operators. By combining a diffusive scaling with a slow-fast formulation of the kinetic reaction operators, we formally derive a macroscopic reaction-cross-diffusion system for the total population densities of prey and predators. The limiting model consistently recovers the Beddington-DeAngelis functional response along with its associated nonlinear diffusion terms. Crucially, the kinetic upscaling yields a predator-dependent prey growth term taking a logistic form with an effective carrying capacity. This carrying capacity depends non-monotonically on predator density because prey redistribute between active and refuge states. Since density-dependent competition acts separately within these behavioral states, intermediate predator levels reduce aggregate crowding and thereby increase the effective carrying capacity of the total prey population. Overall, our derivation provides a mechanistic link connecting predator-induced behavioral shifts, refuge use, and macroscopic cross-diffusion.

ScaleBrain: A Scale-Consistent Framework for EEG Foundation Models

Minyu Zhou, Yajing Wu, Yongqiang Tang, Wang Jia, Wensheng Zhang

Foundation models pre-trained on large unlabeled electroencephalography (EEG) corpora have emerged as a transformative paradigm for Brain-Computer Interface (BCI) applications. Recent EEG foundation models have shown that multi-scale representations derived from fixed-length patches can improve EEG representation learning. Existing frameworks commonly construct temporal scales through feature-level operations. However, these operations leave the correspondence between learned features and scale-specific characteristics of the original EEG signals implicit. Moreover, when the self-supervised objective is not aligned with the multi-scale input representations, information at some temporal scales may receive insufficient supervision during pre-training. In light of this, we propose ScaleBrain, a scale-consistent pre-training framework that explicitly coordinates multi-scale representations of EEG from both the model input and optimization objective sides. Specifically, ScaleBrain introduces multi-scale EEG tokenization to represent the same EEG segment through analysis windows with different temporal spans and shared temporal anchors, and pairs these representations with scale-specific reconstruction to align with the multi-scale input structure and provide supervision across temporal resolutions during self-supervised learning. We further develop an axial spatio-temporal attention mechanism to contextualize the fused multi-scale representation along spatial and temporal dimensions. Extensive evaluations on six public BCI benchmarks against 12 representative baselines show that ScaleBrain achieves the highest mean Balanced Accuracy on five datasets and ranks third on the remaining one, with only 5.0M parameters, demonstrating strong performance and favorable parameter efficiency.
