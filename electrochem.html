Bond-valence theory is a general electrostatic valence rule

M. L. Whittaker

Pauling's electrostatic valence rule assigns to each bond of a cation of charge $z$ with $n$ anion neighbors the strength $z/n$. Bond-valence theory replaces the equal split of valence among bonds by $s=\exp[(R_0-d)/B]$ in the bond length $d$ and takes the sum rule, that the valences at each atom add to its formal charge, as its first axiom. Here the bond valence is defined in the electron density. Atomic basins of a first-principles density are bounded by faces, one per bond critical point, and the electric flux of that density through a face is a charge associated with the bond. The fluxes leaving a cation sum to its basin charge by Gauss's law, so on this surface the sum rule is a theorem. Across 3680 oxides, halides, chalcogenides and pnictides the flux follows the bond-valence law site by site, with a median deviation of ten percent. The softness $B$ varies nearly an order of magnitude from stiff sulfides to softer arsenides. In all cases it is set by the electrostatic flux at atomic basin faces. $R_0$ then follows from the bond lengths and their differences. Anion valences equal those of bonded cations to within 5\% for most of the oxide, fluoride, sulfide and nitride sites. Transferability of $(R_0,B)$ between compounds fails in 96 of 106 families, suggesting that the softness is a local site property defined by the basin charge rather than a transferable constant of a given cation--anion pair. Eliminating $B$ by the sum rule gives $s=(z/n)^{(R_0-d)/(R_0-d_B)}$, Pauling's strength raised to a distance exponent at the softness-weighted mean bond length $d_B$. Thus, bond-valence theory is Pauling's rule with a distance exponent, and the electron density supplies the surface on which the two are equivalent.

Minimal model of a Plateau-Rayleigh instability of a solid binary alloy nanowire: impacts of surface segregation

Mikhail Khenner

Minimal model and its linear stability analysis (LSA) for Plateau-Rayleigh (PR) instability of an intermixed, solid binary alloy nanowire is presented. Model accounts only for the surface diffusions of the alloy components without anisotropies of a surface energies, and for a classical thermodynamic surface segregation. Model allows to assess a deviation of a longwave instability cutoff wavelength $λ_c$ from the classical value $2πr_0$ (a wire circumference) as a function of a surface composition deviation from its equilibrium value, a wire radius, and a bulk concentration of a minority component. Results of LSA are presented for AuNi, PtNi, and CoNi nanowires, where an important distinction is that a surface segregation curve for CoNi is qualitatively different from the one for AuNi and PtNi. For each nanowire there is a sizable region of a parameter space where $λ_c<2πr_0$ and correspondingly, a fastest growing perturbation wavelength $λ_{max}$ is also less than a wire circumference. This is in stark contrast to PR instability of a single-component nanowire transporting mass by surface diffusion - in this case $λ_c$ and $λ_{max}$ are larger than a wire circumference. Strong surface segregation is primarily responsible for unusually small $λ_c$ and $λ_{max}$. Results suggest that a large number of small and closely spaced nanoparticles may emerge upon disintegration via annealing of a binary alloy nanowire.

Efficient simulations of spectra and transport for multiorbital quantum impurities

Hao-En Li, Zhen Huang, Lin Lin

Quantum impurity calculations become challenging when multiple correlated orbitals are treated explicitly, particularly in nonequilibrium steady states. We introduce Coupled-Cluster Coupled Lindblad (C$^3$L), a deterministic approach that combines the recently developed coupled-Lindblad compact bath representation with coupled-cluster theory to compute steady states and Green's functions directly in imaginary and real frequency. C$^3$L has favorable polynomial computational complexity and avoids the sign problem of quantum Monte Carlo (QMC). In a real-time multiorbital benchmark, it agrees closely with state-of-the-art QMC while reducing computational cost by four to five orders of magnitude, with a further 10--100-fold speedup on a GPU. Beyond the practical reach of established real-time QMC solvers, four-orbital nonequilibrium impurities with general interactions and biased reservoirs become accessible with C$^3$L. We obtain resonance-resolved transport signatures relevant to scanning tunneling microscopy experiments on molecular junctions.

Analog signal processing, storage, and demultiplexing via in-materia wave-based computing

Saeed Behboodi, Mohamed Mousa, Mostafa Nouh

The ability of mechanical systems to perform basic computations has gained traction in recent years, providing a robust supplement to digital computing in off-grid, low-power, and extreme environments where electronics are inaccessible or risk failure. However, prior work has focused on Boolean logic applied via bistable structures, snap-through mechanisms, or origami lattices. Digital signal processing (DSP) underpins a wide range of computational tasks, providing the foundation for information encoding, manipulation, and analysis. In contrast to conventional DSP approaches, we present a new paradigm for analog information processing, where an elastic substrate houses a spatial wave-based platform for mechanical signal manipulation in the temporal domain. The proposed metasurface-based system exploits temperature-dependent phase transitions of shape memory alloys (SMAs) to realize key signal-processing functionalities, including gating, retention, and programmable delay of impinging mechanical loads. In doing so, we address a new set of challenges by manipulating transient mechanical waves through dispersive and lossy media. Building on this capability, we introduce the concept of mechanical wave-based memory, in which a locally resonant metamaterial's dispersion characteristics are actively reconfigured to achieve programmable delays while preserving spatial waveform integrity, and demonstrate its utility in the context of input shaping and frequency demultiplexing of superimposed waves. More broadly, the modular and scalable nature of the demonstrated framework paves the way for advanced mechanical computing architectures, enabling higher-order operations such as signal classification, parallel processing, and reservoir computing through the controlled sequencing and temporal alignment of mechanical signals.

Ferron polariton Hall effect: Deflecting optically rectified polarization with magnetic fields

Omer Yaniv, Dominik M. Juraschek

Ferrons are elementary excitations of ferroelectric order that can propagate and transport polarization when excited with light. Here, we show theoretically that ferron polaritons exhibit a Hall effect: an applied magnetic field generates polarization transport perpendicular to the propagation direction of a ferron polariton excited by an incident terahertz pulse. We demonstrate this effect for the example of ferroelectric SnTe using first-principles calculations and a microscopic theory of the phonon-polariton current. The direction and magnitude of the transverse polarization transport can be controlled by the polarization of the terahertz pulse and the applied magnetic field. Our results establish a route toward light-controlled directional transport of electric polarization in ferroelectrics.

Long-time Brownian dynamics of hard and sticky colloids by geometric projection

Rémi Martin, Sandrine Duluard, Céline Merlet

Particle-resolved colloid simulations are often limited by the fastest microscopic time scale retained in the model, which can be many orders of magnitude shorter than the collective process of interest. This is especially restrictive for aggregating colloids, where explicit near-contact potentials force the time integration to resolve the rapid positional relaxation of bonded pairs within narrow attractive wells. Here we introduce an overdamped constraint-based Brownian dynamics method that replaces the time-resolved integration of stiff near-contact forces by a geometric projection step. After each free Brownian update, hard-sphere exclusion, sticky bonds, and hard-wall interactions are enforced by projecting the particle positions onto the constrained configuration set. The method preserves hard-sphere structure and diffusion, recovers the aggregation kinetics of sticky hard spheres, and extends the physical time reached at fixed computational cost by one to two orders of magnitude relative to the explicit-potential simulations tested here. This acceleration makes Brownian-dominated, low-Péclet electrophoretic deposition accessible to particle-scale simulations, revealing a pathway in which the suspension first densifies and only subsequently develops a bonded network. Implemented as a LAMMPS fix, the framework provides a modular route to long-time Brownian simulations of dense constrained colloidal systems.

Periodically poled lithium niobate with submicrometer periodicity using electric-field poling technique

Yuchen Zhang, Sanbing Li, Shengbo Song, Xitan Xu, Xudong Tong et al. (12 authors)

A series of monocrystalline lithium niobate (LN) thin films on low-resistivity silicon (LNOS) substrates, with thicknesses ranging from micrometers down to tens of nanometers, were prepared through chemical-mechanical polishing of pristine crystal films. By employing the electric-field poling technique, high-quality periodically poled lithium niobate (PPLN) structures with submicrometer periodicity were fabricated in these LNOSs, and full-thickness polarization reversal beneath the poling electrodes with a well-defined depth profile was achieved. The coercive field was found to follow the Kay-Dunn scaling law, while the lateral domain broadening generally increased with increasing film thickness, and it remained approximately constant across different electrode linewidths at a given film thickness. PPLN structures with periods ranging from 6 $μ$m down to 200 nm were demonstrated. These findings provide guidance for poling electrode design and lateral domain broadening pre-compensation, offering a practical route toward scalable domain engineering in LN thin films with promising potential for compact and highly integrated nonlinear photonic devices.

Optical Ceiling on Polaron Mobility in Disordered Molecular Semiconductors: Lessons from Eumelanin

Alvan Hunt

Polaron hopping in disordered molecular semiconductors such as eumelanin is set by energetic disorder $σ$ and reorganization energy $λ$. We show that the reduced polaron absorption band encodes this transport problem. Its value at zero energy fixes the mean hop rate, which bounds the mobility in any dimension. For Gaussian disorder and classical vibrations its centroid is $E_op=λ+(1-c)σ^2/k_BT$, its variance obeys the Hush relation, and the bound is the Marcus rate at $λ\to E_op$; a 1D lower bound vanishes at $k_BT^*=(1-c)σ^2/λ$, read from optics.

Ta-based S-I-S junctions for integrated superconducting Electronics

Trent Borman, Cameron Bullock, Cole Chason, Bradley Christensen, Jonathan Cochran et al. (27 authors)

In this work, we have presented S-I-S Josephson junctions with $\rmα$-Ta electrodes and a ${\rm TaO}_x$ barrier. We grew base and top $α$-Ta electrodes using seed layer engineering, showing both Nb and ${\rm TaN}_x$ are viable seed layer choices, and particularly emphasize that at room temperature a re-seed layer is required to nucleate $\rmα$-Ta for the top electrode. However, we note that the choice of seed layer can have subtle implications on the electrical tunneling behavior of the junctions. We used room-temperature probing of variable junction areas, combined with Ambegaokar-Baratoff physics, to characterize the wafer scale critical current densities. For 0.4 $μ$m$^2$ junctions, we found junction spreads as low as 2.0% across the 20 x 20 mm chip relevant for device performance. The entire process was carried out using standard industry foundry processing using 150 mm wafer tooling, which is an important step towards proving that Ta-based junctions can be implemented at scale. We have successfully fabricated and tested junctions with $\rmα$-Ta electrodes and a ${\rm TaO}_x$ insulating tunnel barrier, demonstrating that this is a material platform which merits further investigation and studies for potential application in classical and quantum superconducting technologies.

Band structure of two-dimensional graphene and of one-dimensional nanoribbons via variational quantum algorithms: from energies to wavefunction quality

Raúl Medrano Millán, Rodrigo Gil-Merino y Rubio, Juan Borge, Unai Aseguinolaza Aguirreche

Simulating quantum many-body systems classically encounters exponential bottlenecks, which quantum devices circumvent by natively encoding wavefunctions. In the Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum (NISQ) era, however, hardware noise constrains simulation accuracy. As a stepping stone toward strongly correlated materials, we compute the band structure of 2D monolayer graphene and 1D nanoribbons (zigzag and armchair) using a single variational workflow in the $p_z$ tight-binding approximation. Non-interacting ground states also provide essential initial reference states for subspace and warm-started variational methods to solve interacting spectra. By mapping the Bloch Hamiltonian to Pauli operators, we extract the ground band via the Variational Quantum Eigensolver (VQE) and excited subbands via Variational Quantum Deflation (VQD), validating our approach from ideal simulations to execution on physical IBM Quantum processors. In ideal simulations, the workflow reproduces exact diagonalization results with high precision, whereas hardware noise introduces energy deviations of a few hundredths of the hopping parameter. While qubit counts scale logarithmically with ribbon width, fixed-depth ansatz accuracy degrades with Hilbert space dimension. Crucially, deepening the ansatz improves ideal simulation accuracy by over three orders of magnitude but worsens energy estimates under noise, proving that practical circuit depth is bounded by hardware noise rather than expressivity. Finally, we complement energy evaluations with a wavefunction-level diagnostic measuring subspace containment. This diagnostic captures degenerate manifolds and exposes optimization failure modes disguised by energy agreement alone. Operating directly on NISQ hardware, this validated workflow establishes a controlled baseline for studying topological and strongly correlated phases in realistic materials.
