ThinkWorld: World Imagination Reinforces Chain-of-Dynamics Reasoning

Chaehyun Kim, Chi-Pin Huang, Jisoo Kim, Min-Hung Chen, Yu-Chiang Frank Wang et al. (7 authors)

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models have made substantial progress in robotic manipulation, but their generalization remains constrained by the diversity and coverage of action-labeled robot demonstrations. Human videos provide much broader behavioral and environmental experience, while learning from them faces a fundamental trade-off between scalable data and explicit action grounding. We introduce ThinkWorld, a VLA learning framework that grounds action reasoning in world dynamics and strengthens it through reinforcement learning with world imagination. ThinkWorld represents each action thought as a Chain-of-Dynamics (CoD), a sequence of discrete motion tokens that captures the intermediate dynamics connecting the current world to a future state and serves as a common action representation across embodiments. We first initialize CoD with action-labeled demonstrations, then predict the future induced by each CoD and reinforce those whose predictions better match the observed outcome. This enables videos without action annotations to provide direct supervision from observed state transitions, allowing human and robot experience to refine the same reasoning space. Experiments in simulation and on real robots demonstrate effective human-to-robot transfer and strong generalization to unseen tasks and environments.

Pushing Dexterity and Safety for In-Hand Object Pose Reaching That Works Remarkably Well

Ian Chuang, Hiya Shah, Vamsi Chilamkur, Iman Soltani, Claire Tomlin

Dexterous manipulation with multi-fingered hands is the next frontier of robotics, yet learned hand skills remain limited to simple grasps or basic repeated motions, such as rotating an object. As robots attempt more demanding in-hand maneuvers, safety, such as not dropping the object, becomes critical, yet it is rarely measured. We study in-hand object pose reaching, a task that demands diverse and difficult grasps: moving an object to almost any position and orientation within reach of the fingers, at any wrist orientation, through long sequences of consecutive goals. We present a recipe for training such policies for a LEAP hand in simulation without human data, built on a range of insights, from diverse resets and goals via generated grasp datasets to an adaptive friction curriculum. With careful system identification, the policies transfer zero-shot to the real hand, using either motion capture or a single wrist-mounted depth camera, and prove remarkably capable and robust. On top of the task policy, we add a learned residual safety filter with adjustable conservativeness, letting us tune the balance between performance and safety and greatly reduce object drops. In 13.3 hours of real-robot evaluation over 4,800 unseen goals across 4 objects and diverse wrist orientations, the state-based policy reaches 95.2% of goals within 2 cm and 0.4 rad, and the filter cuts its drops by 75% while still reaching 91.5% of goals. The policies can also spin a lightbulb held in an arbitrary pose, reach out-of-distribution goals, withstand fast arm motion and perturbations, and, needing only a wrist camera, serve as portable skills such as screwing in a lightbulb. We release the full recipe as a platform for studying performance, reliability, and safety in high-dimensional dexterous systems. Videos and more are available at https://ian-chuang.github.io/dexsafe/.

A heuristic for optimal coordination of autonomous vehicles: real-time algorithms

Saugat Shahi, Jonathan Frey, Filippo Fabiani, Moritz Diehl, Mario Zanon

When coordinating connected and automated vehicles at an unsignalized intersection, the crossing order and the vehicle trajectories must be decided jointly. For a fixed crossing order, the problem can be formulated conveniently in a bi-level form where each vehicle solves its own optimal control problem, whose optimal value is a function of its entry and exit times, and a coordinator optimizes these times subject to precedence constraints. We compute the first and second order sensitivities of lower-level problems, which are required to solve the upper-level problem, by means of algorithmic differentiation using the real-time software package \texttt{acados}. This implementation exploits tailored algorithms to obtain a computationally efficient implementation of the optimal coordination algorithm. We validate our implementation in numerical simulations that demonstrate both real-time feasibility and effectiveness of our algorithm.

Perceiving and Predicting Dynamic Sports: Spatiotemporal Intelligence in Vision Models for Egocentric Tennis Video

Tudor Braicu, Joseph DelPreto, Jared Boyer, Mónika Farsang, Maximilian Stölzle et al. (7 authors)

Agile robots require spatiotemporal intelligence to interpret scenes, reason about interactions, and anticipate future events. Yet it remains unclear how well foundation vision models support these capabilities, particularly from a moving egocentric camera, which is a natural view for robots. To investigate this, we collect an annotated tennis dataset and train a suite of vision models on spatiotemporal tasks. Our dataset contains egocentric video synchronized with five allocentric videos, estimated global positions, labeled contact times, and annotated stroke types. We then evaluate seven prediction tasks from egocentric images: 2D ego player and 3D ball localization, future player and ball trajectories, forehand or backhand stroke anticipation, time to the next ego player ball contact, and 3D ball position at that contact. We compare image encoders, video encoders, and vision-language models with jointly trained prediction heads. The best performing models achieve mean current-frame localization errors of 0.14 m for the ego player and 0.54 m for the ball, future forecasting errors of 0.34 m for the ego player and 0.91 m for the ball over 1.1 s, and a mean contact-time prediction error of 0.14 s. Auxiliary depth information substantially improves 3D ball localization, suggesting that inferring metric depth remains a challenge. Longer image histories do not improve ball forecasting, indicating that representations exploit scene context rather than rely solely on inter-frame motion. Finally, we propose a conditional diffusion model to generate whole-body kinematic tennis motion from vision encoder predictions on egocentric video at real-time rates, adapting movements as predictions are refined. These results move towards a deeper understanding of how foundation vision models can support real-time dynamic robots using egocentric video.

RESPLE2: Inertial-Informed Recursive Non-Uniform Spline Estimation for Multi-Sensor Odometry with Global Refinement

Ziyu Cao, William Talbot, Song Li, Pakpong Chirarattananon, Fredrik Gustafsson et al. (7 authors)

We present RESPLE2, a recursive multi-sensor state estimation framework using non-uniform B-splines for 6-DoF motion representation. By exploiting the knot-clamping boundary property, we propose a theoretically grounded mechanism for exact control-point prediction directly informed by inertial kinematics along the trajectory, with knot placement adapted to motion and measurement availability. Within an iterated extended Kalman filter, we perform this prediction using a subset of inertial measurements. A subsequent reset step preserves trajectory consistency as the knots extend. The update step further incorporates heterogeneous sensor measurements at their native timestamps through continuous-time observation models, supporting flexible combinations of LiDARs, cameras, inertial measurement units, and leg kinematics. We further develop a lightweight global optimization module that directly refines non-uniform B-spline control points by incorporating loop-closure constraints derived from LiDAR-visual cues. We conduct extensive evaluations on public and experimental datasets across handheld, tracked, legged, and hybrid aerial-legged platforms, covering highly dynamic maneuvers with extreme accelerations. Compared with state-of-the-art multi-sensor odometry systems, RESPLE2 achieves competitive or superior estimation accuracy and robustness in the corresponding sensor configurations, particularly under aggressive motion, while maintaining real-time operation. We publicly release our code and experimental datasets at https://github.com/ASIG-X/RESPLE2.

EgoThumb: A Vision-Driven Supernumerary Robotic Thumb for Autonomous Grasp and Holding Assistance

Jianyu Hou, Jiawei Fu, Weikang Wan, Minghui Jiang, Jiajun Li et al. (6 authors)

Supernumerary robotic fingers augment human dexterity by enabling a single hand to perform complex "hold-and-manipulate" tasks that typically require bimanual coordination. However, existing devices are often hindered by bulky hardware that limits interaction to large objects and rely on explicit hand motion triggers that lack environmental context. To address these challenges, we present EgoThumb, a fully open-source, vision-driven supernumerary robotic thumb. EgoThumb features a compact, dual tendon-driven finger whose actuation is relocated to the forearm, reducing distal weight while enabling precise active joint control. The system utilizes a visual imitation learning policy that maps egocentric images from a palm-facing camera directly to continuous joint positions. By implicitly decoding human intent from visual cues capturing both the hand and the object of interest, EgoThumb achieves robust autonomous manipulation and strong generalization across diverse users and environments. All hardware designs and software implementations are fully open-sourced at https://ego-thumb.github.io.

Learning Visuomotor Quadrotor Flight Through Dynamic Narrow Gaps via Differentiable Simulation

Riccardo Bianco, Yunfan Ren, Jiaxu Xing, Davide Scaramuzza

Threading a quadrotor through a moving narrow gap is a demanding test of agile flight, requiring centimeter-level spatial alignment and precise temporal execution. We address this challenge using a control policy that operates directly on onboard visual and inertial measurements to output collective thrust and body-rate commands. The task is difficult because success lies on a thin feasible manifold: small errors in timing, position, attitude, or visual observability cause collision. Prior planning and learning methods rely on privileged state, teachers, planners, or distillation; learning dynamic narrow-gap traversal directly, from scratch, remains open. We introduce a differentiable moving-gap formulation that gates centering, alignment, and smooth collision terms by signed distance to a time-varying gap plane, turning a sparse traversal event into a dense pathwise objective. We integrate this formulation into a visual-feature-to-command policy trained in one stage from random initialization, without a teacher, planner, or distillation. We validate our approach in both synthetic and real-world experiments. In controlled benchmarks, we demonstrate that pathwise gradients significantly outperform score-function gradients for precision-critical tasks, yielding a 99.3% success rate in simulation after only a few minutes of training on a single GPU. Furthermore, the policy transfers directly to a real quadrotor, successfully crossing a dynamic narrow gap from varied initial conditions using onboard sensing and inertial measurements, achieving an 80% success rate over 10 physical trials.

Kinematic Constraint-Referenced Evaluation for Articulated Object Manipulation

Baptiste Bonin, Caro Strickland, Audrey Durand

Manipulation policies are evaluated predominantly by binary task success, which cannot tell whether the commanded gesture is the one the object's kinematics admit. We introduce Constraint-Referenced Strategy (CRS), which projects the commanded end-effector twist onto the object's joint axis, separating progress along the joint from off-constraint waste. CRS requires a joint model and a joint-following scripted controller derived from it, but no recorded demonstration or learned judge. On 74 simulated articulated objects, success registers three transfer failures as lower rates but attributes none, while the two CRS channels give each a distinct signature. A door-trained behavior-cloning policy opens drawers in 59 of 110 episodes, and in these passes it commands about $40\times$ the off-axis rotation of a drawer-trained policy's passes ($1.27$ vs.\ $0.032$ rad/s). Relative to a scripted controller scored with the same axis, CRS verdicts are unchanged under joint-axis errors up to $30^\circ$; absolute thresholds tolerate about $8^\circ$.

When, Where, and What to Correct: Human-Guided Adaptive Residual Correction for Robot Policies

Anukriti Singh, Maxime Caniot, Pratap Tokekar, Firas Abi-Farraj

Imitation policies that reliably execute most of a manipulation trajectory can still fail in localized regions requiring last-mile precision. Updating the full policy or exploring residual actions everywhere treats this local failure as a global learning problem. We introduce Adaptive Residual Correction (ARC), a lightweight post-training module that uses reinforcement learning (RL) to learn a residual correction on top of a frozen policy while using sparse human corrections to structure autonomous exploration. Rather than treating human corrections as imitation targets, ARC uses pre-intervention states to determine 'when' residual control should become active and correction geometry to determine 'where' in residual action space autonomous refinement should explore; task reward determines 'what' residual is ultimately executed. Across three precision-manipulation tasks on a real robot, ARC improves average task success from 33.3% to 75.0%. The human corrections define task-dependent 2-3 dimensional subspaces of the seven-joint residual space that retain 96.0-97.7% of squared correction magnitude. Together, these results show that sparse human corrections can localize and constrain autonomous post-training without requiring the observed correction itself to serve as the final action target.

Rendering-Free Event Frame Simulation for Fast Reinforcement Learning of Autonomous Robot Navigation

Dequan Ou, Austin Lopez, Quentin Missinne, Christophe De Wagter, Guido C. H. E. de Croon

Event cameras offer low latency, high dynamic range, and milliwatt-level power consumption, making them ideal for agile robots such as tiny drones navigating through cluttered environments. However, existing simulators generally render photorealistic images before converting them into events, making large-scale reinforcement learning prohibitively slow. We propose an analytic event simulator that exploits what event cameras fundamentally measure - moving contrast edges - to synthesize event streams directly from scene geometry and ego-motion without visual rendering. Our simulator generates a 64x64 event observation in 17 microseconds on a single GPU - over 400x faster than a state-of-the-art rendering pipeline - enabling end-to-end reinforcement learning for goal-directed obstacle avoidance to converge in under 30 minutes - three orders of magnitude faster than training on rendered events. Policies transfer zero-shot to physically based event simulations (ray-traced Isaac Sim). Our policy reaches 83% success in cluttered courses unseen during training (mid-difficulty, traversability probability approx. 0.57), at 2.4 m/s average goal velocity, and retains 70% in a simulated texture-dense indoor hall. Furthermore, we quantized the policy to a 1.2 MB int8 network running fully onboard a 56-gram Crazyflie equipped with a Prophesee GenX320 event camera and an OpenMV N6 microcontroller board. Without any fine-tuning, external processing, or positioning, the policy achieves an average success rate of 82% (5/5, 5/6, 4/6 flights) across three real-world obstacle courses of increasing difficulty. Code: https://github.com/tudelft/rendering-free-event-nav
