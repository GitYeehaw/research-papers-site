EEG Correlates of Verbal and Perceptual Reasoning

Gwyneth Rolph

Identifying the neural underpinnings of intelligence is one of the major goals of modern intelligence research. However, few imaging studies explore the high extremes of intelligence, and most use only a single composite IQ score. This study used quantitative EEG recordings to investigate correlations between the EEG variables of individual alpha peak frequency, relative power, and coherence, and scores on verbal and perceptual reasoning tests in participants with IQs from the low-average to exceptionally gifted ranges. Overall, the results did not reveal any pattern of significant relationships between these EEG measures and the two psychometric tests. Since this study had only a small sample, heavily skewed towards the high-IQ end of the scale, no definitive conclusions about the EEG measures and verbal and perceptual reasoning can be drawn. Nevertheless, it reveals many opportunities for further research, and suggestions are made for progressing this topic in the future.

Reciprocity coordinates a three-way trade-off in recurrent neural circuits

Xiaoyu Zhang, Mingyi Huang, Yixuan Cui, Xin Lu, Yuguo Yu

Biological neural circuits contain both unidirectional and reciprocal connections, but how reciprocity affects circuit computation remains unclear. We varied reciprocity \r{ho} across 21 levels in recurrent-network simulations while holding network size, connection density, and directed-edge count fixed. Low-reciprocity networks produced more diverse stimulus-driven activity and performed better on four continuous-input tasks, whereas higher reciprocity generally improved performance on three delayed-memory tasks. Increasing reciprocity reduced online memory capacity and accelerated recovery from perturbations. A post-cue noise experiment showed that higher reciprocity suppressed internal noise propagation but made cue-evoked states less distinguishable, and component matching separated the contributions of cue separation and within-cue dispersion to readout robustness. Within this ensemble, reciprocity thus mediates a three-way trade-off among representational capacity, local stability, and task-state separability: higher reciprocity lowers capacity, strengthens stability, and compresses separability. Whether it improves behaviour depends on whether the stability gain outweighs the lost separation between task states. Connectome and microcircuit datasets from five species showed intermediate reciprocity, while reciprocity varied substantially across adult Drosophila brain regions, providing compatible structural context and motivating circuit-specific functional tests.

How Long Is the Coast of a Neuron? Resolution-Dependent Interaction Order and Computational Capacity

Nima Dehghani

Mandlebrot's coastline paradox reframes measurement as a joint property of an object and its ruler. Here we ask how observational resolution shapes the apparent structure of neural computation. We introduce a scale-indexed decomposition that separates the predictive information captured by direct dependencies from the residual outside that model family. Exact constructions show that projection can convert higher-order dependence into pairwise structure. Projection can increase the direct fraction of surviving information even as total predictive information decreases. A schematic branch-to-soma cascade grounds these effects in local coincidence detection, saturating integration and somatic mixing. Resolving organized nonlinear subunits improves approximation and raises effective representational rank. In a nested hierarchy, these gains occur without increasing information about the target. This distinction separates information availability from access by a restricted readout. A point-neuron or direct population model therefore establishes an effective description at a specified scale. It does not uniquely identify the finer computation that generated the observed relation. Which resolution is adequate depends on the observable and perturbation of interest.

Effects of cortisol time-scales in a model of emotion regulation

Michael Buccino, Joshua Ginart, Eva Kaslik, Anca Radulescu

Stress reshapes cortico-limbic dynamics across multiple timescales, with important consequences for healthy and pathological arousal responses. We develop and analyze a Wilson-Cowan model of prefrontal-amygdala circuitry, incorporating excitatory and inhibitory populations involved in emotional regulation. The effects of stress cortisol are represented as history-dependent modulations of two stress-sensitive interactions: recurrent prefrontal excitation and inhibition of basolateral amygdala principal cells. Using numerical simulations and bifurcation analysis, we examine how this self-interacting system organizes transitions between stable activity and infra-slow oscillations, and how this structure changes with cortisol-related temporal memory. To distinguish the effects of memory temporal distribution from those of its timescale, we compare weak and strong Gamma kernels with a discrete-delay limit, representing progressively different ways in which past activity can influence current coupling. Introducing temporal memory reshapes the parameter region supporting oscillations and alters their persistence. Weak and strong Gamma kernels produce remarkably similar global dynamics, whereas the discrete-delay limit generates a substantially richer bifurcation structure, including quasiperiodic activity. Together, these results suggest that the dynamical effects of cortisol depend not only on the characteristic timescale of its action, but also on the shape of the temporal memory through which past neural activity influences the present. Different forms of temporal integration may provide a mechanism by which neural systems implement distinct functional dynamics, while changes in this integration could promote atypical or pathological rhythmic regimes.

Simulating Alzheimer's amyloid-like pathology on a fly central nervous system connectome

Taeho Jo

In Alzheimer's disease, the amount of pathology in the brain is an imperfect predictor of how much function is lost. Pathology leads to that loss through neural circuits, but these cannot be observed neuron by neuron in patients. This study therefore simulated the central nervous system of the adult male Drosophila melanogaster: 166,700 integrate-and-fire neurons wired by the MaleCNS connectome. The simulator reproduced a published fly brain model (rate correlation 0.9997). Three kinds of damage reported in amyloid-$β$ flies, a lowered spike threshold, synapse loss and neuron loss, were imposed with assumed magnitudes, and circuit responses were measured in 1,424 simulated flies. The number of neurons lost was a poor guide to the deficit, which depended partly on how many synaptic contacts went with them and whether they included input neurons or one command neuron. With the same 12% of neurons removed, odour-evoked whole-brain activity was 93%, 47% or 31% of the healthy value when damage fell on poorly connected, random or highly connected neurons; these flies lost 20%, 35% and 48% of contacts. When the contacts lost were equalised, the three groups differed less; whether the remaining difference reflects where the damage fell could not be established. In 48 flies, restoring only the contacts that surviving neurons had lost recovered 62% of the whole-brain deficit, and restoring the removed neurons 39%. In flies that had lost neurons delivering the odour, these accounted for about half the deficit due to neuron loss. One neuron could decide an outcome: all 66 flies that lost the giant fibre on the stimulated side, which commands escape, had a giant fibre rate below 100 Hz, against 1 of 254 that kept it. Whole-brain activity revealed damage at milder severity than the learning index, because it changed much relative to its variation between trials.

Deep Generative Models for Phylogenetic Inference with Complex Evolutionary Processes

Ethan Baron, Alan N. Amin, Andrew Gordon Wilson

Inference of phylogenetic trees from sequences plays a central role in understanding evolutionary relationships, with applications ranging from tracking pathogen spread to reconstructing the history of life. Conventionally, practitioners obtain posteriors over trees using likelihood-based methods like MCMC. However, likelihoods are only available for simple evolutionary models with restrictive assumptions. For realistic evolutionary models, inference using conventional methods is prohibitively expensive, inaccurate, or even impossible. In practice, practitioners settle for using as flexible a conventional method as possible, even though they expect it is misspecified. In principle, we could use simulation-based inference: simulate tree and sequence data from an evolutionary model and train a neural network to predict the posterior over trees given sequences. However it is a mathematical challenge to represent and train complex posterior distributions over trees. Here we present a flexible family of conditional generative models over trees. Our models iteratively build trees using three natural paradigms -- top-down, middle-out, or bottom-up -- and are trained using a discrete diffusion framework. For real and synthetic datasets of up to $65$ taxa and $>10^5$ tokens, our models recover MCMC posteriors much more accurately than the previous state-of-the-art simulation-based method Phyloformer 2. Finally, we compare to IQ-Tree, a popular flexible-but-misspecified conventional method. Our models match its tree estimates on simple data, but on more realistic data, our method is reliably more accurate.

Deep learning-based virtual perturbation reveals increasing heterogeneity of gene regulatory responses with aging

Yongjian He, Julia Hanna Haddad Guez, Xu-Wen Wang, Orr Levy

A central hypothesis in aging biology proposes that the gradual accumulation of stochastic molecular damage drives transcriptional deregulation (TD), ultimately leading to functional decline. However, existing single-cell analyses largely rely on static expression measurements and provide limited resolution of how regulatory responses change at the level of individual cells. Here, we leverage DKO, a deep learning framework that predicts cell-specific virtual gene perturbation effects from unperturbed transcriptomes, capturing cell-to-cell differences in regulatory context without requiring a reconstructed gene regulatory network or matched perturbation data. Applying DKO to single-cell transcriptomic datasets from humans, mice, Drosophila, and C. elegans revealed that the transcriptional consequences of perturbing the same gene became increasingly heterogeneous across cells with age, indicating that gene regulatory responses become less consistent and more dependent on cellular context. Furthermore, we find that cells with higher somatic mutation burdens have greater knockout-response variability, linking accumulated molecular damage to regulatory inconsistency. Within the same cellular populations, higher impact variability was also associated with higher senescence scores, linking transcriptional dysregulation to senescence-associated states. Our findings identify a progressive loss of regulatory robustness at both the gene and cell levels as a conserved feature of aging, linking stochastic molecular damage to increasing heterogeneity in cellular regulatory responses.

Two Routes to Interoceptive Prioritisation: Dissociating Likelihood Precision and Preference Reweighting in Active Inference

St John Grimbly, Nicolas Kuske, Evert A. Boonstra, Charel van Hoof, Rowan Hodson et al. (9 authors)

An embodied agent must prioritise among competing bodily needs. In active inference, it can respond to an urgent need by increasing the precision of information about that need or by increasing its preference for outcomes that relieve it. We compare these two interventions, separately and together, in a foraging gridworld with three lethal needs, and ask how each changes survival, perception and the body model the agent learns. Precision sharpens interoceptive posteriors and improves survival. Preference reweighting keeps bodily states further from their lethal boundaries and changes the states from which the agent learns, so its learned model covers fewer need levels, with lower quality across all levels but slightly higher quality on the levels it visits. An experiment on 72 further layouts does not establish the predicted preference benefit on difficult layouts, but both interventions improve average survival there and their interaction is positive. Survival alone therefore gives an incomplete account of how a model prioritises needs and learns about its body.

The influence of decay in dynamic models of gene regulatory networks

Oleksandr Pokutnyi, Felix Sadyrbaev

We investigate the influence of decay rates on the dynamics of three-dimensional gene regulatory networks modeled by nonlinear differential equations with logistic sigmoidal regulatory functions. Particular attention is paid to the existence, location, and stability of equilibrium points in systems involving numerous interacting parameters. An explicit analytical representation of a central equilibrium is obtained under suitable relations between the regulatory weights, thresholds, and decay coefficients, allowing its location to be determined without solving the corresponding nonlinear algebraic system numerically. General formulas for the characteristic polynomial of the linearized system are derived, and spectral conditions for instability are established for several classes of regulatory interactions. We demonstrate how variations in decay rates can change the number and stability of equilibria, transform oscillatory dynamics, and influence the emergence or suppression of complicated attractors. The possibility of chaotic behavior is investigated through eigenvalue configurations, Lyapunov exponents, and the Kaplan-Yorke dimension. Numerical examples illustrate the sensitivity of the dynamics to decay parameters, including the suppression of previously observed chaotic behavior. The results provide an analytical framework for investigating equilibrium configurations, stability transitions, and complex dynamics in parametrized gene regulatory networks.

Sense-Making phase transition framework for Affect

Miguel Bosch, Laura Fanfarillo, Gustavo Diez, Victor Gómez Mayordomo, J. Ricardo Arias-Gonzalez et al. (6 authors)

Contemporary accounts of affect emphasize different perspectives, ranging from the regulation of bodily states in allostatic approaches to the relational and sense-making character stressed by enactive theories. Here, we show that affect can be made explicit within the sense-making phase transition framework previously introduced for the perception of an inner sensation. Within this same formal structure, each stable minimum of the sense-making potential defines an affective state: its position determines valence, while its depth determines arousal. Affect therefore is not introduced as an independent ingredient, but emerges as the associated state in the valence-arousal space, providing a link between allostatic and enactive approaches to affect. This formulation distinguishes valence intensity from arousal, allows for dominant and secondary affective states, and naturally accommodates mixed-valence configurations beyond a strictly bipolar description. Revisiting representative landscapes of the model, we show how neutral, uncertain, polarized, and mixed-valence regimes arise as a function of contextual sensory information, learned historical evaluation, and externally supplied information. We further show that the resulting valence-arousal relation provides a theoretical basis for the upper-open V-shaped organization discussed in the affect literature, while also opening a natural route toward a dynamical description of affect and emotions.
