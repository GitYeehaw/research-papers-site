One Prior, Two Dynamics: Repurposing Video Diffusion Transformers for Self-Supervised Latent Action Learning

Yuxin Jiang, Wenqiang Sun, Jiaqi Li, Yuchao Gu, Lvmin Zhang et al. (7 authors)

Videos reveal how the world changes, but rarely the actions that cause those changes. Latent action learning recovers such supervision from visual transitions, yet often learns the underlying dynamics largely from scratch. We ask whether a pretrained video generator can instead provide the prior for both inverse and forward dynamics. We introduce O$^{2}$LaF, built on a simple principle: one prior, two dynamics. We initialize both models from the same pretrained video DiT and adapt them to their distinct roles: a feed-forward transition encoder with learnable action queries for inverse dynamics, and a causal, action-conditioned video generator for forward dynamics. By controlling information access and optimizing both dynamics end-to-end through a compact bottleneck, O$^{2}$LaF learns transferable actions from unlabeled videos. Across navigation and robotic manipulation, O$^{2}$LaF sets a new state of the art in few-shot action probing and enables stronger zero-shot action transfer across visual contexts and embodiments. Beyond generative video priors, we further extend our framework to predictive representation priors such as V-JEPA2, achieving competitive performance. Our findings suggest a broader paradigm for latent-action learning: repurposing pretrained video models to discover transferable actions and predict their consequences.

AgentRoboCritic: Understanding the Capability of Frontier Agents on Direct Robot Control

Yiyang Du, Yuxuan Kuang, Qitao Zhao, Yixuan Wang, Zhanqiu Guo et al. (10 authors)

Frontier multimodal language agents such as GPT-6 Astra have demonstrated strong capabilities in controlling robots directly via low-level numerical actions without high-level primitives. However, existing benchmarks report only episode-level success rates, which show neither how these agents succeed nor where they fail. In this paper, we present a systematic action-level analysis of frontier agents on direct robot control across 12 manipulation benchmarks. We introduce AgentRoboCritic, an intent-grounded critique framework in which the policy agent states a local intent alongside each action, and a critic evaluates the action's outcome and failure mode against this intent. Our action-level analysis demonstrates the promising capabilities of frontier agents on direct robot control, including closed-loop recovery from local execution failures, reliable semantic grounding across diverse scenes, and strong zero-shot manipulation without task-specific adaptation. It also identifies their current limitations, including task-relevant physical execution bottlenecks such as grasping and alignment, and uneven recovery across failure modes. Finally, we validate our critic on a 400-action human-annotated benchmark and release a dataset of 12,844 annotated actions alongside a fine-tuned open-source critic, Muse-Glimmer-30B, whose failure-mode accuracy rises from 32.58% to 74.24%, enabling low-cost action-level analysis.

A Tutorial on Complementarity-Based Contact Models for Robotics

Onur Beker, A. René Geist, Michael Posa, Marc Toussaint, Justin Carpentier et al. (6 authors)

Simulating rigid-body dynamics with contact is fundamental to robotics, from motion synthesis and control to mechanism design. Many widely used simulators rely on cone complementarity formulations, whose physical assumptions and numerical solution require substantial background. While excellent surveys, monographs, and course notes exist, they mostly address computational mechanics or computer graphics or document a single simulator. To the best of our knowledge, a self-contained pedagogical treatment for robotics, bringing these formulations and their solvers together with complete derivations and a unified notation, does not yet exist. This tutorial addresses this gap by connecting rigid-body dynamics, conic optimization, and frictional contact. In particular, we explain how complementarity conditions encode non-penetration and frictional dissipation, determining separation, sticking, and sliding without prescribed contact modes. We distinguish equivalent transcriptions from convex relaxations, showing how the latter can introduce artifacts such as lift-off and creep. We examine the existence and uniqueness of motion and contact forces, including the solver's role in selecting among multiple solutions. We then compare first-order and Newton-based methods, explain regularization and proximal strategies, and discuss trade-offs between computational cost, robustness, and physical fidelity. Connections to widely used simulators, optimal control, and differentiable simulation help readers interpret simulation results, choose suitable formulations and solvers, and develop their own contact dynamics routines.

PGX: Physically Grounded Execution for Long-Horizon Humanoid Loco-Manipulation

Kai Lu, Chenyang Ma, Ioannis Havoutis

Robotic vision-language-action (VLA) models can execute diverse individual manipulation skills, yet they still struggle with long-horizon loco-manipulation tasks, where temporal structure, skills, and precision are tightly coupled within a monolithic model. We propose Physically Grounded Execution (PGX), a framework that offloads temporal and precision control to two classical training-free components, allowing the VLA to focus on stage-specific skill dynamics. PGX decomposes long-horizon loco-manipulation into three layers: (i) a predicate-based state machine governs task progression, using physical predicates to gate phase transitions, trigger recovery, and coordinate navigation and waiting. Shared predicate semantics connect offline demonstration segmentation, online execution, and stage-wise evaluation; (ii) VLA skills operate within active manipulation phases, with flat or stage-trained models concentrating on task-relevant actions; and (iii) world-coordinate locking (WCL) maintains spatial consistency through base station keeping, object-relative positioning, and world-anchored contact targets. We demonstrate the effectiveness of PGX with extensive experiments conducted on three simulated tasks and one real-world task using a Unitree G1 humanoid robot for long-horizon loco-manipulation, showing improvements in stage progression and task success rates. PhyDEX: A Benchmark for Physical Reasoning in Vision-Language-Action Models via Dynamic Extrinsic Dexterity

Hao Vo, Taisei Hanyu, Yuki Ikebe, Pham Tri Quang, Sreevenkata Anjani Tishita Godavarthi et al. (11 authors)

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models have advanced rapidly in language-conditioned robotic manipulation, yet existing benchmarks mainly evaluate gripper--object contact and largely ignore how objects interact with the environment through gravity, friction, inertia, and mass distribution. As a result, it remains unclear whether current policies truly understand physics or merely interpolate from their training data. We address this gap through the lens of dynamic extrinsic dexterity and introduce PhyDEX, a throwing benchmark in which a robot must infer an object's mass, center of mass, and friction to send it to a destination beyond its kinematic workspace. PhyDEX contains 35K trajectories (143 hours) across nine tasks and 100 scenes, covering uniform, irregular, and dynamic-mass objects with static, multiple, and moving destinations. All data are generated by a scalable, fully automatic pipeline that combines grasp generation, motion planning, and closed-loop throw optimization under randomized physical properties. We evaluate three representative VLAs, GR00T N1.7, DynamicVLA, and ForceVLA, and find that all achieve success rates below 3\%. Our analysis reveals two key bottlenecks: limited robustness of pretrained models, which causes most failures before the throw, and physical blindness, as providing ground-truth physical properties roughly doubles success. Experiments on a humanoid robot and in the real world show the same failure patterns. We hope PhyDEX will drive the development of physically grounded robot policies.

Learning Control-Relevant Koopman Representations from Input-Output Trajectories

Maria Bartzioka, Mohammad Khosravi

Predictive accuracy alone does not ensure that data-driven Koopman representations are suitable for output-feedback control. This paper develops a continuous-time framework for learning control-relevant Koopman representations using only measured input-output trajectories. A causal encoder constructs the lifted state from a finite input-output window, and an additive lifted model is identified by integral regression. We distinguish observability, input-map compatibility, controllability, and data informativity. We characterize compatibility with a constant lifted input matrix, quantify input-map mismatch, and, under exact lifting assumptions, relate iterated Lie brackets to the controllable subspace of the lifted realization. A covariance-normalized finite-horizon observability criterion is optimized subject to separate bounds on output-prediction and temporal-consistency errors. Under the stated exactness and observability assumptions, the encoded lifted state depends only on the current nonlinear state. For approximate representations, we bound the difference between encodings of distinct past trajectories ending at the same state. An exactly liftable controlled system provides analytical ground truth, while a controlled Duffing oscillator illustrates the approximate setting, for which an informative exact additive lifting on an open set is shown to be impossible. On the exact benchmark, encoder initialization determines whether the learned representation recovers the analytical lift. In the reported Duffing experiments, observability promotion improves nominal Gramian measures without improving observer-based estimation or tracking, while updating the lifted-state estimate through the encoder at every sampling instant has a larger effect on tracking accuracy.

From power to spectrum: an off-the-shelf photodiode turned into a spectrometer by its bias alone

Roi Yozevitch, Shmuel Sternklar, Ziv Glasser

Computational spectrometers replace dispersive optics with detectors whose spectral response can be varied, but every reported single-detector device obtains this response through dedicated fabrication: two-dimensional heterostructures, graded epitaxial junctions, organic stacks, filters or photonic circuits. We demonstrate a generic, fabrication-free alternative in which a standard commercial photodiode becomes a spectrometer through its electrical drive alone, requiring no modification of the device, no optical filter and no modulation of the light. The mechanism is bias-modulated optoelectronic chromatic dispersion (OED): light of different wavelengths is absorbed at different depths, and a sinusoidal bias changes how carriers generated at each depth are collected, so that every spectrum is encoded in the shape of the photocurrent waveform over one bias period. Applied to an unmodified InGaAs PIN photodiode at room temperature, a 10 us waveform decoded by a neural network reconstructs spectra of one to three laser lines across the 80 nm of the telecom C+L band, locating the lines to 0.3-0.4 nm, counting them in about 99% of measurements and identifying their power levels, with line shifts tracked to tens of picometres. Because OED relies on universal semiconductor transport physics, the approach is material-agnostic and applies in principle to any off-the-shelf photodiode, turning detectors already deployed as power monitors into low-cost spectral sensors.

Thermal-Guided Ensemble Reinforcement Learning for Regime-Adaptive Short-Term GPU Power Forecasting in AI Data Centers

Yuanshuo Zhang, Ying Zhang, Ziying Wang, Fanping Sui

AI data centers (AIDCs) introduce bursty and regime-dependent load patterns, posing significant challenges for reliable energy management and predictive grid control. To address these challenges, this letter proposes a thermal-guided ensemble reinforcement learning framework for probabilistic AIDC load forecasting. A soft actor-critic (SAC) agent with a simplex-preserving policy adaptively weights LSTM, TCN, and Transformer predictors at every time step based on their realized errors. A thermal-guided neural correction estimates the ensemble residual, while a sensitivity-projection regularizer enforces the non-negative sensitivity of power to GPU utilization and temperature. The corrected ensemble output is further processed by a Bayesian probabilistic layer to produce predictive distributions and quantify forecasting uncertainty. Case studies on 1-second MIT Supercloud telemetry show that the proposed method reduces the normalized mean absolute error to about 1% across all computing regimes, compared with 4-11% for deep learning-based and ensemble learning benchmarks, and converges faster with narrower prediction intervals than a DRL ensemble without thermal guidance.

ACE-Brain-0.7: Learning Execution-Grounded Reasoning for Embodied Agents

Yinhe Wang, Shijun Shi, Feng Pan, Tao Tao, Wei Mao et al. (14 authors)

Reliable reasoning for long-horizon embodied tasks requires predictions grounded in the observed execution state. We present ACE-Brain-0.7, a 9B vision--language model that combines spatial grounding, task understanding, and progress estimation. To train the model, we curate 3.7 million question--answer pairs, drawing execution supervision from egocentric human videos, robot episodes, and simulation. These questions span different stages of an execution and connect task-level judgments with observed progress and outcomes. We then fine-tune Qwen3.5-9B on this collection together with additional task, spatial-grounding, and progress-estimation data. Across six public task-reasoning benchmarks, ACE-Brain-0.7 achieves an average score of 76.5, ahead of GPT-6 Astra at 72.7 and Claude Opus 5.5 at 73.5. Our model also leads the evaluated models in spatial grounding, averaging 75.90 across seven benchmarks and reaching 91.25 Acc@0.5 on RefCOCOg. Controlled evaluations further indicate that our task-only model makes more effective use of visual history and more accurately distinguishes execution states. Together, these results show that ACE-Brain-0.7 effectively combines spatial perception with temporal understanding, delivering strong execution-grounded reasoning and comparing favorably with closed-source models.

JARVIS: An Agent That Designs, Builds, and Tests Circuit Boards on Its Own

Shibo Zhou, Tianqi Hong, Zheng Yan, He Yang

Power electronics is central to the energy transition, but developing new hardware still relies on human-led design, fabrication, testing, and iteration. We present JARVIS, an embodied AI agent that autonomously closes this loop for circuit boards. JARVIS maintains machine-specific capabilities and constraints, scans its workspace using a robot-mounted 3D scanner, and constructs an executable digital twin in which it rehearses robot motions, fabrication steps, and circuit behavior before consuming materials. A single text instruction initiates the physical experiment. Using unmodified desktop equipment, the agent coordinates a robot arm, a PCB printer that deposits conductive silver ink and solder paste, and a pick-and-place machine. It verifies progress using cameras and sensors, then tests the fabricated circuit with an OPAL-RT real-time simulator and an oscilloscope. Based on the measurements, JARVIS diagnoses deviations from the design and generates a revised design in KiCad for subsequent rehearsal. In a demonstration involving a 60 Hz notch filter for grid-connected converter sensing, JARVIS completed a first-run experiment in 2 h 39 min without human intervention, achieving 50.6 dB attenuation at 60 Hz. Its digital-twin rehearsal identified five issues before fabrication, including a test criterion that would have incorrectly rejected a functioning board. During execution, the agent also resolved encountered faults autonomously. These results demonstrate an integrated agent-driven workflow for physical design, fabrication, measurement, and iterative improvement of power-electronic circuitry. To our knowledge, JARVIS is the first system in power electronics in which an agent closes the physical design-build-test-learn loop for circuit boards.
