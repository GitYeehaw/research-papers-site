Mutual Information in High-Dimensional Neural Coding

Zimo Li, Yuchang Tian, Matthew P. Leighton, Christopher W. Lynn

Estimating mutual information in large neural populations is limited by the exponential growth of possible neural response patterns. Here we apply a Monte Carlo framework to make this problem tractable by sampling from a conditionally independent population model fit from single-neuron responses. The estimator accurately recovers analytical ground truth, corrects finite-data bias, and scales to empirical recordings containing 100 to 10,000 neurons from visual cortex, hippocampus, and retina. Across systems, stimulus uncertainty decays exponentially with population size, revealing a simple scaling law for high-dimensional neural codes.

Panoramic modelling of single-cell transcriptomes at scale with CellNet

Xiaoqing Lian, Pei Liu, Jiahui Tan, Luoda Tan, Chaoyi Li et al. (8 authors)

Comprehensively characterizing cellular diversity is critical for understanding tissue development, identifying disease-associated populations and investigating gene programmes linked to specialized functions. Advances in single-cell profiling reveal additional cell-specific transcripts and molecular associations, yet many foundation models represent only a restricted portion of this increasingly rich molecular information. Here we present CellNet, which enables panoramic modelling of single-cell transcriptomes by integrating DNA-derived gene identities and measured RNA expression in a shared image representation. Pretrained on 231 million cell profiles spanning 40 species, CellNet improves rare-population and condition-associated state recognition in evaluated cohorts while retaining competitive general cell representations. It predicts withheld coding-gene expression and selected non-coding targets in total-RNA data, and recovers lineage-specific expression patterns in spatial measurements. Prediction also extends across species, including to targets without expression-pretraining exposure. Its gene representations retrieve known non-coding modules and developmental or neural programmes, including from sparsely detected RNA queries. Together, these results establish CellNet as a scalable framework for interpreting cellular diversity through increasingly comprehensive transcriptomic measurements.

Transferable conditional brain charts link cortico-subcortical structure to Alzheimer's disease progression

Nitin Sharma, Sicheng Dai, Francesco A. Mallus, Atharva Kand, Marc Lemnitzer et al. (32 authors)

Normative models chart how brain measures vary across the lifespan, expressing each individual as a deviation from a healthy reference. However, most regional models of cortical thickness disregard dependencies between cortical and subcortical anatomy, and transferring them to a new site typically requires retraining or a recalibration of location and scale alone. We condition lifespan norms of cortical thickness on subcortical volume, such that each deviation indicates whether an individual's cortex lies within the healthy range given their subcortical anatomy, and make them transferable. In 21,351 healthy individuals from 216 sites, conditioning on ten subcortical structures improved out-of-sample prediction on average, most for the amygdala and hippocampus. Refitting only the intercepts of all four distributional parameters on about 20 healthy individuals, as implemented in our open-source package NormGAMLSS, calibrated most of six unseen sites without retraining, for base and conditional models. In the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative, global deviation and structural dissociation (the reduction in mean absolute deviation under conditioning) predicted conversion to mild cognitive impairment and dementia beyond the Clinical Dementia Rating and dissociation added beyond global deviation for dementia, even at similar clinically recorded impairment. Conditional norms thus provide relevant, individual-level information.

Neural Computation Runs on Dissipative Structures Under Cyclic Calibration: A Systems Perspective on Sleep

Javier Diaz

Biological function operates far from thermodynamic equilibrium, sustained by metastable structures that continuously cycle through synthesis, function execution, and degradation--consuming energy and exporting entropy in the process. Neural computation exemplifies this dissipative organization, relying on low-entropy structures engaged in dynamic processes that define key information transfer and processing parameters such as gain, threshold, and integration time. This perspective proposes that sleep serves as a systemic recalibration phase required to reset crucial signaling parameters that drift during dissipative processing. During wakefulness, neural activity progressively consumes and disrupts the organization of the very substrate from which it emerges, leading to parameter drift and reduced computational precision. Sleep maintains computational accuracy across activity cycles by recalibrating function-structure coupling--that is, by counterbalancing entropic drift. Rather than explaining the experimentally observed sleep need through homeostatic regulation of unknown physiological variables, this perspective reframes it as an emergent property of neural computation under thermodynamic constraints.

Language Models Share a Readout Bottleneck in Working Memory

Dongyu Gong

Large language models (LLMs) reproduce a human working-memory signature on the N-back task: performance declines as memory load N increases, and errors concentrate on lures, items that repeat the current stimulus at a neighboring but incorrect serial position. We ask whether this shared behavioral signature reflects a shared mechanism. The literature offers two candidate accounts. On a precision account, softmax attention cannot concentrate its weight on the item N positions back, so adjacent items are retrieved instead. On an interference account, attention selects the correct item, but the content of competing items corrupts the decision. We dissociate these accounts in 15 pretrained LLMs from three families (70 million to 14 billion parameters) and in minimal transformers trained from scratch. In all three families, the attention heads that carry the task attend to the target on most missed matches. Linear probes decode near-perfectly whether a matching letter occurred at the target or at a lure position, even on trials where the model false-alarms to the lure, although its own decisions barely distinguish the two. Sharpening attention yields negligible gains, whereas suppressing the letter identity of non-target items improves sensitivity (d') in nearly all model-by-load conditions. In larger models the task is spread over more heads, and removing interference helps more. In minimal transformers, precision limits a single attention layer, but a second layer removes this constraint. The working-memory limit of LLMs therefore lies in reading items out under interference rather than in locating them, consistent with interference accounts of human working memory.

OmicsBench: a long-horizon multi-turn benchmark of AI agents on research-study-scale bioinformatics

Gideon Shaked, Siddharth Nagisetty

Computational biologists increasingly delegate substantial analyses to AI agents in long-horizon, multi-turn sessions, yet most benchmarks of agents' bioinformatics capabilities pose a single short task with a single-shot prompt. We present OmicsBench, a long-horizon, multi-turn bioinformatics benchmark for AI agents. Each of its 97 tasks links dependent subtasks into a directed acyclic graph, for 662 underlying subtasks in all. Each subtask is posed as one user turn and graded deterministically against an answer key, with optional in-context feedback on incorrect answers. Across 12 models on three harnesses, the slowest agent averages 3.6 hours per task, and no combination comes near saturating the benchmark, with mean scores of 0.43 to 0.57 out of a possible 1 without in-context feedback and 0.54 to 0.67 with it. Multi-turn evaluation also uncovers behavior that single-shot prompts cannot, as agents score lower on subtasks that depend on earlier subtasks, and in-context feedback helps agents recover some of that loss. Because deterministic scoring gives no qualitative account of how an agent failed, we also introduce an unsupervised failure mode discovery pipeline that describes, embeds, and clusters agents' failed attempts to surface emergent failure modes. Applied to OmicsBench, this pipeline exposes qualitative shortcomings in how agents carry out bioinformatics analyses, ranging from agents applying their own statistical filters in place of the specified ones to agents writing whole answers from memory.

Statistical Mechanics of Spike Trains: Self-Consistent Activity Ensembles and Phase Transitions

Jan Pyrzowski, Laura Messio, Guillaume Marrelec, Marcin Markiewicz

We introduce a statistical-mechanical framework for spiking neuronal systems in which entire spike trains, defined as spatiotemporal configurations of discrete events, are treated as microstates of the system. Instead of modeling explicit network dynamics, we construct an ensemble over complete spike histories and assign to each configuration an effective energy functional that quantifies its consistency with intrinsic synaptic interactions. This formulation leads to a Boltzmann-like distribution over spike configurations, where an effective temperature captures the influence of external inputs acting as a stochastic drive. Macroscopic states are described in terms of coarse-grained observables, such as population firing-rate densities, and arise from a competition between energetic contributions favoring interaction-consistent spike patterns and entropic contributions favoring spatio-temporal disorder. Within this framework, we primarily characterize the transition between oscillatory and non-oscillatory activity. The non-oscillatory regime contains both broadly distributed, approximately uniform states and temporally localized collapsed states, whereas the oscillatory regime is characterized by repeated activity peaks. These regimes are diagnosed using order parameters derived from the population spike-rate density, including its entropy, temporal localization measures, and autocorrelation-based indicators. To complement the analytical treatment, we perform Monte Carlo simulations that sample the ensemble of admissible spike configurations using a Metropolis-Hastings scheme defined directly on spike trains. The simulations reveal the emergence of the predicted macroscopic regimes and provide a direct sampling-based probe of the free-energy landscape.

Cross-subject decoding of imagined speech from scalp EEG: a leakage-controlled evaluation on three public corpora

Blessing Isoyiza Adeika, Onyema Osuagwu

Objective. A decoder of imagined speech that works for a new user without calibration would make a non-invasive speech interface practical. We tested whether imagined-speech content recorded with scalp electroencephalography (EEG) transfers across people, and examined why accuracy within a single person remains low. Approach. We analyzed three public corpora, ds003626, KaraOne and Track 3 of the 2020 International Brain-Computer Interface Competition, under a protocol that enforces split integrity by assertion and reports exact binomial thresholds, group tests and bootstrap confidence intervals. We trained decoders on each participant and tested them on every other, compared representational geometry across participants without a classifier, applied subject-level alignment, and tested five explanations for the within-subject ceiling. Main results. Across 482 ordered pairs of participants, no pair survived correction for multiple comparisons; mean pair-transfer accuracy lay within 0.006 of chance on every corpus, while every within-subject reference lay above it. Imagery against pre-stimulus rest generalized at about 0.61 after artifact and elapsed-time controls, but its decoder labelled the interval preceding imagery as imagery as often as imagery itself, so the contrast reflects a post-stimulus state, not imagery. Within subject, only the competition corpus decoded above threshold for every participant (0.3147; chance 0.200); syllable count explained little of this, but its frequency profile failed an artifact audit. Significance. In these corpora, imagined-speech content did not transfer between people through any representation we examined. Trial count per class was the only variable that ordered the corpora, which favors larger single-condition datasets over further model development.

How large an effect would we have seen? Positive controls, detection floors and dataset pitfalls in scalp-EEG speech decoding

Blessing Isoyiza Adeika, Onyema Osuagwu

Objective. Studies that decode imagined speech from scalp electroencephalography (EEG) commonly validate their pipelines with a positive control, usually overt speech, and report null results without stating what effect size the pipeline could have detected. Approach. Using ds003626 ("Thinking out loud"), KaraOne and Track 3 of the 2020 International Brain-Computer Interface Competition, we audited decoding of overt speech, articulator preparation and imagined speech with artifact-channel subsets, band-limited decoding and trial selection, under a protocol that enforces split integrity by assertion. We injected class-dependent effects of known amplitude into the real recordings to measure the detection floor of five decoder families, and we checked the ds003626 labels against the dataset's acquisition protocol. Main results. On ds003626, two lip electrodes decoded overt speech at 0.7430 and the eight external channels at 0.7068, against 0.4231 for the 128-channel scalp montage, and no decodable signal survived in the 8-30 Hz band. On the other two corpora, within-subject accuracy rose monotonically with frequency into the range dominated by muscle activity. Band-power logistic regression detected an injected 8-30 Hz desynchronization of 0.54 uV under a criterion fixed in advance, whereas a compact convolutional network (EEGNet) detected nothing within subject up to 4.31 uV. In ds003626, the condition codes are the reverse of the intuitive reading, the class encoding changed between public releases, and condition is a deterministic function of time within each session. Significance. In these corpora the overt-speech positive control is dominated by myogenic signal and cannot validate a decoding pipeline. We recommend that speech-decoding studies report band-limited and artifact-channel accuracy, the direction of accuracy across frequency, and a measured detection floor.
